# 10.3 — Crawlability & Crawl Management

[← Domain 10 — Technical SEO](README.md)

Crawlability describes whether an automated client can discover and retrieve a URL under the site's technical configuration and the client's own policies, capacity, and scheduling. It is a property of the interaction between a client and a site—not a guarantee that a particular URL will be fetched at a particular time.

## 1. Discovery, access, and retrieval

A URL may become known through internal links, external links, sitemaps, redirects, feeds, or other discovery mechanisms. Discovery does not mean the crawler will fetch it. A fetch attempt can fail because of DNS, network, TLS, server availability, access rules, rate limits, or other response conditions. Google documents these as distinct crawling and indexing concerns. [1][2]

Keep these states separate:

- **Discovered:** the crawler has learned the URL.
- **Crawled/fetched:** the crawler requested a resource and received a response.
- **Rendered:** the crawler processed resources and executed relevant rendering behavior where supported.
- **Indexed:** the search engine selected a representation for its index.
- **Served:** the search engine chose to show a result for a query or product.

A crawl log can establish that a request reached the logging layer; it cannot establish that the page was indexed or ranked. [1]

## 2. Crawl capacity and crawl demand

Google describes crawl budget for Google Search in terms of crawl capacity limit and crawl demand. Capacity is influenced by how much crawling the host can sustain without degrading availability; demand is influenced by Google's interest in the site's URLs, including perceived inventory, popularity, and staleness. This is Google's documented model and should not be presented as a universal specification for every crawler. [3]

Google's current guidance is aimed primarily at very large or frequently updated sites, including sites with millions of unique URLs or large URL inventories changing daily. Smaller, stable sites that are crawled promptly generally do not need a dedicated crawl-budget optimization program. [3]

The practical implication is not “make crawlers visit more often at any cost.” Maintain a coherent URL inventory, reduce accidental duplication and unbounded URL generation, keep important resources available, and avoid overloading infrastructure. Google's documentation also warns that blocking URLs with robots.txt is not a general-purpose method for reallocating crawl budget to other URLs. [3][4]

## 3. Crawl paths and URL inventory

Crawl management begins with a reliable inventory. Compare URLs generated by the CMS/router, internal links, XML sitemaps, server logs, and search-engine reports. Differences can reveal orphaned URLs, stale sitemap entries, parameter explosions, unexpected redirects, and important resources that are not reachable through intended paths.

A crawl-path review should identify:

- Important pages reachable through ordinary HTML links.
- Redirect chains and loops.
- Internal links to error responses or non-canonical variants.
- Faceted and parameterized URLs that create excessive combinations.
- Resources required to render meaningful content.
- URLs that should be removed, consolidated, or excluded under a documented policy.

Do not assume every crawler executes JavaScript or supports every browser API. Use crawlable links with real destinations and test critical content using the crawler and rendering context that matters. Google documents its own JavaScript rendering process; other crawlers may behave differently. [5]

## 4. Diagnose before changing controls

A useful diagnosis correlates multiple evidence sources:

1. Check DNS, TLS, server availability, response latency, and 5xx/429 patterns.
2. Inspect robots.txt and response-level indexing directives.
3. Compare sitemap URLs with the intended indexable inventory.
4. Crawl representative templates and parameter combinations.
5. Review server logs and verify crawler identity where provider-specific attribution matters.
6. Consult the relevant search-engine crawl reports and URL inspection tools.
7. Re-test after remediation and record the same scope and time window.

Google recommends monitoring crawl statistics and availability and avoiding long redirect chains and soft 404s. Its documentation describes provider-specific effects of HTTP status codes; do not generalize those outcomes to all search engines. [3][6]

## 5. Operational safeguards

Set crawl and rate limits based on service capacity and legitimate client behavior. Do not block essential CSS, JavaScript, or image resources if the crawler needs them to understand the page. Avoid changing robots rules solely to make a report look cleaner. When permanently removing a resource, return an accurate response rather than hiding the URL behind a disallow rule. [3][4]
