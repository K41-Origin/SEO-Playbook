# 09.2 — Entity Fundamentals & Entity Identification

**Domain:** [09 — Semantic SEO](README.md)  
**Topic ID:** 09.2  
**Status:** Documented; source-checked  
**Review date:** 2026-11-19

Before a website can model relationships between things, it needs to establish **which things it is actually talking about**. That is the job of entity identification.

An entity is not the same thing as a keyword, a phrase, a page title, or a URL. Those can be ways of referring to, describing, or documenting something. The entity is the subject the model intends to represent.

This topic covers the foundations and first-pass identification of entities for a website knowledge model. It does not cover detailed identity resolution, attribute design, alias management, or ambiguity resolution; those belong to 09.3 — Entity Attributes, Identity & Disambiguation — once that planned topic is documented.

## 1. What Is an Entity?

In this playbook, an **entity** is a distinct real-world thing, abstract concept, event, or other identifiable subject that a knowledge model chooses to represent as one unit.

Examples include:

- A person, organization, or company.
- A physical product, product model, or component.
- A place, building, or geographic feature.
- An event, project, or documented occurrence.
- An abstract concept, method, discipline, or defined subject.
- A species, material, or other identifiable category of thing.

This is a practical modeling definition, not a claim that every discipline or data system uses the word *entity* identically.

The W3C RDF specification uses the broader term *resource* for things represented in an RDF model. It explicitly includes physical things, documents, abstract concepts, numbers, and strings; it also explains that an IRI denotes a resource called its referent. That standards-level scope is intentionally broad. A website knowledge model still needs project-specific rules for deciding which subjects deserve their own entity records. [1]

Wikidata provides a useful applied example: its items can represent topics, concepts, objects, and events, and it distinguishes a concept such as *planet* from an individual instance such as *Earth*. This demonstrates why entity modeling should not collapse a category and one member of that category into the same record. Wikidata's own inclusion and notability rules are rules for Wikidata, not universal requirements for SEO projects. [2]

## 2. Entity, Concept, Instance, Term, and Document

These concepts can appear together in a sentence or page, but they do different jobs.

| Term | What it represents | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Entity | A distinct subject represented by the model. | Earth; the company Acme; the event Paris 2024. |
| Concept or class | A general idea or category that can organize or describe instances. | Planet; bicycle; search engine. |
| Instance | A particular member or occurrence of a class or concept. | Earth as an instance of a planet; a specific bicycle; one particular search engine. |
| Term or expression | A word, phrase, name, acronym, or label used to refer to or describe a subject. | “Earth”, “Blue Planet”, “planet”, “bicycle”. |
| Document or page | An information resource that contains, presents, or discusses information. | A product page, an article, a PDF manual, a catalog entry. |

A term is not automatically an entity. The string “jaguar” is a linguistic expression; depending on context, it might refer to an animal, a vehicle brand, or something else. The candidate entity is the intended referent—not the string itself.

Likewise, a page about a product is not necessarily the product. The page is a document; the product is the subject being described. In a broad data model, both documents and products can be represented as resources, but they play different roles in the website's knowledge model. [1]

A useful distinction:

- **“Planet”** can represent a general concept or class.
- **“Earth”** can represent a specific planet.
- **“Blue Planet”** can be an expression used to refer to Earth.
- **An article about Earth** is a document describing that subject.

Do not merge these into one record just because the words appear on the same page.

## 3. Why Entity Identification Matters

Entity identification establishes the units that later modeling work will describe and connect.

If the candidate units are wrong, downstream work inherits the error. A model may accidentally merge two different products, treat a broad category as a single item, or create separate records for several expressions that refer to the same subject. Relationships, taxonomies, topical models, and page architecture built on top of those decisions will then be unreliable.

A disciplined first pass helps teams:

- Identify the actual subjects relevant to the website's declared scope.
- Separate general concepts from their individual instances.
- Distinguish entities from the words and pages used to describe them.
- Avoid treating every noun phrase or keyword as a separate entity.
- Create a traceable starting inventory for later identity and attribute work.
- Make entity selection consistent across editorial, research, and architecture work.

This is a knowledge-modeling rationale. It does not establish that a search engine recognizes every entity in the model or rewards a site for listing them.

## 4. Entity Categories for Initial Discovery

The categories below are practical discovery prompts, not a universal or exhaustive ontology. A project may need only a small subset.

| Candidate category | What to look for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Person | A particular individual relevant to the subject area. | A named author, inventor, executive, or practitioner. |
| Organization | A distinct institution, company, association, agency, or group. | A manufacturer or standards organization. |
| Product or product model | A defined product, service offering, or model that can be distinguished from its maker or category. | A specific camera model. |
| Place | A named geographic location or built environment. | A city, park, airport, or building. |
| Event | A particular occurrence or organized event. | A conference edition or product launch. |
| Concept or discipline | An abstract subject with a definable meaning or scope. | Information retrieval or photosynthesis. |
| Process or method | A defined procedure, technique, or repeatable approach. | A maintenance procedure or analytical method. |
| Physical object or component | A distinct material object or part relevant to the model. | A bicycle brake rotor. |
| Species or biological category | A defined organism or taxonomic subject. | A particular species of plant. |

The category label is only a starting point. A specific product model and the broader product category are not the same entity. A company and its products are not the same entity. A conference series and one annual edition may also need separate records if the project must distinguish them.

Do not over-model. If a category or detail does not help represent the website's subject matter, it may not need an entity record at all.

## 5. Entity Candidate Discovery

Entity identification starts with evidence about the website's subject area—not with blindly exporting every noun phrase from a keyword tool.

Useful candidate sources include:

1. **Business and product records:** official product catalogs, service documentation, organization records, manufacturer specifications, and current business information.
2. **Existing website content:** product pages, service pages, category pages, guides, author biographies, glossaries, and other materials already describing relevant subjects.
3. **Subject-matter documentation:** technical standards, authoritative registries, government datasets, professional bodies, and specialist references relevant to the domain.
4. **Audience and query research:** search queries, customer questions, support tickets, and interviews can reveal how people refer to subjects or which distinctions matter. These sources help discover expressions and information needs; a query itself does not prove that a separate entity exists.
5. **Internal subject experts:** product owners, practitioners, editors, and domain specialists can identify distinctions or terminology missing from published content. Their claims still need evidence where factual accuracy matters.
6. **Existing structured datasets:** catalogs, databases, and public knowledge bases can provide candidate records and identifiers. Their definitions, coverage, freshness, and reuse conditions must be checked before adoption.

Use more than one source when a candidate's identity or relevance is consequential. An internal spreadsheet can show what a company calls a product; it may not establish the product's technical classification or whether two differently named entries are the same model.

Google's SEO Starter Guide discusses organizing website information logically and making pages understandable to users and search engines. It does not prescribe a universal entity-discovery workflow. The workflow in this topic is therefore a playbook method for knowledge modeling, not a Google requirement. [3]

## 6. A First-Pass Entity Identification Workflow

Use this sequence to create a candidate inventory. Detailed identity decisions are intentionally deferred to 09.3.

### Step 1 — Define the model's scope

Write down the website's subject, audience, business purpose, and boundaries.

For example, a bicycle maintenance website might model bicycle systems, components, tools, procedures, and safety concepts. It probably does not need to model every bicycle ever manufactured if its content does not depend on those distinctions.

### Step 2 — Collect candidate references

Gather names and descriptions from the sources relevant to the declared scope. Record where each candidate appeared and why it may matter.

At this stage, capture candidate references rather than assuming every phrase represents a unique entity.

### Step 3 — Identify the intended subject

For each candidate reference, ask:

- What thing or concept is this expression intended to denote?
- Is it a specific individual, a general category, a document, a process, or simply a descriptive phrase?
- Does the source provide enough context to understand what is being referred to?
- Is the candidate relevant to the model's declared scope?

If the referent cannot be determined confidently, mark it as unresolved. Do not manufacture certainty just to make the inventory look complete.

### Step 4 — Assign a provisional category

Use a practical category such as Person, Organization, Product, Place, Event, Concept, Process, or Component. If no category fits, record the candidate without forcing it into an unsuitable class.

This is a provisional classification for discovery. Formal class definitions, hierarchies, and constraints belong to the ontology and taxonomy work in 09.5.

### Step 5 — Distinguish categories from instances

Ask whether the candidate represents a general class or a particular instance.

For example:

- “Camera” is a broad product category.
- “Mirrorless camera” is a narrower category or product type.
- “Acme X1” is a particular product model.

Whether each level deserves a separate entity record depends on the model's purpose and the distinctions the website needs to explain.

### Step 6 — Record source and confidence

Record the source supporting the candidate, the exact reference as encountered, and the reason it was included. If sources disagree or the referent is unclear, flag the issue for review.

A candidate inventory should preserve uncertainty rather than silently converting weak evidence into a confirmed identity.

### Step 7 — Hand off unresolved identity questions

Candidates that may refer to the same subject, share a name, have competing identifiers, or have uncertain boundaries should be flagged for 09.3. Do not solve difficult identity collisions by guessing during initial discovery.

## 7. Candidate Selection: What Deserves an Entity Record?

Not every noun, keyword, product mention, or phrase deserves its own entity record.

Use the following practical checks:

| Check | Question | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Scope relevance | Does this subject materially belong to the website's defined knowledge domain? | Exclude unrelated mentions or keep them as incidental references. |
| Distinctness | Is there a meaningful subject to represent, rather than just a word or descriptive fragment? | Do not create an entity from a phrase alone. |
| Identifiability | Can the intended subject be described clearly enough to distinguish it as a candidate? | Mark unclear references as unresolved. |
| Evidence | Is there a reliable source or defensible internal record supporting its existence and relevance? | Record the source; seek corroboration for important candidates. |
| Modeling utility | Will representing it separately help explain the subject, a meaningful distinction, or a relationship? | Avoid records that add complexity without informational value. |
| Granularity | Is the candidate at a useful level—neither too broad nor needlessly microscopic? | Adjust the modeling level to the project purpose. |

These are project-level decision criteria, not universal search-engine rules. A small specialist website may need a compact set of entities; a large catalog or technical reference may need much finer granularity.

A useful test: **if merging this candidate with another would erase a meaningful distinction the website needs to communicate, separate representation may be justified.** If keeping it separate adds no useful distinction, the record may be unnecessary.

## 8. Naming and Reference Capture at the Discovery Stage

Initial identification needs a readable working name and a traceable reference. It does not yet require a complete identity profile.

For each candidate, capture:

- A provisional preferred label for team communication.
- The exact name or expression found in the source.
- The source location and access date where relevant.
- A short note describing the intended subject.
- A provisional category.
- The reason the candidate belongs in the model.
- Any unresolved ambiguity or possible duplicate.

Do not assume that the most frequent phrase is automatically the best entity name. Frequency can help reveal common language, but naming and alias policy need to account for accuracy, context, language, and identity evidence.

Wikidata illustrates the distinction between an entity and its human-readable terms: its glossary explains that an item represents a subject with an identifier, while labels and aliases are terms used to name or find it. It also notes that labels need not be unique. This is a useful modeling example, not a requirement to adopt Wikidata's data model or naming rules for a website. [2]

Schema.org similarly separates an item's name from properties such as alternateName, identifier, and disambiguatingDescription. These properties illustrate that names, identifiers, and descriptive context have different roles. This topic uses that distinction conceptually; it does not prescribe Schema.org markup or define its implementation. [4]

## 9. Example: Initial Entity Discovery for a Bicycle Maintenance Site

**Illustrative example — original playbook example, not an external case study or ranking experiment.**

Suppose a website publishes maintenance guidance for bicycles with disc brakes.

A first-pass review of its pages, product manuals, and support questions produces these references:

| Reference found | Initial interpretation | Candidate decision |
|---|---|---|
| “Disc brake” | A type of braking system. | Candidate concept/category. |
| “Brake rotor” | A component used in a disc braking system. | Candidate component entity/category. |
| “Rotor” | A shorter expression that may refer to a brake rotor in this context. | Record as a reference; do not automatically create a second entity. |
| “Acme Road 2” | A named bicycle model mentioned in a compatibility guide. | Candidate product model; confirm against product documentation. |
| “Cleaning a rotor” | A maintenance action or procedure, not necessarily a physical entity. | Candidate process/method if the site needs to model procedures. |
| “How to clean disc brakes” | A page title or query expression. | Treat as a document/query reference, not automatically as an entity. |

The initial inventory could therefore contain a braking-system concept, a rotor component, a specific bicycle model, and a cleaning procedure. “Rotor” and “brake rotor” remain candidate expressions associated with the same intended subject until identity review confirms how they should be represented.

This first pass does not yet decide the rotor's full attributes, assign a persistent identifier, resolve every compatibility variant, or define the relationships between all candidates. Those are later modeling steps.

## 10. Common Entity Identification Errors

| Error | Why it fails | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating every keyword as an entity | A query is a language expression and may refer to a need, category, action, or several possible subjects. | Interpret the intended referent before creating a record. |
| Treating every noun phrase as a distinct entity | Phrases can be descriptions, fragments, plural forms, or alternate expressions for an existing subject. | Capture references first; consolidate candidates only after evidence review. |
| Confusing a class with an instance | A category and a particular member have different modeling roles. | Decide whether the model needs both levels. |
| Confusing a page with its subject | A page is an information resource; its main subject is a different modeling object. | Record the document and described entity in their proper roles. |
| Creating entities for every incidental mention | The model becomes noisy and bloated without improving subject understanding. | Apply scope, distinctness, evidence, and utility checks. |
| Assuming a name uniquely identifies a subject | Names can be shared, translated, abbreviated, or reused. | Keep ambiguity visible and route identity resolution to 09.3. |
| Importing external knowledge-base records without review | External datasets have their own scope, inclusion rules, definitions, and data quality limitations. | Check the source's policy, provenance, freshness, and fit for the project. |
| Treating entity extraction output as ground truth | Automated extraction can surface candidates, but it does not establish that every candidate is relevant or correctly identified. | Use extraction as a discovery aid; validate candidates against context and evidence. |
| Turning entity counts into an SEO target | More records do not mean a better model. | Model what the subject actually requires. |

## 12. Key Takeaways

- An entity is the distinct subject a knowledge model intends to represent; a keyword or phrase is an expression that may refer to it.
- Entities may represent concrete things, people, organizations, places, events, or abstract concepts, depending on the model's scope.
- A concept/category and one of its instances are different modeling units.
- A document or page can describe an entity without being that entity.
- Candidate discovery should draw on relevant website, business, subject-matter, and audience evidence.
- Candidate selection should consider scope, distinctness, identifiability, evidence, utility, and granularity.
- Initial identification is not the same as identity resolution. Difficult collisions, attributes, identifiers, aliases, and disambiguation belong to 09.3.
- Do not create entity records to inflate counts or imply ranking benefits. Model what the subject actually requires.
