# 09.7 — Topical Modeling & Knowledge Domains

**Domain:** [09 — Semantic SEO](README.md)  
**Topic ID:** 09.7  
**Status:** Documented; source-checked  
**Review date:** 2026-11-21

Topical modeling is the work of describing a subject as a structured knowledge domain: what the domain is about, which concepts belong inside it, how those concepts are grouped, which dependencies or relationships matter, and where the boundaries sit.

It is not keyword clustering with a fancier name. A keyword cluster groups search expressions for research or targeting. A topical model describes the subject matter those expressions may point toward. The two can inform each other, but they answer different questions.

This topic owns the abstract model of a knowledge domain. It does not own the evaluation of model completeness (09.8), website information architecture (09.9), keyword research and intent clustering (Domain 05), editorial planning or content inventories (Domain 08), or graph serialization (09.6).

## 1. What Is a Topical Model?

A **topical model** is a structured representation of a defined subject area. It identifies the central subject, its constituent concepts, meaningful groupings, dependencies, and scope boundaries.

A **knowledge domain** is the bounded field of subject matter being modeled. It may be broad (for example, bicycle engineering) or narrow (for example, bicycle braking systems). The boundary is a modeling decision: it defines what the model intends to explain and what it deliberately leaves outside.

A useful topical model answers:

- What is the central subject or problem space?
- Which concepts are necessary to describe it?
- Which concepts are distinct, overlapping, dependent, or part of another concept?
- Which relationships matter to understanding the domain?
- What belongs inside the declared scope, and what is adjacent but outside?
- At what level of detail should the model operate?

This is a knowledge-modeling framework for SEO work, not a W3C-defined SEO method. Standards such as OWL and SKOS provide formal ways to represent concepts, classes, hierarchies, and semantic relations; they do not prescribe a universal topical-model workflow for websites. [1][2]

## 2. The Building Blocks of a Knowledge Domain

A model can use different structures depending on the domain. The following components are a practical modeling framework, not a mandatory schema.

| Component | Purpose | Example: bicycle braking domain |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | Declares the subject boundary | Bicycle braking systems |
| Core concept | Names the central subject or organizing idea | Braking system |
| Subdomain | Groups a coherent part of the subject | Brake types; components; maintenance |
| Concept | Represents a distinct idea within the domain | Disc brake; rim brake; brake pad |
| Attribute / dimension | Describes a relevant characteristic | Rotor diameter; pad material |
| Relationship | States how concepts are connected | Brake pad is a component of a brake assembly |
| Dependency | Identifies prerequisite understanding or conceptual reliance | Friction and force help explain braking performance |
| Boundary | Marks included, excluded, and adjacent subjects | Motorcycle braking systems are outside a bicycle-only model |
| Evidence / source | Records why the concept or relation belongs | Manufacturer technical documentation; engineering reference |

Entities and identity resolution are covered in 09.2–09.3; relationship meaning is covered in 09.4; ontology and vocabulary design is covered in 09.5. This topic combines those inputs into a coherent subject-domain model rather than redefining each component.

### 2.1 Domain, concept, topic, and query are not synonyms

These terms operate at different levels:

- **Domain:** the bounded subject area being modeled.
- **Concept:** an idea or category represented within that domain.
- **Topic:** a subject of discussion or information need; its granularity depends on context.
- **Query / keyword:** a search expression used by a person. It may refer to one concept, several concepts, a task, or an ambiguous need.

One concept can be expressed through many queries, and one query can touch multiple concepts. A query list therefore cannot be treated as a complete or authoritative model of the underlying subject.

## 3. Why Build a Topical Model?

A topical model can help a team:

1. **Make scope explicit.** It prevents a project from expanding into every adjacent subject merely because it appears related.
2. **Separate concepts that are routinely conflated.** Similar words do not guarantee identical meaning.
3. **Expose dependencies.** Some concepts are easier to understand after prerequisite concepts have been defined.
4. **Coordinate research.** Contributors can use a shared map of the subject rather than inventing incompatible structures.
5. **Provide a stable input to later work.** Coverage assessment (09.8) and website information architecture (09.9) can use the model as a declared reference.
6. **Keep decisions traceable.** Teams can record why a concept is included, excluded, merged, split, or treated as uncertain.

These are operational benefits of a coherent knowledge model, not claims that creating a topical model directly improves Google rankings. A model is a planning and reasoning artifact; any search-performance effect requires separate evidence.

## 4. Model the Subject Before Modeling the Website

Start with the subject itself, not with URLs, menu labels, or a spreadsheet of target keywords.

A website structure is one possible expression of a knowledge model. It is not the model itself. The same knowledge domain might be presented through a reference library, a product catalogue, a troubleshooting guide, a course, or several different site structures.

A useful separation:

| Layer | Main question | Canonical owner |
|---|---|---|
| Subject knowledge | What concepts make up this domain, and how do they relate? | Domain 09.7 |
| Query research | How do people express needs and search for information? | Domain 05 |
| Editorial execution | What content should be planned, produced, reviewed, or updated? | Domain 08 |
| Website information structure | How should the validated model be organized into website sections and page roles? | Domain 09.9 |
| Page implementation | How are individual page elements and markup implemented? | Domain 07 / Domain 10 |
| Hyperlink architecture | Which pages should link to which other pages, and why? | Domain 11 |

Do not let a content calendar, keyword export, or existing URL structure silently dictate what the subject domain supposedly contains.

## 5. A Practical Topical-Modeling Workflow

### Step 1 — Define the modeling purpose

Write down the decision or problem the model should support. Examples:

- Build a reference model for bicycle braking systems.
- Organize a technical knowledge base for camera optics.
- Define the conceptual scope of a product category.
- Align several teams around a shared subject vocabulary.

Avoid vague objectives such as “cover the whole topic” or “build topical authority.” They do not define a testable modeling task.

### Step 2 — Declare the domain boundary

Write a short scope statement containing:

- The central subject.
- The audience or use context, if it changes the meaning or depth.
- Included subdomains.
- Explicit exclusions.
- Adjacent subjects that may be referenced but are not modeled in depth.

Example (original): “This model covers bicycle braking systems, their components, operating principles, selection, inspection, and maintenance. Motorcycle braking systems and general vehicle dynamics are adjacent domains, not core scope.”

A boundary is not a claim that excluded subjects are unimportant. It is a declaration of what this model is responsible for.

### Step 3 — Gather concept candidates from multiple evidence sources

Possible sources include:

- Technical standards and authoritative reference works.
- Product and service documentation from relevant manufacturers.
- Professional and educational materials from credible institutions.
- Existing domain taxonomies, ontologies, and controlled vocabularies.
- Expert interviews and documented operational experience.
- Search queries and audience questions as evidence of expressed information needs.
- Existing site content as an inventory of what the organization already discusses.

Treat source types according to what they can actually establish. Search queries can reveal language and demand patterns; they do not, by themselves, prove that two phrases are distinct concepts or that a domain model is complete. Existing site content describes current publishing choices, not necessarily the correct conceptual structure.

Record the source and rationale for important candidate concepts. Do not promote every phrase found in a keyword export into a separate concept.

### Step 4 — Normalize and define concepts

For each candidate:

1. Give it a stable working identifier.
2. Choose a preferred label.
3. Write a concise definition.
4. Record aliases or alternate expressions where useful.
5. State its scope and relevant context.
6. Distinguish it from concepts that are easily confused.
7. Record the evidence or source that supports its inclusion.

This builds on entity identity and controlled-vocabulary practices described in 09.3 and 09.5. SKOS, for example, supports concept identifiers, lexical labels, documentation notes, and semantic relations for knowledge organization systems. [2]

### Step 5 — Group concepts into meaningful subdomains

Create groups that represent coherent parts of the subject. A group should have a defensible organizing principle, such as:

- A system component.
- A process or lifecycle stage.
- A functional role.
- A technical discipline.
- A class of problems.
- A level of abstraction.

Avoid groups that exist only because several keywords share a word, have similar search volume, or happen to be assigned to the same editor.

A useful group should answer: “What makes these concepts belong together?” If the answer is merely “they sound related,” the grouping needs work.

### Step 6 — Define conceptual relationships and dependencies

Identify relationships that help explain the domain:

- Part–whole: a brake pad is part of a brake assembly.
- Type–class: a disc brake is a type of braking system.
- Process: braking involves force and friction.
- Functional: a lever actuates a braking mechanism.
- Dependency: understanding friction supports an explanation of braking behavior.
- Contextual association: brake maintenance may be discussed alongside wheel inspection.

These are illustrative relationship families, not a universal mandatory list. Define each relationship precisely and preserve direction, context, and evidence as required by 09.4.

A dependency is not always the same as a factual relationship. “Concept B is easier to explain after Concept A” is an instructional or explanatory dependency; it does not necessarily mean A causes B or is physically part of B.

### Step 7 — Set the right level of granularity

A model can be too broad to guide decisions or so detailed that it becomes impossible to maintain.

Use a practical stopping rule:

- Split a concept when it has a distinct definition, scope, relationships, or decisions that matter independently.
- Keep concepts together when the distinction is merely wording or does not change the model's use.
- Add detail only when it helps explain the domain or supports a real downstream requirement.
- Record unresolved distinctions rather than forcing false precision.

There is no universal correct number of concepts, subdomains, or hierarchy levels. Granularity depends on the purpose, subject complexity, available evidence, and maintenance capacity.

### Step 8 — Record uncertainty and competing interpretations

Not every domain has universally agreed boundaries. Concepts may overlap, terminology may vary by community, and sources may classify the same subject differently.

Record:

- Alternative definitions.
- Disputed boundaries.
- Different classification traditions.
- Missing or weak evidence.
- Decisions made for the model and why.
- Conditions under which the decision should be revisited.

Do not hide uncertainty by forcing every concept into one neat tree. A model that acknowledges unresolved boundaries is more useful than a tidy but misleading diagram.

### Step 9 — Publish a model specification

The output should be reviewable by someone who did not build it. A practical specification contains:

- Purpose and scope statement.
- Domain boundary and exclusions.
- Concept inventory with definitions.
- Subdomain groupings and their rationale.
- Relationship and dependency descriptions.
- Evidence and provenance notes.
- Open questions and confidence notes.
- Version, owner, and review date.

This is a recommended project artifact, not a prescribed W3C document format.

### Step 10 — Hand off to coverage assessment

Once the model has a stable working scope, 09.8 can assess whether relevant concepts, attributes, relationships, and information needs are adequately represented.

Do not quietly expand the model during a coverage review without recording the scope change. Otherwise, the target keeps moving and the assessment becomes impossible to interpret.

## 6. Example: A Bicycle Braking Knowledge Domain

The following is an original illustrative model. It is not an industry-standard taxonomy, complete engineering ontology, or ranking strategy.

**Domain:** Bicycle braking systems.

**Purpose:** Organize technical knowledge about how bicycle braking systems are structured, operate, selected, inspected, and maintained.

**Boundary:** Includes bicycle brake types, components, operating principles, compatibility considerations, inspection, and maintenance. Excludes motorcycle braking systems and broad vehicle dynamics except where a limited reference is needed.

### 6.1 Example concept map

| Subdomain | Example concepts | Why they belong |
|---|---|---|
| Brake architectures | Rim brake, disc brake, mechanical disc brake, hydraulic disc brake | Different system configurations |
| Components | Lever, cable, hose, caliper, rotor, pad, rim braking surface | Parts involved in the braking system |
| Operating principles | Friction, force transmission, heat, mechanical advantage | Concepts used to explain operation |
| Compatibility | Rotor size, mounting standard, lever–caliper compatibility, pad shape | Constraints affecting component combinations |
| Inspection and maintenance | Pad wear, rotor condition, cable adjustment, hydraulic bleeding | Tasks related to system condition and service |
| Performance context | Modulation, stopping distance, heat management, wet-weather behavior | Factors used to discuss system behavior and use conditions |

The groups are not all the same kind of thing. Some are system types, some are components, some are explanatory principles, and some are operational tasks. That is acceptable if the model makes each grouping's organizing logic explicit. Do not pretend every branch is a single clean taxonomic hierarchy.

### 6.2 Example dependency map

An explanatory sequence could be:

1. Basic force and friction concepts.
2. How a braking system transfers force.
3. Main brake architectures and their components.
4. Compatibility between components.
5. Inspection and maintenance tasks.
6. Performance behavior under different conditions.

This is an instructional dependency, not a universal prerequisite order. An expert reference model may organize the same concepts differently because its use case is different.

### 6.3 What this model is not

It is not:

- A list of target keywords.
- A decision about how many website URLs to create.
- A content calendar.
- A list of pages that must link to each other.
- Proof that publishing one page for every concept improves rankings.

Those decisions belong to their respective workflows and require additional evidence.

## 7. Topical Model vs Keyword Cluster, Content Cluster, and Taxonomy

These structures can overlap in practice, but they are not interchangeable.

| Structure | Primary unit | Main purpose | Typical output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topical model | Concepts and knowledge relationships | Describe the subject domain | Concept map, domain model, scope specification |
| Keyword cluster | Queries or search expressions | Group search demand and language patterns | Query groups and research mapping |
| Content cluster | Content assets or planned pieces | Coordinate editorial coverage and navigation | Pillar/supporting content plan |
| Taxonomy | Categories and classification relations | Classify concepts or items | Hierarchical or faceted classification scheme |
| Knowledge graph | Represented resources and graph statements | Store or expose connected modeled knowledge | Nodes, predicates, graph/dataset |

A taxonomy can be one component of a topical model. A topical model may later inform keyword research, content planning, and website architecture. But the output of one workflow should not be relabeled as another without doing the underlying modeling work.

## 8. Quality Checks Before Calling the Model Usable

This is a readiness checklist, not the formal completeness audit owned by 09.8.

- The purpose and domain boundary are explicit.
- Major concepts have definitions, not just labels.
- Similar or ambiguous concepts are distinguished.
- Grouping logic is stated and applied consistently.
- Important relationships have precise meanings.
- Exclusions and adjacent domains are documented.
- Concept granularity is appropriate to the intended use.
- Sources and unresolved disagreements are traceable.
- The model is understandable without relying on the author's memory.
- A named owner and review trigger exist.

Passing this checklist means the model is ready for a dedicated coverage assessment. It does not prove that the model is complete.

## 9. Common Modeling Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating a keyword export as the topical model | Queries reflect expressions and demand, not the complete conceptual domain | Use query research as one evidence input |
| Defining the domain from existing URLs | The current site may contain gaps, legacy structure, or arbitrary publishing choices | Model the subject independently first |
| Creating groups based on shared words | Lexical similarity does not establish conceptual unity | Group by explicit domain logic |
| Making every concept a page | Concepts do not map one-to-one to URLs | Leave page-role decisions to 09.9 and editorial decisions to Domain 08 |
| Forcing a single hierarchy | Some concepts have multiple valid dimensions or cross-cutting relationships | Use multiple views or explicit associative relations where justified |
| Over-modeling minor distinctions | Increases maintenance cost without improving decisions | Split only when the distinction matters |
| Ignoring scope exclusions | Adjacent subjects expand the model indefinitely | Record included, excluded, and adjacent areas |
| Treating dependencies as causal facts | Explanatory order and real-world causation are different | Label dependency type precisely |
| Claiming ranking benefits from topical completeness | The model itself does not establish a search ranking effect | Evaluate search outcomes separately with appropriate evidence |

## 11. Key Takeaways

- A topical model describes a bounded subject domain through concepts, groupings, relationships, dependencies, and scope.
- A knowledge domain must have an explicit boundary; otherwise the model expands without a clear stopping point.
- Queries and keywords are evidence about expressed information needs, not a complete representation of the subject itself.
- Build the conceptual model before deciding how it should map to URLs, editorial assets, or internal links.
- Use definitions, grouping logic, relationship meaning, and provenance to make the model reviewable.
- Choose granularity based on the model's purpose; there is no universal target number of topics.
- A topical model is a knowledge-planning artifact, not a confirmed Google ranking factor.
- The next step is to assess its coverage in 09.8.
