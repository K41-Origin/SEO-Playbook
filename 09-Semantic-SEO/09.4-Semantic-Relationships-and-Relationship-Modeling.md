# 09.4 — Semantic Relationships & Relationship Modeling

**Domain:** [09 — Semantic SEO](README.md)  
**Topic ID:** 09.4  
**Status:** Documented and source-checked  
**Review date:** 2026-11-21

An entity inventory tells us **what subjects exist in the model**. Relationship modeling tells us **how those subjects are connected, in what direction, under which conditions, and based on what evidence**.

The distinction matters. A company can manufacture a product, distribute another brand's product, own a subsidiary, or merely mention a competitor. Those are not interchangeable relationships. If the model collapses them into a vague “related to” connection, it stops explaining the subject and starts manufacturing ambiguity.

This topic defines relationship meaning and how to model relationship assertions responsibly. It does not own ontology/taxonomy design (09.5), knowledge-graph representation patterns and graph structures (09.6), search-engine relationship extraction, or website internal-link strategy (Domain 11).

## 1. What Is a Semantic Relationship?

A **semantic relationship** is a defined connection between two or more modeled things. The things may be entities, concepts, events, documents, or values, depending on the model's scope.

Examples:

- A person **founded** an organization.
- An organization **manufactures** a product.
- A product **is compatible with** a component.
- An author **created** a work.
- A document **describes** an entity.
- An event **takes place at** a venue.

A relationship is not just a reason to put two topics beside each other. It makes a claim about how they are connected.

In RDF, a statement is expressed as a subject–predicate–object triple. The predicate names the relationship between the subject and object, and the direction from subject to object is meaningful. RDF therefore provides a formal example of why relationship labels and direction must be explicit. [1]

### 1.1 Entity association is not automatically a relationship fact

Suppose a website publishes pages about a camera, a lens, and a photographer. Their presence on the same website or in the same article does not establish that:

- The photographer designed the camera.
- The lens is compatible with that camera.
- The camera belongs to the photographer.
- The photographer endorses the product.

Those may be possible claims, but each requires its own evidence. Co-occurrence, topical similarity, proximity in a document, or a shared category can suggest a relationship candidate; they do not independently prove the relationship.

**Modeling principle:** record the relationship that evidence supports—not the relationship that would make the graph look more connected.

## 2. The Components of a Relationship Assertion

A useful relationship definition should specify at least:

| Component | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject / source entity | Who or what is the starting participant? | Company A |
| Predicate / relationship type | What connection is being asserted? | manufactures |
| Object / target entity | Who or what is the other participant? | Product B |
| Direction | Which participant performs or holds the relationship? | Company A → Product B |
| Scope / context | Under what domain or conditions does it apply? | Product line sold in Indonesia |
| Evidence / provenance | What supports the assertion? | Manufacturer's product catalogue |
| Time / validity | When does it apply? | Valid from a recorded date or period |
| Assertion status | Is it directly supported, inferred, disputed, or unresolved? | Source-supported |

The first three elements correspond to the basic RDF triple structure. The remaining fields are a practical modeling extension for preserving context and auditability; they are not extra mandatory fields in every RDF triple. [1]

### 2.1 Predicate names must carry a precise meaning

Prefer a relationship label that states the actual connection:

- manufactures
- distributes
- founded
- employs
- authored
- located_in
- compatible_with
- part_of
- describes

Avoid vague labels such as:

- related_to
- associated_with
- connected_to
- linked_with

Broad predicates can be useful as deliberately weak associations when that is all the evidence supports. But do not use them as a substitute for defining a more precise relationship when the distinction matters.

For each predicate, write a short definition. If two contributors could reasonably interpret the same label differently, the relationship definition is not finished.

## 3. Relationship Types: Model the Meaning, Not Just the Wording

There is no single universal list of relationship types that fits every subject domain. The following groups are practical prompts for discovering the relationships a project may need. They are not a formal ontology or an exhaustive taxonomy.

| Relationship family | What it expresses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Identity / equivalence | Whether two references denote the same modeled subject | Record A is the same entity as Record B |
| Classification | Membership in a class or category | Product X is a type of camera |
| Part–whole | Component, membership, or inclusion | Lens mount is part of a camera body |
| Creation / authorship | Who created, authored, designed, or produced something | Person A authored Book B |
| Organizational | Employment, ownership, founding, subsidiary, membership | Company A owns Company B |
| Spatial | Location, containment, adjacency, or geographic relation | Venue A is located in City B |
| Temporal | Before, after, during, or valid-at-time relations | Event A occurred after Event B |
| Functional | Purpose, capability, compatibility, dependency, or use | Battery A is compatible with Device B |
| Causal | A cause, effect, or contribution claim | Failure A caused Incident B |
| Descriptive / informational | A document or source describes, reports, or refers to a subject | Page A describes Product B |

Some relationships can fit more than one family depending on the model. A category assignment is not a substitute for a precise predicate definition.

### 3.1 Similar words can encode different relationships

Consider these distinctions:

- **owns** is not the same as **operates**.
- **manufactures** is not the same as **brands**.
- **located in** is not the same as **serves**.
- **authored by** is not the same as **published by**.
- **part of** is not the same as **related to**.
- **compatible with** is not the same as **commonly used with**.
- **caused** is not the same as **correlated with**.

These distinctions are original modeling guidance. The examples illustrate why a relationship vocabulary needs explicit definitions and evidence, rather than relying on everyday language to do all the work.

## 4. Direction, Inverses, and Symmetry

### 4.1 Direction is part of the meaning

“Company A manufactures Product B” does not mean “Product B manufactures Company A”.

A relationship predicate should be defined from a consistent direction. A model may expose the inverse wording for navigation or readability, but the inverse must preserve the same underlying fact.

Examples:

| Forward relationship | Inverse wording |
|---|---|
| Person A founded Organization B | Organization B was founded by Person A |
| Organization A owns Organization B | Organization B is owned by Organization A |
| Author A created Work B | Work B was created by Author A |
| Product A is compatible with Component B | Component B is compatible with Product A |

The inverse wording is not necessarily a second independent fact. It may be a derived view of the same assertion. Whether to store both directions depends on the model and its application.

### 4.2 Not every relationship is symmetric

A relationship is symmetric only if reversing its participants preserves the intended meaning.

- is_sibling_of may be symmetric in a model of family relationships.
- is_compatible_with may be symmetric only if the compatibility definition guarantees mutual compatibility.
- owns is not symmetric.
- is_part_of is not symmetric.
- is_older_than is not symmetric.

Do not assume symmetry because a sentence sounds reciprocal. Compatibility, for example, may depend on direction, version, configuration, or operating conditions.

### 4.3 Do not assume transitivity

A relationship is transitive when a chain of the same relation supports the corresponding connection between the endpoints.

For example, if a model explicitly defines part_of as transitive, and A is part of B while B is part of C, then the model may infer A is part of C.

But this property must be justified by the meaning of the predicate. “Works with”, “mentions”, “near”, and “collaborated with” are not automatically transitive. A is near B and B is near C does not prove A is near C under every distance threshold or context.

Symmetry, transitivity, and inverse behavior are semantic characteristics of particular relationship types—not decorative switches to enable globally.

## 5. Domain, Range, and Relationship Constraints

A relationship definition should state what kinds of subjects and objects it is intended to connect.

For example:

| Predicate | Expected subject | Expected object |
|---|---|---|
| founded | Person or organization, depending on scope | Organization |
| authored | Person or collective agent | Creative work or document |
| located_in | Place, facility, or organization location record | Geographic place |
| compatible_with | Product or component | Product or component |
| describes | Document or information resource | Entity or concept |

These are illustrative constraints. A real model must define its own intended classes and exceptions.

In RDF Schema, rdfs:domain and rdfs:range describe the classes associated with the subject and object positions of a property. Their formal semantics matter: in RDFS, these declarations support inference that a subject or object belongs to a class; they are not simply database validation rules that reject invalid records. [2]

Therefore, do not confuse:

- **Semantic definition:** what a relationship means and which kinds of things it connects.
- **Data validation:** whether a particular record conforms to project rules.
- **Inference:** what additional class membership or relationship conclusions a formal model permits.

If a project needs operational validation constraints, it must select an appropriate validation approach. Domain 10 owns technical implementation; Domain 09 defines the intended meaning and modeling requirements.

## 6. Context, Qualifiers, and Time

A relationship can be true only within a particular context. Removing that context can turn an accurate statement into a misleading one.

Examples:

- A product is compatible with a device **only for firmware version X**.
- A company operated a branch **between two dates**.
- A person worked for an organization **in a specific role and period**.
- A venue hosted an event **on a particular date**.
- A distributor sold a product **within a defined territory**.

For each context-sensitive relationship, consider whether the model needs:

- Start and end dates or a validity period.
- Geographic or jurisdictional scope.
- Product version, configuration, or operating conditions.
- Role or capacity of each participant.
- Status, rank, or confidence as defined by the project.
- Evidence source and review date.

Wikidata provides a concrete, system-specific example: its statements can be supplemented with qualifiers, references, and ranks to contextualize values and support source traceability. This is an illustration of one knowledge-base data model, not a universal requirement for every semantic model. [3]

### 6.1 Relationship facts can change

A relationship should not be treated as timeless just because the entity records are stable.

A company can stop owning a subsidiary. A person can leave an organization. A product can lose compatibility after a software update. A venue can move. Preserve the period to which the assertion applies when that information matters.

Do not silently overwrite historical relationships if the model needs to answer questions about earlier states. Record the new assertion and its time context according to the project's maintenance rules.

## 7. Evidence: Asserted, Inferred, Disputed, or Unknown

A relationship model must distinguish what a source says from what the model concludes.

Use explicit assertion states such as:

| State | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Source-asserted | A source explicitly states the relationship | Manufacturer says it produces Product B |
| Corroborated | Multiple suitable sources support the relationship | Official catalogue and regulatory record agree |
| Inferred | The relationship is derived from other facts or context | A product may be compatible based on a documented interface specification |
| Disputed | Credible sources conflict or the assertion is challenged | Two official records give different ownership dates |
| Unknown / unresolved | Evidence is insufficient to decide | A page mentions two brands but does not establish a partnership |
| Rejected | Reviewed evidence does not support the proposed relationship | The supposed subsidiary is an unrelated company with a similar name |

These labels are a suggested project workflow, not a formal standard.

### 7.1 Co-occurrence is not proof

The fact that two entities appear in the same article, category, navigation menu, or search result does not establish a specific relationship between them.

A page may mention a brand while criticizing it. A comparison article may list competing products. A retailer may sell a product without manufacturing it. A directory may list two businesses in the same city without establishing a partnership.

The evidence must support the predicate—not merely the presence of both entities in the same source.

### 7.2 Preserve provenance at assertion level

For important relationships, record:

- Source URL or source record.
- Publisher or responsible agent.
- What the source directly establishes.
- Date accessed or reviewed.
- Validity period, if known.
- Whether the assertion is direct, inferred, disputed, or provisional.
- Who or what process made the modeling decision.

W3C PROV-O formalizes provenance using concepts including entities, activities, and agents, and provides terms for describing how information was generated or derived. A project can apply the underlying provenance discipline without implementing PROV-O as its technical data model. [4]

## 8. When a Binary Relationship Is Not Enough

A simple relationship connects two participants. Some real-world facts need more context or involve more than two participants.

Examples:

- A customer bought a product from a retailer for a particular price, on a particular date, using a particular payment method.
- A person held a role at an organization during a specific period.
- A product was compatible with a device only under a defined firmware version.
- An event involved an organizer, venue, performer, and date.

The W3C Semantic Web Best Practices Working Group published an informative note describing patterns for n-ary relations—relations involving more than two participants or additional attributes such as time, certainty, or amount. One pattern is to model the relation instance as its own resource and connect its participants and contextual properties to it. The note is useful design guidance, not a normative W3C Recommendation. [5]

### 8.1 Practical modeling example

Instead of flattening a purchase into:

- Customer A purchased Product B.

A model may need a separate **Purchase Event** record with:

- Buyer: Customer A.
- Seller: Retailer C.
- Item: Product B.
- Purchase date: a recorded date.
- Price: a recorded amount and currency.
- Evidence: receipt or transaction record.

This is an original illustrative example of the n-ary relation pattern. The purchase event is a modeled occurrence that groups the participants and context; it is not merely another name for the product or customer.

### 8.2 Do not create relation objects for everything

A simple binary predicate is enough when the relationship is clear and no extra context needs to be attached.

Create a relation instance when the relationship itself has meaningful properties, multiple participants, a lifecycle, or evidence that must be tracked independently. Complexity should solve a modeling problem—not create one.

## 9. Relationship Modeling Workflow

Use this workflow when defining a new relationship or reviewing an existing one. It is an original operational method informed by the cited standards and examples.

1. **Start with resolved entities.** Use the identity and attribute work from 09.3. If the participants are ambiguous, resolve that first.
2. **State the claim in plain language.** Write one sentence describing the exact connection.
3. **Choose a precise predicate.** Avoid vague labels unless a broad association is genuinely all that is known.
4. **Set direction.** Identify the subject and object and document the inverse wording if useful.
5. **Define participant types.** Specify expected subject and object classes, plus legitimate exceptions.
6. **Check relationship characteristics.** Decide whether symmetry, transitivity, or inverse behavior actually follows from the predicate's meaning.
7. **Add context.** Record time, place, role, version, scope, or other qualifiers where they materially affect interpretation.
8. **Attach evidence and provenance.** Record the source, its claim, and the review context.
9. **Mark assertion status.** Separate source-asserted facts from inference, dispute, and uncertainty.
10. **Choose the simplest representation.** Use a binary relationship unless the relationship instance itself needs additional participants or properties.
11. **Review for contradictions.** Check whether the new assertion conflicts with other records, temporal states, or relationship definitions.
12. **Maintain the assertion.** Revisit it when sources, entities, or real-world circumstances change.

## 10. Worked Example: A Camera Knowledge Model

The following example is original and illustrative. It demonstrates relationship meaning and evidence handling; it is not an external factual claim about real brands or products.

Assume a website documents a fictional camera system:

| Subject | Predicate | Object | Context / evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company A | manufactures | Camera Model B | Official product catalogue |
| Camera Model B | uses_mount | Mount Standard C | Technical specification |
| Lens D | compatible_with | Camera Model B | Compatibility list, firmware/version scope recorded |
| Retailer E | sells | Camera Model B | Retailer catalogue; sales territory recorded |
| Article F | reviews | Camera Model B | Article itself |

These assertions must not be collapsed:

- Retailer E selling Camera Model B does not make Retailer E its manufacturer.
- Article F reviewing Camera Model B does not make the article its creator.
- Lens D being compatible with Camera Model B does not establish that every lens using Mount Standard C is compatible under every condition.
- A compatibility claim without version or configuration context may be too broad to be reliable.

The model becomes useful because each predicate carries a distinct meaning and the evidence is attached to the exact assertion it supports.

## 11. Common Relationship Modeling Failures

| Failure | Why it breaks the model | Better practice |
|---|---|---|
| Using one generic related_to predicate for everything | Erases meaningful distinctions | Define precise relationship types |
| Reversing subject and object carelessly | Changes the claim | Set and document direction |
| Assuming every relationship is symmetric | Creates false reciprocal assertions | Test symmetry against the predicate's meaning |
| Assuming transitivity from a chain of associations | Produces unsupported endpoint relationships | Declare transitivity only when logically justified |
| Treating co-occurrence as evidence | Confuses mention with a factual connection | Require evidence for the exact predicate |
| Omitting time or scope | Turns context-dependent facts into misleading absolutes | Record qualifiers and validity |
| Treating inferred links as verified facts | Hides uncertainty | Mark assertion status and inference basis |
| Modeling a multi-party event as a vague binary link | Loses participants and event context | Model the relation instance when needed |
| Creating complex relation objects for trivial links | Adds maintenance cost without value | Use the simplest adequate representation |
| Confusing semantic relationships with hyperlinks | Mixes knowledge meaning with navigation implementation | Keep Domain 11's internal-link strategy separate |

## 13. Key Takeaways

- A semantic relationship is a defined claim connecting modeled subjects—not merely co-occurrence or topical similarity.
- Every relationship needs a clear predicate, direction, participants, and meaning.
- Similar everyday words can represent materially different relationships.
- Symmetry, transitivity, and inverse behavior must be justified by the predicate's semantics.
- Domain and range describe the intended participant types; formal RDFS semantics should not be confused with application-level validation.
- Context, time, evidence, and provenance can change how a relationship should be interpreted.
- Distinguish source-asserted, inferred, disputed, and unresolved relationships.
- Use a relation instance when a multi-party fact or the relationship itself needs additional properties; do not over-model simple links.
- Semantic relationships are not the same thing as internal hyperlinks or search-engine ranking signals.
