# 09.10 — Semantic Model Validation, Maintenance & Governance

A semantic model is not an artifact created once, displayed in a diagram, and then considered finished. Entities change, definitions evolve, evidence sources are revised, relationships become outdated, and website structures move. Without validation and governance, a once-neat model gradually becomes a museum of assumptions.

Domain 09.10 defines how to inspect semantic-model integrity, manage changes, preserve provenance, and keep website representation aligned with the approved model. Its focus is **knowledge-model quality and sustainability**, not editorial auditing, technical website auditing, or SEO performance measurement.

## 1. What Does Semantic Model Validation Mean?

**Semantic model validation** is the systematic process of checking whether a semantic model meets its declared definitions, boundaries, constraints, evidence requirements, and intended purpose.

It does not answer one abstract question such as “is this model correct?” It breaks the question into explainable checks:
- Are entities identified and distinguished consistently?
- Are attributes and identifiers used according to their definitions?
- Do relationships have appropriate meaning, direction, context, and evidence?
- Are schemas, taxonomies, and ontologies used consistently?
- Does every assertion have traceable provenance and status?
- Does the model remain within its agreed scope?
- Does website structure represent the model and information needs without changing their meaning?

Validation must state the **object inspected, criteria, evidence, method, result, and limits of inspection**. “Validated” without those details is just a sticker.

### 1.1 Validation is not the same as truth

A model can pass structural checks while containing false factual claims. Conversely, an assertion may be supported by evidence but fail a technical constraint in a particular representation.

Separate at least four questions:

| Dimension | What is checked | What a pass does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Structural conformance | Does data follow the declared schema, vocabulary, and constraints? | It does not prove every factual statement is true. |
| Logical consistency | Do axioms and assertions contradict one another under the formal semantics used? | It does not prove the model is complete or matches reality. |
| Evidence and provenance | Are sources, context, time, and acceptance basis traceable? | It does not automatically prove sources are correct or current. |
| Domain and architecture fitness | Does the model still fit scope, information needs, and approved website representation? | It does not prove SEO performance or business success. |

This distinction matters. SHACL, for example, defines how to validate an RDF graph against conditions in a shapes graph. The result is a validation report against those conditions—not a certificate that every graph fact is true in the real world. [1]

## 2. What Must Be Validated?

Use the following dimensions as an inspection framework. Specific criteria must depend on model type, purpose, risk, and user needs.

### 2.1 Entity identity and boundaries
Check that one entity is not split into multiple records without a sound identity reason; distinct entities are not merged merely because labels are similar; identifiers, preferred labels, aliases, and descriptions are not confused; entity boundaries remain clear as scope expands; and identity changes or record merges have evidence and decision history.

A shared label is not proof of shared identity. This check extends the identity and disambiguation principles in [09.3](09.3-Entity-Attributes-Identity-and-Disambiguation.md).

### 2.2 Attribute and relationship integrity
Check that:
- attributes are used for appropriate entity types;
- attribute values have correct types, units, language, and context where relevant;
- predicates have stable definitions and are not used as generic catch-all terms;
- relationship direction is not reversed;
- domain, range, cardinality, symmetry, transitivity, or inverse behavior are applied only when defined;
- temporal relationships have sufficient time context;
- co-occurrence, similarity, or association is not promoted to a factual relationship without evidence.

Definitions and constraints must be traceable to relevant schemas or model decisions. Editor intuition alone is not a sufficient validator.

### 2.3 Schema, taxonomy, and vocabulary consistency
Check that classes, properties, concepts, and labels are used consistently; hierarchies contain no cycles or conflicts prohibited by model rules; preferred and alternative labels follow language and naming policy; mappings between vocabularies do not claim stronger equivalence than evidence supports; and definition changes do not silently alter the meaning of existing records.

SKOS defines integrity conditions for its data model and explains that these checks focus on consistency with the SKOS model. This illustrates why validation rules must follow the semantics of the vocabulary in use rather than assumed generic rules for every model. [2]

### 2.4 Provenance, evidence, and assertion status
For important assertions, preserve enough information to answer:
- Who or what system created the assertion?
- Which source supports it?
- When was the source or assertion obtained?
- Is the assertion quoted, verified, inferred, disputed, or unresolved?
- Who approved a status change?
- Why was the assertion accepted, rejected, or replaced?

PROV-O provides a vocabulary for representing entities, activities, and agents involved in producing data or artifacts. It can inform provenance modeling, but is not mandatory for every project and does not automatically assess source credibility. [3]

### 2.5 Scope and model boundaries
Check whether the model still serves its stated purpose; does not silently absorb concepts outside its domain; preserves limitations, exceptions, and context that affect interpretation; separates facts, assumptions, inferences, and unresolved questions; and avoids complexity without a clear need.

Scope validation differs from topical coverage assessment. Domain 09.8 assesses model coverage against scope and information needs. Domain 09.10 ensures scope remains explicit, is followed, and boundary changes are governed.

### 2.6 Alignment between semantic model and website architecture
Check whether page roles and content groups still represent intended concepts; page boundaries do not merge concepts that should remain distinct or split one information need without reason; category labels do not distort or obscure concept meaning; conceptual relationships used to organize information are not mistaken for factual claims or mandatory hyperlinks; and model changes affecting architecture have an owner and follow-up.

Domain 09.9 defines how to translate the model into information architecture. Domain 09.10 checks consistency and manages changes between them.

## 3. Validation Methods: Choose the Right Tool for the Question

No single validator can inspect every dimension. Choose methods based on the type of claim being tested.

### 3.1 Manual and expert review
Use structured review to inspect definition accuracy and entity boundaries; whether predicates express intended relationship meanings; provenance quality and sufficiency; consistent application of scope and exclusions; and whether the model makes sense to domain experts and information owners.

Expert review still needs criteria and decision records. “Looks correct to me” is not an audit trail.

### 3.2 Rule-based and structural validation
For RDF-based models, SHACL can express constraints on nodes and properties, then validate data graphs against shapes graphs. Examples include value counts, node kinds, datatypes, classes, value patterns, and combinations of conditions. SHACL produces a validation report showing results against the specified constraints. [1]

Conceptual project rules might include:
- Every entity record must have an internal identifier.
- Every relationship assertion must have a predicate.
- Every assertion with approved status must have a provenance record.
- Selected attribute values must use a controlled vocabulary.
- Certain relationships may connect only defined entity types.

These are project rules that must be declared. SHACL does not independently determine business rules or domain meaning.

### 3.3 Ontology reasoning and consistency checks
For OWL models, a reasoner can help check logical consequences and inconsistencies under the axioms and OWL semantics used. OWL 2 has formal semantics; reasoning results depend on the axioms and assumptions included in the ontology. [4]

Important limits:
- Logical consistency is not empirical proof that facts are true.
- A reasoner finds only consequences entailed by the representation and semantics used.
- Modeling errors or omitted assumptions will not automatically be discovered.
- A consistent model may still be incomplete, irrelevant, or unsuitable for user needs.

### 3.4 Competency questions and scenario testing
Use representative questions and scenarios to check whether the model supports its intended tasks.

Examples:
- Can the model distinguish two components with similar names?
- Can a user find a prerequisite relationship that genuinely matters?
- Can a reviewer trace the source of a disputed assertion?
- Can a changed attribute identify affected pages and records?

This tests model usefulness and fit with declared needs. Do not repeat all topical-coverage scoring here; 09.8 assessment results are one input.

### 3.5 Website architecture review
Compare the approved knowledge model with the conceptual sitemap, page-role matrix, taxonomy, and architecture specification from 09.9. Use information-need walkthroughs rather than merely checking whether every node has a page.

This inspection concerns model and architecture alignment. HTML, URL status, crawlability, canonicalization, structured-data implementation, and internal-link execution remain with Domains 07, 10, and 11.

## 4. Build a Validation Framework

A validation framework turns quality principles into rules that can be executed and reviewed.

### 4.1 Define the validation contract

| Field | What to define |
|---|---|
| Model identifier | Stable ID for the model or component |
| Purpose and scope | Intended use, included concepts, and exclusions |
| Model owner | Person or role accountable for the model |
| Authoritative definitions | Canonical schema, vocabulary, and policy documents |
| Validation dimensions | Identity, relationships, provenance, scope, architecture alignment, etc. |
| Rules and acceptance criteria | Explicit conditions that can be reviewed or tested |
| Validation method | Manual review, SHACL, reasoning, scenario test, or combination |
| Severity levels | How findings are classified and escalated |
| Review frequency and triggers | Scheduled review and event-based reassessment |
| Evidence record | Sources, reports, decisions, exceptions, and timestamps |

### 4.2 Use severity based on consequence

Severity labels are a governance convention, not a universal standard. Define them within the project and connect them to actions.

| Severity | Example | Expected response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Entity collision changes the meaning of a high-impact model component; unsupported relationship treated as authoritative fact | Stop affected release/use and resolve before approval unless a documented emergency exception applies |
| High | Broken identity mapping, invalid core relationship, missing provenance for a high-risk assertion | Assign owner and remediation deadline; block dependent changes where appropriate |
| Medium | Inconsistent labels, incomplete metadata, non-critical taxonomy mismatch | Schedule correction and assess downstream impact |
| Low | Cosmetic documentation issue or non-impacting metadata inconsistency | Add to maintenance backlog |

Severity should reflect impact, likelihood, scope, and downstream dependency—not how annoying a dashboard warning looks.

### 4.3 Record validation outcomes

A useful validation report includes:
- model version and validation date;
- scope and components inspected;
- rules and tool versions used;
- data snapshot or commit identifier;
- passed and failed checks;
- findings, severity, evidence, and affected components;
- accepted exceptions and rationale;
- reviewer and approval status;
- required remediation and follow-up date.

State what was not checked. “No issues found” is meaningless if the reviewer checked only label formatting.

## 5. Semantic Model Governance

**Semantic model governance** comprises ownership, decision, access, documentation, and change-control practices that keep a model understandable and dependable over time.

Governance does not require a committee for every small vocabulary. A small site may need one accountable owner and a lightweight review process. A multi-team knowledge platform may need formal roles and approval gates. Scale governance to risk and complexity.

### 5.1 Define roles and decision rights

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Model owner | Accountable for purpose, scope, priorities, and approval |
| Domain expert | Reviews domain meaning, facts, terminology, and exceptions |
| Model editor / curator | Maintains entities, attributes, relationships, labels, and records |
| Evidence reviewer | Checks source quality, provenance, and assertion status |
| Architecture owner | Maintains alignment between semantic model and website IA |
| Technical steward | Maintains technical representation and validation pipeline where one exists |
| Change approver | Authorizes material changes, deprecations, and releases |

One person may hold multiple roles. Responsibility and decision authority must remain explicit.

### 5.2 Establish decision policies

Document policies for:
- creating and retiring entity records;
- merging or splitting entity identities;
- adding or redefining attributes and predicates;
- changing domain/range or other formal constraints;
- accepting inferred or externally sourced assertions;
- handling disputed or contradictory evidence;
- adding external vocabulary dependencies;
- changing preferred labels and multilingual terms;
- deprecating concepts, relationships, or page roles;
- approving exceptions and emergency changes.

Policies should specify who proposes, reviews, approves, and what evidence is required. Do not let an informal edit silently redefine a core concept.

### 5.3 Maintain provenance and decision history

Keep traceable records of source evidence and retrieval date; assertion creator or importing process; reviewer and approval decision; rationale for accepting/rejecting a change; previous and new values; related validation findings; and affected models, pages, and downstream consumers.

PROV-O can represent provenance entities, activities, and agents when interoperable provenance modeling is useful. A simpler change log may suffice for a small editorial knowledge model. The format is optional; traceability is not. [3]

## 6. Versioning and Change Management

A model changes when meaning, scope, structure, dependencies, or approved assertions change. Versioning must make those changes discoverable and explain their consequences.

### 6.1 What should trigger a new version?
Examples include entity identity being merged, split, or redefined; a core predicate changing meaning or direction; a class hierarchy or controlled vocabulary changing; a constraint becoming stricter or more permissive; a source being invalidated and assertions revised; scope expanding or contracting; an external ontology dependency changing; or a model-to-website mapping changing materially.

Not every spelling correction needs a major model release. Define version policy by impact and compatibility.

### 6.2 A practical versioning convention
A project may adopt a semantic-style scheme:
- **Major:** incompatible change to meaning, identifiers, core relationships, or constraints that may break dependent models or systems.
- **Minor:** backward-compatible addition of concepts, attributes, relationships, or approved scope.
- **Patch:** correction that does not intentionally change meaning or compatibility.

This is a project convention, not a W3C requirement or universal ontology-versioning rule.

For OWL 2 ontologies, the specification distinguishes an ontology IRI from an optional version IRI, which can identify a particular ontology version. This formal ontology identity/versioning mechanism is distinct from project release labels. [4]

### 6.3 Change request workflow
1. **Propose:** describe requested change, reason, evidence, and urgency.
2. **Classify:** identify affected entities, attributes, relationships, vocabulary terms, constraints, and page roles.
3. **Assess impact:** inspect dependent models, assertions, content architecture, integrations, and consumers.
4. **Review evidence:** verify support and identify competing interpretations.
5. **Approve or reject:** record decision, owner, and rationale.
6. **Implement:** update the canonical model and dependent representations.
7. **Validate:** run applicable structural, logical, provenance, scope, and architecture checks.
8. **Release:** publish the version and change summary.
9. **Monitor and reconcile:** confirm downstream owners have handled the change and close the request.

### 6.4 Deprecation is not deletion
When a concept, property, or entity is no longer recommended:
- mark its status deprecated or retired;
- record reason and effective date;
- identify a replacement where one exists;
- preserve historical identifiers and decision trail where needed;
- document migration guidance for dependent records and pages;
- do not silently reuse the old identifier for a different meaning.

OWL 2 includes ontology annotations such as prior-version and compatibility-related annotations. They support ontology-level version documentation but do not replace a complete operational migration policy. [4]

## 7. Monitor Change and Define Review Triggers

Scheduled reviews help, but event-based reviews are often more important. Reassess a model when supporting evidence or the environment changes.

### 7.1 Scheduled review
Choose cadence based on rate of change in the subject domain; consequence of incorrect information; volume of new or revised assertions; number of downstream models and website components; and available review capacity.

There is no universal monthly, quarterly, or annual schedule for every semantic model. Record why the selected cadence is adequate.

### 7.2 Event-based review triggers
Reassess when:
- a primary standard, policy, or authoritative source changes;
- a key entity changes identity, ownership, status, or definition;
- a high-impact assertion is disputed or invalidated;
- a vocabulary or external ontology releases a breaking change;
- a model constraint produces unexpected failures;
- a website redesign changes page roles, information grouping, or content ownership;
- a new audience, product, service, market, or use case changes scope;
- a dependent system reports a mapping or interpretation failure.

### 7.3 Maintenance backlog

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | Stable reference |
| Affected component | Entity, relationship, vocabulary, rule, page role, or model |
| Description | What is wrong or uncertain |
| Evidence | Source, report, or observation |
| Severity and impact | Why the issue matters |
| Owner | Responsible person |
| Decision | Fix, accept, defer, reject, or investigate |
| Due/review date | When it must be revisited |
| Resolution | What changed and how it was validated |

A backlog is not a dumping ground. Every item needs an owner, decision, and path to closure.

## 8. Align Model Changes with Website Architecture

A semantic-model change can affect the website, but impact is not always one-to-one. A new concept may belong within an existing page; a changed distinction may require a new page role, revised grouping, or clearer labels.

Use an explicit impact map:

| Model change | Possible architecture impact | Responsible domain |
|---|---|---|
| Entity identity merge or split | Review page ownership, labels, and possible overlap | Domain 09.9, with Domain 08 for editorial assets |
| Concept definition changes | Review page scope, terminology, and explanatory requirements | Domain 09.9 and Domain 08 |
| Relationship semantics change | Review conceptual grouping and information pathways | Domain 09.9; Domain 11 decides actual links |
| Taxonomy or preferred-label changes | Review category labels, filters, and navigation terminology | Domain 09.9; Domains 07/10 implement |
| Page role or content grouping changes | Review editorial planning and page-level requirements | Domains 08 and 07 |
| URL or routing changes | Assess redirects, canonicalization, crawl/index behavior, and migration | Domain 10, with Domain 07 |
| Internal-link changes | Review link targets, anchors, and implementation | Domain 11 |
| Organic performance change | Measure and interpret outcome | Domain 14 |

The semantic-model owner must not directly change URLs, redirects, HTML, internal links, or analytics definitions outside the relevant domain's process. Governance coordinates the change; it does not take ownership away from other domains.

## 9. Practical End-to-End Governance Workflow

1. **Register the model:** assign stable model ID, owner, purpose, scope, and authoritative location.
2. **Baseline the model:** record approved schema, vocabulary, entities, relationships, provenance rules, and architecture mapping.
3. **Define validation criteria:** specify checks, acceptance thresholds, severity, and exceptions.
4. **Run validation:** combine automated checks and human review appropriate to the model.
5. **Record findings:** preserve evidence, affected components, and validation limitations.
6. **Resolve or accept findings:** assign owners and document risk-based decisions.
7. **Review change requests:** assess semantic meaning, compatibility, provenance, and downstream impact.
8. **Update the canonical model:** make approved changes in the authoritative source.
9. **Revalidate dependent representations:** include graphs, vocabularies, architecture specifications, and affected website information structures.
10. **Release and document:** publish version, change log, validation report, and migration notes.
11. **Review triggers and backlog:** schedule the next review and monitor event-based changes.

This workflow is tool-agnostic. It can be implemented with a spreadsheet and controlled review, a version-controlled ontology repository, or a formal knowledge-management platform. The control objective matters more than buying a fancy tool.

## 10. Example: Maintaining a Bicycle-Braking Knowledge Model

**Illustrative example only.** This scenario shows validation and governance decisions; it is not a technical specification for bicycle components.

Assume a model contains entities for disc brakes, brake pads, rotors, calipers, and hydraulic actuation; relationships describing component membership and functional interaction; attributes for component type, compatibility, and maintenance status; and website pages for braking overview, component reference, maintenance procedure, and troubleshooting.

A domain expert reports that one relationship between a component and a braking system is too broad. The predicate implies every instance of the component belongs to every system of that type.

Governance process:
1. Open a change request with the disputed assertion and source evidence.
2. Determine whether the problem is entity identity, an overgeneralized relationship, or a missing qualifier.
3. Review predicate definition, affected records, and provenance.
4. Update the relationship model only after domain review.
5. Run structural checks on affected records and review logical consequences where formal reasoning is used.
6. Check whether component reference pages or overview explanations rely on the old statement.
7. Assign editorial and technical follow-up to their respective owners.
8. Release the change with a version note and validation record.

The important outcome is not “we updated a triple.” Meaning, evidence, downstream impact, approval, and correction are all traceable.

## 11. Common Governance Failures

| Failure | Why it causes trouble | Better control |
|---|---|---|
| Treating structural conformance as factual truth | A perfectly shaped graph can contain false assertions | Separate structural, logical, evidence, and domain review |
| No accountable model owner | Changes become inconsistent or disputes remain unresolved | Assign decision rights and escalation paths |
| Untracked edits to core definitions | Dependent records silently inherit changed meanings | Require change requests and versioned decisions |
| No provenance for important assertions | Reviewers cannot reconstruct why a statement was accepted | Preserve source, time, status, and decision history |
| Reusing deprecated identifiers | Old references may point to a different meaning | Retire identifiers and provide explicit migration guidance |
| One validation tool for every problem | Tools check only conditions and semantics they support | Combine rule checks, reasoning, expert review, and scenario tests |
| Treating every warning as critical | Teams ignore noisy reports or waste effort | Define severity by consequence and risk |
| Never reviewing external dependencies | Vocabulary or ontology changes can break assumptions | Track dependencies and release changes |
| Updating model but not website architecture | Website labels and page roles drift from approved meaning | Run impact analysis and coordinate with 09.9 and downstream owners |
| Governance by committee without clear authority | Decisions stall and accountability disappears | Define who proposes, reviews, approves, and implements |

## 13. Key Takeaways

- Validation checks a model against explicit criteria; it does not certify universal truth.
- Structural conformance, logical consistency, evidence quality, scope fitness, and website-architecture alignment are different dimensions.
- SHACL can validate RDF graphs against declared constraints; OWL reasoning checks consequences under formal semantics. Neither replaces evidence review.
- Provenance and decision history make important assertions auditable.
- Governance requires clear ownership, decision rights, change policies, and maintenance.
- Versioning and deprecation must preserve compatibility information and historical traceability.
- Model changes require impact analysis across website architecture and downstream domains.
- Governance should match model complexity and risk. More bureaucracy is not automatically more rigor.

The output is a maintainable knowledge model—not a ranking-factor checklist.
