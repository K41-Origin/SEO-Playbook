# 09.3 — Entity Attributes, Identity & Disambiguation

**Domain:** [09 — Semantic SEO](README.md)  
**Topic ID:** 09.3  
**Status:** Documented and source-checked  
**Review date:** 2026-11-21

Entity identification gives a model a candidate subject. It does not automatically tell us exactly which subject it is, how to distinguish it from similarly named subjects, or which facts can safely describe it. Topic 09.3 handles that next layer: **identity, attributes, identifiers, naming variants, ambiguity, and evidence**.

The goal is not to collect as many properties as possible. The goal is to describe an entity well enough for the model's purpose, distinguish it from plausible alternatives, and make every important identity decision traceable.

## 1. From Candidate to Defensible Entity Record

Topic [09.2 — Entity Fundamentals & Entity Identification](09.2-Entity-Fundamentals-and-Entity-Identification.md) produces a candidate inventory. Topic 09.3 turns suitable candidates into more carefully described records.

A useful working record distinguishes five things:

1. **Entity:** the subject the model intends to represent.
2. **Identity:** the criteria and evidence used to decide which subject the record refers to.
3. **Attributes:** facts or characteristics used to describe that subject.
4. **Identifiers:** values or references used to distinguish or refer to the subject within a defined system or context.
5. **Names and labels:** human-readable expressions used to refer to or describe the subject.

These are related, but they are not interchangeable. A name is not automatically unique; an identifier is not necessarily meaningful to a human; and a page describing an entity is not the entity itself. RDF distinguishes a resource from the IRI that denotes it, while Schema.org separately defines properties such as `name`, `identifier`, `alternateName`, and `sameAs`. [1][2]

## 2. Entity Identity and Entity Boundaries

### 2.1 What does “the same entity” mean?

Two records should represent the same entity only when the evidence supports that they refer to the same intended subject under the model's declared scope.

This sounds obvious until the data gets messy:

- Two people share the same name.
- One organization uses a legal name, a trading name, and a former name.
- A product family contains several distinct models.
- A product model and one physical unit of that model are different things.
- A company changes its name but remains the same legal organization.
- Two separate organizations merge, or one organization splits into successor entities.

The correct decision depends on what the model is trying to represent. A product catalogue may need separate records for a product model and each sellable variant. A legal-entity register may distinguish organizations by jurisdiction and registration. There is no universal boundary rule that fits every domain.

### 2.2 Define the unit before merging records

Before treating two references as the same entity, state the unit being modeled:

| Modeling unit | Example | Boundary question |
|---|---|---|
| Person | A specific individual | Is this the same individual, or merely someone with the same name? |
| Organization | A registered company | Is a brand, subsidiary, branch, and legal entity being treated as one or several subjects? |
| Product model | A particular espresso machine model | Are regional variants or revisions separate models for this use case? |
| Product instance | One physical machine | Is this a specific unit with its own serial number? |
| Place | A city, building, or branch | Is the record about the geographic place, the facility, or the business operating there? |
| Concept | A defined technical concept | Is this a distinct concept, a synonym, or a broader/narrower concept? |

These examples are an original modeling aid, not a universal classification standard.

### 2.3 Avoid both false merges and needless splits

A **false merge** combines distinct subjects because they share a label, identifier-like string, location, or association.

A **false split** creates multiple records for one subject because its name, spelling, language, address format, or public presentation changed.

Both errors contaminate everything downstream. A false merge assigns the wrong attributes and relationships; a false split fragments one subject across several records.

Do not resolve either problem by string matching alone. Compare the candidate's context, type, authoritative identifiers where available, relevant attributes, source records, and temporal context. If the evidence is insufficient, preserve separate provisional records or mark the identity as unresolved rather than forcing a confident-looking answer.

## 3. Attributes: Describe What Matters

An **attribute** is a property used to describe an entity. Depending on the subject, useful attributes may include:

- Names and naming variants.
- Type or category.
- Dates, such as founding date, publication date, or model release date.
- Geographic location or jurisdiction.
- Manufacturer, creator, or responsible organization.
- Model number, registration number, ISBN, or another domain-specific identifier.
- Status, such as active, discontinued, dissolved, or historical.
- Distinguishing characteristics relevant to the model.

Schema.org's `Thing` vocabulary provides examples of general properties including `name`, `alternateName`, `description`, `identifier`, and `disambiguatingDescription`. These are useful vocabulary examples, not a mandatory attribute checklist for every project. [2]

### 3.1 Attribute selection is purpose-dependent

Do not add every fact you can find. Select attributes that help the model:

- Identify or distinguish the entity.
- Describe its role within the declared subject domain.
- Support relevant relationships or classification.
- Answer a real information need.
- Maintain consistency across records.

For a local repair business, service area and official registration details may be useful. For a book, author, publication date, edition, and ISBN may matter. For an abstract concept, a clear definition, scope, and accepted terminology may be more useful than physical-world properties.

The attribute list should follow the entity type and modeling purpose—not a generic SEO template.

### 3.2 Keep attribute values precise

Record enough context to prevent misleading values:

| Weak record | Better record |
|---|---|
| Founded: 2010 | Founded: 2010; source and evidence recorded |
| Location: Jakarta | Headquarters: Jakarta, Indonesia; distinguish from service locations |
| Status: active | Status: active as of the recorded review date; source recorded |
| Model: X200 | Product model: X200; manufacturer and variant context recorded |
| Name: ABC | Legal name, trading name, language, and validity period recorded separately where relevant |

The examples are illustrative. The right fields and precision depend on the domain.

Avoid silently converting an estimate into a fact, a historical value into a current value, or a source's claim into an independently verified truth.

### 3.3 Attributes can change

Some attributes are relatively stable; others change over time. An organization's legal name, address, ownership, or status can change. A product may be discontinued. A person may acquire a new role. A relationship or status that held at one time may not hold later; RDF explicitly notes that statements about relationships and RDF source contents can vary over time. [1]

For attributes likely to change, record when the value was observed or when it was valid, and retain the source. Do not overwrite history when the historical state matters to the model.

## 4. Identifiers: What They Identify—and Within Which System

An identifier is a value or reference used to distinguish or refer to something within a defined system or context. Examples include:

- A database primary key.
- A government or company registration number.
- An ISBN for a particular book edition.
- A GTIN for a trade item.
- A product model number.
- A persistent URI/IRI used in a knowledge representation.

Schema.org describes `identifier` broadly and gives examples such as ISBNs, GTINs, and UUIDs. [2]

### 4.1 Identifier is not the same as name or URL

| Element | Main purpose | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Name/label | Human-readable reference | May be shared, translated, abbreviated, or changed |
| Alias | Alternative name or expression | Does not independently prove identity |
| Identifier | Distinguishes a record or subject within a defined scheme | Its scope and issuing authority matter |
| URL | Locates a web resource | The page may describe an entity rather than identify the entity itself |
| IRI | Denotes a resource in an RDF graph | The intended referent must be established and maintained |

Wikidata illustrates the separation: items have unique Q-identifiers, while labels and descriptions are human-readable fields; its documentation notes that labels need not be unique. This is how Wikidata works, not a requirement that every website create a Wikidata item or use a global external identifier. [3]

### 4.2 Record the identifier's scope

An identifier is only useful when its context is clear. Record, where relevant:

- Identifier value.
- Identifier type or scheme.
- Issuing authority or source system.
- Entity type or class it applies to.
- Whether it identifies a class/model, a specific instance, a legal entity, or a document.
- Validity or status, if the scheme can change or be retired.
- Source and verification date.

For example, a product model number should not be treated as the serial number of one physical unit. A company's website URL should not automatically be treated as its legal registration identifier.

### 4.3 Do not mint identifiers casually

If a project controls its own knowledge model, it may need stable internal identifiers. Design them so records remain referentially consistent when labels, URLs, or descriptive attributes change.

In RDF, an IRI is intended to denote a resource; W3C guidance says an IRI's intended referent should not change once minted. This is a data-modeling principle, not a requirement to expose an RDF identifier on every public web page. [1]

Do not reuse an identifier for a different subject merely because the original record was deleted, renamed, or inconvenient. If the modeled subject genuinely changes, decide whether this is a new entity, a successor, or a revised description—and document that decision.

## 5. Names, Labels, Aliases, and Language Variants

An entity may be referred to by multiple expressions:

- Official or legal name.
- Common name.
- Former name.
- Brand or trading name.
- Acronym or abbreviation.
- Nickname.
- Translation or transliteration.
- Spelling or orthographic variant.

Wikidata uses labels, descriptions, and aliases as separate item fields. Its documentation explains that labels are not necessarily unique and that aliases can include alternative names, acronyms, and translations. Schema.org's `alternateName` similarly represents an alias. [2][3][4]

### 5.1 An alias is a candidate expression, not identity proof

Two entities can share an alias. One entity can have several names. Therefore:

- Do not merge entities just because their names match.
- Do not create separate entities just because their labels differ.
- Do not assume every similar spelling is an alias.
- Do not assume a translated term always refers to exactly the same concept in every context.

Wikidata explicitly permits different items to share an alias when their descriptions distinguish them. [4]

### 5.2 Keep language and context visible

Where multilingual or regional naming matters, record the language or locale of each label. Keep transliteration, translation, historical spelling, and official naming distinct where those distinctions affect interpretation.

For example, a company may have a registered Indonesian legal name and an English trading name. Both may refer to the same organization, but the record should preserve which is which rather than flattening them into an undifferentiated list.

## 6. Disambiguation: Resolve the Referent, Not Just the String

Disambiguation is the process of deciding which entity a reference denotes when more than one interpretation is plausible.

A practical workflow:

1. **List competing candidates.** Do not assume the first matching record is correct.
2. **Compare entity types.** A person, organization, place, and product may share the same string.
3. **Inspect contextual clues.** Relevant clues may include location, industry, role, creator, date, product category, or surrounding text.
4. **Check authoritative identifiers.** Use registration records, official catalogues, identifiers, or source-system references when appropriate and available.
5. **Compare independent attributes.** Look for multiple compatible facts, not one superficial match.
6. **Review source quality and provenance.** Establish who published the information, what it directly supports, and when it was valid.
7. **Record the decision and uncertainty.** State why candidates were merged, kept separate, or left unresolved.

This is a practical knowledge-modeling workflow developed for this playbook. It is not a description of Google's or another search engine's entity-linking algorithm. System-side entity extraction and linking remain within Domain 04.

### 6.1 A simple example

Suppose a website mentions “Mercury”. Candidate interpretations could include a planet, a chemical element, a Roman deity, or a brand.

The string alone cannot settle the identity. Nearby context such as “orbital period”, “atomic number”, “Roman mythology”, or a product catalogue may narrow the candidate. The final record should point to the intended subject and retain the evidence that supports the choice.

This is an illustrative example, not a claim about any search engine's disambiguation behavior.

### 6.2 When evidence is not enough

Use an explicit state such as:

- **Confirmed:** identity supported by sufficient evidence for the model's purpose.
- **Provisional:** plausible candidate, but important evidence is missing.
- **Ambiguous:** multiple candidates remain plausible.
- **Rejected:** evidence indicates the candidate is outside scope or refers to a different subject.
- **Merged / split:** a documented modeling decision changed the record structure.

These are suggested project workflow labels, not a universal standard.

## 7. Identity Evidence, Provenance, and Confidence

Identity decisions should be auditable. For each important attribute or identity assertion, record:

- The source or issuing authority.
- The exact source record or URL where practical.
- What the source directly establishes.
- Whether the source is primary, authoritative, secondary, or user-generated.
- The date checked and, where relevant, the period the fact applies to.
- Conflicting evidence or unresolved uncertainty.
- The person, team, or process responsible for the decision.

W3C PROV-O provides a formal vocabulary for representing provenance, including entities, activities, agents, and derivation relationships. A project does not need to implement PROV-O to benefit from the underlying principle: preserve where information came from and how a record was produced or changed. [5]

Do not use a numeric confidence score unless the project defines what the score means, how it is assigned, and how it is calibrated. “92% confident” is decoration if nobody can explain the method.

### 7.1 Distinguish evidence from inference

A source may establish that an organization uses a particular trading name. It may not establish that two similarly named organizations are the same legal entity.

Keep these states separate:

- **Source assertion:** what the source explicitly says.
- **Observed data:** what the researcher directly observes.
- **Model interpretation:** the conclusion drawn from the evidence.
- **Editorial classification:** a category assigned for the project's own organization.
- **Hypothesis:** a plausible but unverified explanation.

A relationship or identity match inferred from context should not be recorded as a verified fact without sufficient supporting evidence.

## 8. The `sameAs` Trap

Schema.org defines `sameAs` as a URL of a reference page that **unambiguously indicates the item's identity**. Its examples include a Wikipedia page, Wikidata entry, or official website. [2]

That definition is narrower than “this page is related to the subject”.

Use the distinction carefully:

- A page about the same entity may be an identity reference if it clearly denotes that exact entity.
- A page about a related organization, parent company, product category, employee, event, or similarly named subject is not automatically an identity match.
- A topical association is not identity.
- A page that merely mentions an entity is not necessarily an identity page.
- A URL being official does not, by itself, prove that every page on that domain denotes the same entity.

If the target cannot be identified unambiguously, do not assert `sameAs`. Use a more appropriate relationship in the knowledge model, or leave the match unresolved. The exact technical implementation of structured data belongs to Domain 10; this section explains the modeling semantics only.

## 9. A Practical Entity Record

The following is an original, lightweight record template for this playbook. It is not a mandatory schema or a prescribed Schema.org implementation.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Internal record ID | Stable reference within the project |
| Preferred label | Main human-readable name |
| Entity type | The class or category used by the model |
| Scope / boundary note | What this record represents—and what it does not |
| Alternate labels | Aliases, translations, former names, abbreviations, with language/context |
| Identifiers | Value, scheme, issuer, scope, and source |
| Disambiguating description | Short explanation distinguishing it from similar candidates |
| Attributes | Relevant facts with source and temporal context |
| Identity evidence | Evidence supporting the match or distinction |
| Provenance | Source, access/review date, responsible agent or process |
| Status | Confirmed, provisional, ambiguous, rejected, merged, or split |
| Open questions | Missing facts, conflicts, and review tasks |
| Last reviewed | Date of latest identity and attribute review |

For larger projects, separate the entity record from its evidence records and time-sensitive attribute assertions. That makes updates and conflicting sources easier to manage.

## 10. Common Identity and Disambiguation Failures

| Failure | Why it breaks the model | Better practice |
|---|---|---|
| Treating a shared name as a unique identity | Creates false merges | Compare type, context, identifiers, and evidence |
| Treating every alias as a separate entity | Creates false splits | Attach verified naming variants to the correct subject |
| Treating an alias as proof of identity | Similar strings can refer to different subjects | Validate the referent independently |
| Confusing a page URL with an entity identifier | A document can describe a subject without being that subject | Record page/document and entity separately |
| Treating every identifier as globally unique | Identifier scope may be limited to an issuing system or category | Record scheme, issuer, and scope |
| Using `sameAs` for related pages | Overstates identity equivalence | Assert only unambiguous identity references |
| Overwriting changing attributes | Erases useful historical context | Preserve source and temporal validity |
| Importing external records without checking definitions | External datasets may use different boundaries and policies | Review scope, provenance, and source rules |
| Assigning arbitrary confidence percentages | Creates false precision | Use evidence-based status labels or a defined scoring method |

## 11. Handoff to Relationship Modeling

After identity and attributes are sufficiently resolved, the model can describe how entities relate to one another.

Topic [09.4 — Semantic Relationships & Relationship Modeling](README.md#domain-structure) will own relationship meaning, direction, constraints, context, provenance, and the distinction between asserted facts and inferred associations. Topic 09.3 supplies better-defined entity records; it does not decide every relationship between them.

A relationship should not be used to patch an unresolved identity problem. First establish which subjects the records represent. Then model how those subjects are connected.

## 12. Key Takeaways

- Entity identity is a modeling decision supported by evidence, not a string-matching result.
- Define the unit and boundary before merging or splitting records.
- Attributes should be selected for the entity type and the model's purpose.
- Names, labels, aliases, identifiers, URLs, and IRIs serve different roles.
- An alias does not prove identity; different names do not automatically mean different entities.
- Record identifier scope, issuer, provenance, and temporal context.
- Disambiguate using multiple relevant clues and reliable sources; preserve uncertainty when evidence is insufficient.
- Schema.org `sameAs` is for an unambiguous identity reference, not a generic related-to link.
- Do not confuse website-side knowledge modeling with search-engine entity-linking algorithms or structured-data implementation.
- Better entity records improve the integrity of the knowledge model; they do not establish a ranking benefit.
