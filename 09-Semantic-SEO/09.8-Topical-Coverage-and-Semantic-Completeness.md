# 09.8 — Topical Coverage & Semantic Completeness

**Domain:** [09 — Semantic SEO](README.md)  
**Topic ID:** 09.8  
**Status:** Documented; source-checked  
**Review date:** 2026-11-21

Topical coverage asks one direct question: **does this knowledge model represent the parts of the subject that matter for its declared scope and intended use?**

The goal is not to include every term, entity, or remotely related idea. The goal is to identify meaningful omissions, unnecessary duplication, weakly supported concepts, and relationships that the model needs but does not represent.

Completeness is always relative to a declared scope, purpose, audience, and set of requirements. A model can be adequate for one use and inadequate for another. “Cover everything” is not a usable requirement; it is how projects end up with an infinite spreadsheet.

This topic evaluates the **knowledge model** built in [09.7](09.7-Topical-Modeling-and-Knowledge-Domains.md). It does not audit the editorial quality of published pages (Domain 08), measure keyword-volume coverage (Domain 05), validate graph syntax or ontology consistency in depth (09.10), or determine search rankings.

## 1. What Is Topical Coverage?

**Topical coverage** is the extent to which a topical model represents the relevant concepts, attributes, relationships, distinctions, and information requirements inside its declared knowledge domain.

**Semantic completeness** is the adequacy of that representation against explicit scope and purpose. It does not mean exhaustive representation of all knowledge about a subject.

Ontology-evaluation literature treats completeness as appropriate coverage of the domain of interest. It also distinguishes completeness from accuracy, conciseness, consistency, adaptability, and clarity. These are related quality dimensions, not interchangeable labels. [2]

A model may therefore be:

- **Broad but shallow:** many concepts appear, but important attributes or relationships are missing.
- **Narrow but adequate:** a deliberately limited model covers its stated task well.
- **Detailed but noisy:** it contains many low-relevance or unsupported concepts.
- **Well organized but incomplete:** the hierarchy is tidy, yet important concepts or connections are absent.
- **Apparently complete but unvalidated:** the team has not tested the model against requirements, evidence, or domain expertise.

The key test is not how large the model looks. It is whether the model represents what its purpose requires—and whether that decision can be defended.

## 2. Coverage Is Relative to Scope

Before looking for gaps, freeze the evaluation frame. Reuse the scope and boundaries defined in 09.7.

Record at least:

1. **Subject boundary:** what knowledge domain is being evaluated.
2. **Intended use:** what the model must help a team understand, organize, or represent.
3. **Audience and information needs:** whose questions or tasks matter in this context.
4. **Required depth:** which concepts need detailed representation and which can remain at a higher level.
5. **Explicit exclusions:** adjacent subjects intentionally outside the model.
6. **Evidence window:** which sources, datasets, expert inputs, and audience research were considered, including their dates and limitations.

The same subject can legitimately produce different models. A model of bicycle braking for consumer maintenance guidance may need practical inspection concepts; a model for mechanical engineering education may need deeper treatment of forces, materials, and system design. Neither is automatically more complete. They have different requirements.

Do not quietly expand the scope whenever a new concept appears. First decide whether it is genuinely required, merely adjacent, or outside the model's purpose.

## 3. What Must Be Covered?

Evaluate more than the presence of concept labels. A useful coverage review considers several layers.

| Coverage layer | What to inspect | Typical gap |
|---|---|---|
| Core concepts | The central concepts required to describe the domain | A necessary concept is absent |
| Subconcepts and distinctions | Important types, states, variants, or contrasting concepts | Two materially different concepts are collapsed |
| Attributes | Properties needed to describe or distinguish concepts | A relevant property is missing or undefined |
| Relationships | Meaningful hierarchical, associative, functional, temporal, or other relationships | Concepts exist but their connection is not represented |
| Dependencies | Prerequisite or explanatory dependencies needed by the model's purpose | A concept relies on an unstated prerequisite |
| Context and qualifiers | Conditions, scope, time, population, units, or circumstances that change meaning | A statement is modeled as universally true when it is conditional |
| Information requirements | Questions or tasks the model is expected to support | The model cannot represent the information needed to answer a required question |
| Boundaries | Included, excluded, and adjacent concepts | The model drifts into unrelated territory |

The distinction between hierarchical and associative relations matters. For example, a narrower concept is not interchangeable with a concept that is merely associated with it. SKOS explicitly distinguishes broader/narrower hierarchical links from associative links, and provides separate properties for those meanings. [1]

A concept inventory that ignores relationships is not a complete topical model. It is just a list with ambition.

## 4. Coverage Gaps, Redundancy, and Noise

Do not treat every review finding as “missing content.” Diagnose the actual model problem.

### 4.1 Missing concept

A concept required by the declared scope or intended use is absent.

Example: a bicycle-braking model includes disc brakes and rim brakes but omits brake pads, despite pad inspection being an explicit requirement of the model.

### 4.2 Missing relationship

Both concepts exist, but a meaningful connection is absent or modeled too vaguely.

Example: the model contains brake pads and rotors but does not represent their contact relationship in the braking process.

### 4.3 Missing attribute or distinction

A concept exists, but the model lacks a property or distinction needed to interpret it correctly.

Example: the model includes brake fluid but does not distinguish fluid specifications relevant to the intended maintenance scope.

### 4.4 Granularity mismatch

A concept is represented too broadly or too narrowly for the intended use.

Example: “brake components” is too broad if the model must distinguish pads, calipers, rotors, levers, and cables for maintenance tasks. Conversely, splitting every minor material variation into a separate top-level concept may add noise.

### 4.5 Redundant representation

The same meaning is represented more than once without a defensible distinction.

Before merging apparent duplicates, check identity, scope, context, and intended meaning. Different labels can refer to one concept; similar labels can refer to different concepts. Identity and disambiguation decisions belong to 09.3.

### 4.6 Irrelevant or out-of-scope concept

A concept may be valid knowledge and still not belong in this model.

Example: a bicycle-braking model may mention motorcycle braking as an adjacent comparison, but a full motorcycle-braking subsystem is outside a bicycle-only scope unless the intended use requires it.

### 4.7 Unsupported or uncertain concept

A concept or relationship has entered the model without adequate evidence, or its status is unclear.

Do not convert an assumption into a fact just to make the map look connected. Preserve provenance and uncertainty; unresolved modeling decisions should remain visible.

### 4.8 Contradiction or inconsistent meaning

Two parts of the model make incompatible assertions, or the same label is used with conflicting meanings. This is a model-integrity issue that may overlap with the validation work in 09.10. Record the finding here when it affects coverage, but do not turn this topic into a full ontology-validation manual. [2]

## 5. Information Requirements and Competency Questions

A practical way to test coverage is to define the questions the model is expected to support.

In ontology engineering, **competency questions (CQs)** are questions an ontology or knowledge model should be able to answer. They help define scope, identify relevant concepts and relationships, and evaluate whether the model represents its requirements. A 2023 survey of 63 ontology engineers found that respondents used CQs mainly for scope definition and evaluation, while also reporting difficulties in writing and managing them. The survey is evidence about ontology-engineering practice, not a universal SEO benchmark. [3]

For a topical model, questions can remain in plain language. Formal query languages are not required unless the model is being implemented as a machine-queryable ontology or knowledge base.

Illustrative questions for a bicycle-braking model:

- What are the main bicycle brake types in scope?
- Which components make up a disc-brake assembly?
- How do brake pads interact with a rotor?
- Which conditions can affect braking performance?
- Which inspection tasks apply to each brake type?
- Which maintenance procedures are explicitly outside this model?

Each question should map to the concepts, attributes, relationships, and context needed to answer it.

A question that cannot be answered may reveal:
- a missing concept;
- a missing relationship;
- a missing attribute or qualifier;
- an unclear definition;
- a scope decision that was never made; or
- a question that does not belong in this model.

Do not assume every imaginable question must be answerable. CQs are bounded by the model's purpose. A useful set is representative and testable, not an infinite wishlist. [3]

## 6. Evidence Sources for Coverage Assessment

No single source reveals the full domain. Combine evidence sources that serve different purposes, and record what each can and cannot establish.

| Evidence source | What it can reveal | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-matter experts | Important distinctions, accepted terminology, domain dependencies, and known omissions | Expert knowledge may be partial, outdated, or shaped by a particular specialty |
| Primary technical references and standards | Established definitions, classifications, requirements, and mechanisms within their scope | A source covers its own remit, not necessarily the entire subject |
| First-party product or service documentation | Real components, supported functions, constraints, and operational terminology | Vendor-specific scope and commercial framing |
| Audience research and support questions | Actual information needs, recurring confusion, and practical tasks | Observed questions are not a complete model of the underlying subject |
| Search queries and SERP observations | Language people use and visible search-task patterns | Query data is not proof that a phrase represents a distinct concept or that the subject is fully covered |
| Existing knowledge models or controlled vocabularies | Candidate concepts, definitions, and relationships for comparison | Another model may have different scope, assumptions, granularity, or quality |
| Relevant document or data collections | Concepts and relationships represented in a defined corpus | Corpus selection, publication bias, duplication, and missing sources can distort the result |

Ontology evaluation research describes several complementary approaches, including comparison with reference models, application-based evaluation, data-source comparison, and expert or user review. Each approach answers a different question; none should be treated as a universal completeness test. [2]

Search-query research can be a useful input, but it belongs to Domain 05. Query frequency or the number of keyword variations does not prove conceptual importance, factual validity, or semantic completeness.

## 7. A Practical Topical Coverage Workflow

Use this sequence after a first topical model exists.

### Step 1 — Freeze the evaluation scope

Record the subject, purpose, audience, required depth, exclusions, and evidence window. If these are vague, the coverage verdict will be vague too.

### Step 2 — Define representative information requirements

Write the questions or tasks the model must support. Include core, explanatory, comparison, and boundary questions where relevant. Keep each question tied to the declared purpose.

### Step 3 — Build a coverage matrix

Map every requirement to the model elements needed to satisfy it.

| Requirement / question | Concepts | Attributes | Relationships | Context | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identify brake types in scope | Brake type; disc brake; rim brake | Type classification | Is-a / type-of | Bicycle context | Covered |
| Explain disc-brake interaction | Pad; rotor | Contact surface; material where relevant | Contacts / applies friction to | Braking operation | Partial |
| Determine inspection needs | Pad; rotor; wear condition | Wear indicators; limits from authoritative sources | Component-of; inspected-for | Specific brake design | Gap |
| Compare motorcycle and bicycle brakes | Bicycle brake; motorcycle brake | Comparison dimensions | Compared-with | Out-of-scope boundary | Excluded |

This is an original illustrative matrix, not an industry standard. The status labels are project conventions.

### Step 4 — Inspect each coverage layer

Review concepts, distinctions, attributes, relationships, dependencies, context, and boundaries. Do not mark a requirement covered merely because one related term appears in the model.

### Step 5 — Compare with independent evidence

Check the model against appropriate technical sources, expert input, audience evidence, and relevant existing vocabularies or models. Record source scope and date. A single source or competitor website is not a complete ground truth.

### Step 6 — Classify every finding

Use clear categories: missing, partial, redundant, irrelevant, unsupported, ambiguous, conflicting, or excluded by design. Separate confirmed gaps from questions that still need research.

### Step 7 — Prioritize by relevance and consequence

Consider:
- importance to the declared purpose;
- impact on required questions or tasks;
- strength and reliability of evidence;
- risk of misunderstanding if the gap remains;
- dependencies on other concepts;
- effort and maintenance cost.

This is a decision framework, not a universal numerical scoring formula. Do not invent weights or thresholds and present them as scientifically validated.

### Step 8 — Revise the model and preserve provenance

Add, remove, split, merge, or redefine model elements only when the evidence and scope justify the change. Record the reason, source, confidence, owner, and date. Revisit affected relationships and dependent concepts.

### Step 9 — Re-test the requirements

Run the representative questions again. A revision that fixes one gap may create a contradiction, duplicate, or scope leak elsewhere.

### Step 10 — Produce a coverage assessment

The deliverable should include:
- evaluated model version and date;
- declared scope and intended use;
- evidence sources and limitations;
- requirements or competency questions reviewed;
- coverage matrix;
- confirmed gaps, redundancy, uncertainty, and exclusions;
- prioritized model changes;
- unresolved issues and next review trigger.

## 8. How to Judge Coverage Without Fake Precision

A coverage assessment should be explainable, not cosmetically mathematical.

Use qualitative statuses such as:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Covered | The model represents the required concepts and their relevant semantics sufficiently for this requirement |
| Partially covered | Some required elements exist, but an important concept, attribute, relationship, or qualifier is missing |
| Not covered | Required representation is absent |
| Uncertain | Evidence or scope is insufficient to decide |
| Out of scope | The requirement is deliberately excluded, with a recorded rationale |
| Not applicable | The requirement does not apply to this model |

These labels are a practical project convention, not a W3C or universal ontology standard.

A percentage may be calculated only when the evaluated requirements have a defensible definition and consistent unit of analysis. Even then, the result describes that specific checklist or sample—not “the percentage of all knowledge covered.” Requirements differ in importance and complexity, and a binary count can hide serious gaps.

Do not use:
- a fixed number of concepts as a completeness target;
- a required number of subtopics or hierarchy levels;
- keyword counts or search volume as a completeness score;
- competitor page counts as ground truth;
- an arbitrary “topical authority” score;
- a single aggregate score that hides unsupported concepts or critical omissions.

Completeness is a reasoned judgment against explicit requirements and evidence, not a trophy for the biggest diagram.

## 9. Topical Coverage Is Not Content or Keyword Coverage

These activities connect, but they evaluate different objects.

| Activity | What is evaluated | Canonical owner |
|---|---|---|
| Topical coverage | Whether the knowledge model represents the relevant concepts, relationships, attributes, and requirements within scope | Domain 09.8 |
| Keyword coverage and query clustering | Whether search-query groups and language patterns have been identified and mapped for research decisions | Domain 05 |
| Editorial content coverage | Whether published content assets adequately and accurately serve their intended reader tasks | Domain 08.10 |
| Page-level / on-page audit | Whether page elements and markup are correctly implemented | Domain 07 |
| Technical validation | Whether technical delivery and structured implementations meet their requirements | Domain 10 |
| Semantic model governance | Whether model integrity, provenance, change control, and maintenance are managed over time | Domain 09.10 |

A knowledge model may contain a concept that has no published page yet. That is a model-to-website planning question for 09.9 and the relevant editorial workflow—not proof that the model itself is incomplete.

Likewise, a published article can mention every concept in a model and still explain them badly. That is an editorial quality problem, not a semantic coverage verdict.

Keyword clusters group queries by shared search task; they do not establish the full conceptual structure of a domain. Domain 05 owns that research method. [05.7](../05-Keyword-and-Search-Intent/05.7-Keyword-Clustering-and-Query-Grouping.md)

## 10. Example: Coverage Review of a Bicycle-Braking Model

**Illustrative example only.** The model is hypothetical and is not a technical maintenance reference.

Declared scope: explain the main concepts and relationships needed for a beginner-level overview of bicycle braking systems. Detailed repair procedures, legal requirements, and motorcycle systems are excluded.

Initial model elements:
- brake types;
- disc brakes;
- rim brakes;
- brake pads;
- rotors;
- calipers;
- braking force.

Review findings:
1. **Missing relationship:** pads and rotors are present, but their interaction is not modeled.
2. **Missing concept:** brake levers are absent even though the model includes how braking is initiated.
3. **Missing context:** “braking force” appears without distinguishing the level of explanation intended.
4. **Potential redundancy:** “disc brake rotor” and “rotor” may be duplicate labels, but this requires identity and definition review before merging.
5. **Out of scope:** motorcycle braking components are not added merely because they are mechanically related.
6. **Evidence gap:** any detailed claim about wear limits or fluid specifications is deferred until authoritative technical sources are selected.

The review does not conclude that the website needs six new articles. It concludes that the conceptual model needs specific corrections, and that some proposed details require better evidence.

## 11. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating completeness as “include everything” | Scope becomes unbounded | Define purpose, boundaries, and exclusions first |
| Counting concepts as a quality score | Volume says little about relevance or meaning | Test requirements and semantic relationships |
| Treating every query as a concept | Queries can express tasks, variants, ambiguity, or multiple concepts | Use query data as evidence, then model the underlying subject |
| Equating model coverage with published URL count | A concept does not automatically require its own page | Evaluate the model first; translate it into architecture separately |
| Adding competitor topics automatically | Competitor coverage can be irrelevant, incorrect, or shaped by another scope | Verify against primary evidence and intended use |
| Merging similar labels without identity checks | Different concepts may be collapsed | Apply 09.3 identity and disambiguation methods |
| Ignoring relationships and context | A term list can look broad while remaining semantically weak | Review relations, qualifiers, dependencies, and evidence |
| Treating inferred associations as facts | The model gains false certainty | Preserve provenance and uncertainty |
| Using one source corpus as complete ground truth | The corpus may be incomplete or biased | Triangulate evidence and record limitations |
| Claiming rankings improve when coverage rises | Model quality does not establish a ranking effect | Evaluate search outcomes separately with appropriate evidence |

## 13. Key Takeaways

- Coverage is adequacy against a declared scope and purpose—not an attempt to model all knowledge.
- Evaluate concepts, distinctions, attributes, relationships, dependencies, context, and information requirements.
- Use competency questions and evidence sources to expose gaps; neither is a magic completeness guarantee.
- Separate missing, redundant, irrelevant, unsupported, ambiguous, and out-of-scope elements.
- A large model is not automatically a good model. Relevance, evidence, and semantic precision matter.
- Topical-model coverage, keyword coverage, and editorial content coverage are different evaluations with different owners.
- A coverage assessment informs later website architecture; it does not automatically create pages or predict rankings.
