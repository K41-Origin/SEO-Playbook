# 07.10 — On-Page SEO Auditing & Quality Assessment

## Purpose of an On-Page Audit

An on-page audit is a structured examination of whether a page's elements and structure are accurate, consistent, understandable, and supportive of its purpose. It is not a ritual of checking every box, a contest to find as many errors as possible, or a ranking-score generator.

Topic 07.10 turns the principles and elements in 07.1–07.8 into a repeatable evaluation framework. Implementation and change workflow belong to 07.9; this audit focuses on **page condition, evidence, findings, risk level, and follow-up recommendations**.

Quality auditing must inspect enough page substance to ensure on-page elements do not promise something the content fails to deliver. Deep editorial assessment, content strategy, and production remain with [Domain 08 — Content SEO](../08-Content-SEO/README.md). Google recommends evaluating originality, completeness, accuracy, expertise, and whether a page genuinely helps readers achieve their goals. [1]

## 1. What Does an On-Page Audit Assess?

The audit examines two connected layers:

1. **Page elements:** title element, meta description, headings, URL, images, and semantic HTML.
2. **Page consistency:** whether those elements accurately represent the actual content, page purpose, and user needs.

An element can technically exist and still be poor. A title may be present but misleading. Alt text may exist but fail to describe an image's function. Headings may look orderly while the page does not answer the need. Audit context, not just markup presence.

### 1.1 Audit scope

| Area | Core checks | Canonical reference |
|---|---|---|
| Page purpose & relevance | Purpose is clear; main content serves it; elements do not promise unavailable information | [07.2](07.2-Page-Level-Relevance-and-Optimization-Principles.md) |
| HTML title | Present, descriptive, specific, accurate, not boilerplate or keyword-stuffed | [07.3](07.3-Title-Tags-and-Page-Title-Optimization.md) |
| Meta description | Relevant, suitably unique, non-misleading summary of content | [07.4](07.4-Meta-Descriptions-and-Snippet-Optimization.md) |
| Headings | Clear section labels, sensible hierarchy, headings match section content | [07.5](07.5-Heading-Structure-and-Hierarchy-Optimization.md) |
| URL wording | Readable, descriptive, consistent, aligned with page identity | [07.6](07.6-URL-Structure-and-Page-Level-URL-Optimization.md) |
| Image elements | Relevant images; alt text fits function; filename/caption helps where relevant | [07.7](07.7-Image-SEO-and-Image-Element-Optimization.md) |
| HTML semantics | Semantic elements match their roles; page structure and landmarks make sense | [07.8](07.8-HTML-Semantics-and-Page-Element-Optimization.md) |
| Cross-element consistency | Title, H1, URL, images, metadata, and content do not contradict one another | [07.2](07.2-Page-Level-Relevance-and-Optimization-Principles.md) |

### 1.2 What is not an on-page audit

An audit may discover indicators in other areas, but it does not take over their domain ownership.

- Crawlability, rendering, HTTP status, canonicalization, robots directives, indexing, structured-data implementation, and Core Web Vitals → **Domain 10 — Technical SEO (planned)**.
- Crawling and indexing system behavior → **Domain 02**.
- Ranking systems and ranking diagnosis → **Domain 03**.
- Keyword research, intent, and keyword-to-page mapping → **Domain 05**.
- Actual SERP title links, snippets, and search features → **Domain 06**.
- Editorial strategy and deep substantive content assessment → **Domain 08 — Content SEO**.
- Entity architecture and topical modeling → [Domain 09 — Semantic SEO](../09-Semantic-SEO/README.md).
- Internal-link architecture and anchor text → **Domain 11 (planned)**.
- Organic performance, attribution, and reporting → **Domain 14 (planned)**.

Do not turn technical findings into copywriting recommendations merely because the audit is being performed by an on-page team. A wrong diagnosis remains wrong, even in a tidy spreadsheet.

## 2. Defensible Audit Principles

### 2.1 Audit against page purpose
Before evaluating elements, understand what the page is meant to do. Use the page brief, Domain 05 mapping, actual content, and available business/user context. If the page purpose itself is unclear, record that as a brief-level limitation or finding; do not invent intent from keywords alone.

### 2.2 Inspect the actual page, not only a source snippet
Inspect what users actually receive, including relevant mobile presentation, dynamically loaded elements, and important interactions. If HTML source and rendered DOM differ materially, preserve both as evidence and escalate rendering concerns to Domain 10.

### 2.3 Separate facts, interpretation, and recommendation
Each finding should separate:
- **Observation:** a condition that can be seen or measured.
- **Evidence:** URL, snapshot, markup, data, or feedback supporting the observation.
- **Interpretation:** why the condition may be problematic in page context.
- **Recommendation:** proposed action.
- **Validation:** how to confirm the action solved the problem.

Example: “The inspected HTML has no title element” is an observation. “This caused rankings to fall” is a causal claim not proven by that observation.

### 2.4 Not every deviation requires a fix
There is no universal requirement that every URL be indexed, every page use one title pattern, or every heading be changed to satisfy a checklist. Google explains that some URLs may appropriately remain unindexed, such as duplicates or pages intentionally marked noindex. Evaluate purpose and page status. [2]

### 2.5 An audit is not a ranking score
Google provides content-quality self-assessment guidance, not a universal weighted on-page audit formula. Google's documentation also says E-E-A-T is not a single specific ranking factor. Do not invent a score such as “SEO health 92/100” and present it as Google's assessment. [1]

## 3. Audit Workflow

### Step 1 — Define the audit scope
Specify audit purpose (baseline, quality review, pre-publication review, remediation check, or periodic audit); page types; URL boundary (one page, template group, directory, or site sample); relevant language, country, device, and access conditions; included/excluded elements; audit owner, reviewer, and change approver.

Do not describe a sample audit as a full audit. State the population, sample size, selection method, and coverage limitations. For large sites, group URLs by template and page function before sampling.

### Step 2 — Establish page purpose and context
For each page or page group, record main purpose; audience or user need; page type and template; relevant query cluster or mapping if available; expected main user action; and authoritative sources for facts, claims, and time-sensitive information.

If mapping is missing or appears unsuitable, mark a dependency for Domain 05. Do not make hidden keyword decisions as part of an element audit.

### Step 3 — Capture the baseline
Preserve audit date and URL; screenshot or page snapshot; HTML title, meta description, headings, URL, and image elements; relevant markup/DOM; desktop and mobile views where relevant; available Search Console data or user feedback with date range and limitations; and page implementation version/status.

A baseline separates the initial condition from later changes. Without it, the team has only memory and confidence—neither is a documentation system.

### Step 4 — Inspect page purpose and main-content fit
Check whether the main content genuinely serves page purpose; readers can understand what the page offers; essential information is available and findable; important claims have appropriate evidence; outdated, contradictory, or harmful information is absent; the page provides clear value rather than merely repeating other sources; and page elements honestly describe the content's scope.

Google recommends evaluating originality, completeness, accuracy, source clarity, and reader satisfaction. These are evaluation prompts, not a numeric ranking checklist. [1]

An on-page audit may flag visible substantive problems, but a full editorial review should involve the content owner or appropriate SME.

### Step 5 — Inspect each on-page element

#### 5.1 HTML title
Check that the title element exists and is not empty; accurately and specifically describes the page; avoids excessive boilerplate and useless repetition; does not promise content the page lacks; is semantically consistent with main content and primary heading; and is not incorrectly applied across a template.

Do not impose an artificial character limit or require a keyword in the first position. Detailed criteria belong to [07.3](07.3-Title-Tags-and-Page-Title-Optimization.md).

#### 5.2 Meta description
Check that a description exists where managed; is specific, accurate, and useful for understanding the content; is not generic boilerplate copied across pages without context; does not include outdated promises, prices, availability, or claims; and avoids unnatural keyword repetition.

A meta description not appearing as Google's snippet does not automatically mean the source element is wrong. Google may select a snippet from page content and adapt it to the query. Keep source-element auditing separate from SERP-display auditing. [3]

#### 5.3 Headings
Check whether headings help readers understand structure; describe the sections they introduce; follow a sensible document hierarchy; avoid empty, confusingly duplicated, or overly generic labels; and are not used solely to achieve a visual font size.

Do not automatically flag multiple H1 elements as an SEO error. Evaluate actual structure and accessibility. One clear H1 can be a useful editorial convention, not a universal ranking rule. See [07.5](07.5-Heading-Structure-and-Hierarchy-Optimization.md).

#### 5.4 URL
Check that the URL matches the intended page; path is readable and sufficiently descriptive; casing and separators are consistent; changes are documented; and slug matches page content and identity.

This audit assesses wording and page-level structure. Redirects, canonicalization, parameters, response codes, and technical URL changes must be handled with Domain 10. Do not change a URL solely to chase a keyword.

#### 5.5 Images
For informative or functional images, check relevance and context; whether alternative text conveys required information or function; whether decorative images appropriately use empty alt text; whether functional images describe the action or link purpose; whether filenames add useful context; whether captions help when visual context needs explanation; and whether important images load correctly on the actual page.

Do not judge alt text by keyword presence. Judge it by image purpose and user need. File formats, compression, responsive delivery, lazy loading, and performance belong to Domain 10. See [07.7](07.7-Image-SEO-and-Image-Element-Optimization.md).

#### 5.6 Semantic HTML
Check whether elements such as `main`, `article`, `section`, `nav`, `header`, `footer`, and `aside` are used according to their meaning and content structure. Check whether headings and landmarks complement one another and, where accessibility is in scope, whether keyboard and assistive-technology navigation remain coherent.

Automated tools can identify some problems but cannot independently establish that a site is fully accessible; competent human evaluation is still needed. [8]

Do not infer a ranking boost from a particular semantic tag. Semantic HTML auditing assesses structural correctness and accessibility, not algorithmic weight. See [07.8](07.8-HTML-Semantics-and-Page-Element-Optimization.md).

### Step 6 — Check cross-element consistency

| Combination | Audit question |
|---|---|
| Title ↔ main content | Does the title promise a topic/scope that is actually covered? |
| H1 ↔ title | Are they semantically consistent even if wording differs? |
| Meta description ↔ page content | Does the summary represent information actually available? |
| URL ↔ page purpose | Does the path still describe page identity? |
| Image ↔ surrounding text | Do image and alternative text fit context and function? |
| Headings ↔ sections | Do headings label the correct sections? |
| Semantic regions ↔ visible layout | Does document structure reflect visible content organization? |

An element that looks good in isolation does not necessarily create a coherent page. Look for contradictions, not just individual errors.

### Step 7 — Check search and technical evidence where relevant

Use Search Console only when access and data are available. Choose tools based on the question:

- **URL Inspection:** checks Google's known information about a URL, indexing status, selected canonical, and live test. Indexed data and live test are different views; live test does not replace index data. “URL is on Google” is not a guarantee of search appearance. [4]
- **Page indexing report:** identifies site-level patterns in indexed and non-indexed URLs and reasons for exclusion. Not every URL should be indexed; check whether exclusions fit the page's purpose. [2]
- **Search performance report:** provides query, page, impressions, clicks, CTR, and trend context. These metrics are defined in Google's reporting documentation, but performance data does not prove a specific on-page issue caused a ranking or traffic change. [5]
- **Manual Actions and Security Issues reports:** help identify policy or security issues that cannot be inferred from page-element inspection alone. Search Console provides dedicated reports for these areas. [6]

If the audit finds problems with HTTP status, rendering, robots, canonical, indexing, structured data, or technical performance, create a separate finding and escalate to Domain 10. Do not merge technical and editorial findings into one vague diagnosis.

### Step 8 — Classify findings

Every finding needs a category and evidence.

| Category | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy / mismatch | Element or content is inaccurate, misleading, or contradictory | Title promises a complete guide but page covers one part |
| Missing / incomplete | Important element absent or information inadequate | Informative image lacks suitable alternative text |
| Structural / semantic | Structure or hierarchy does not represent content properly | Heading does not describe its section |
| Duplication / template | Repeated pattern or template reduces page specificity | Generic description reused across unrelated pages |
| Usability / accessibility | Condition obstructs understanding, navigation, or use | Navigation landmarks cannot be distinguished; functional image control has no clear name |
| Technical dependency | Finding requires infrastructure or implementation review | Rendered HTML differs from expected; canonical is incorrect |
| Policy / trust risk | Potential manipulation, false information, or user risk | Hidden text for manipulation, false claims, or injected content. Google documents hidden text, cloaking, and hacked content in its spam policies; record the indicator and route policy verification through the appropriate process. [10] |

This is an internal audit taxonomy, not an official Google classification.

### Step 9 — Assign severity and priority

Separate **severity** (how serious the problem is) from **priority** (work order). Do not combine them into a ranking score.

| Severity | Operational definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Serious safety, security, legal, trust, or core-function risk; or a policy-violation indicator requiring immediate review | Dangerous incorrect instruction; hacked page displaying harmful content |
| High | Material mismatch that undermines page purpose or may significantly mislead users | Title/content materially differ; main information is wrong or missing |
| Medium | Real issue reducing clarity, completeness, consistency, or accessibility without disabling core function | Ambiguous headings; generic meta description; inadequate informative alt text |
| Low | Minor improvement with limited impact or non-critical editorial preference | Wording could be shorter without a real comprehension issue |
| Informational | Observation or dependency worth recording but not yet shown to be a defect | Intentional template variation; question awaiting content-owner confirmation |

Severity should consider user/page-purpose impact; safety, security, legal, reputational, and policy risk; scope (one URL, one template, or many pages); evidence strength and diagnostic confidence; and possible side effects of a change.

**Priority** may use P0–P3 from workflow 07.9. This is an internal heuristic, not a Google standard, ranking formula, or universal quality measure. Explain when priority differs from severity.

### Step 10 — Recommend and assign remediation

Each actionable finding should include:
- finding ID;
- URL or template group;
- category and severity;
- observation and evidence;
- impact on page purpose/user;
- specific recommendation;
- action owner and relevant domain;
- risks/dependencies;
- acceptance criteria;
- status and target date, if assigned.

Do not write “optimize SEO” or “add keywords.” That is not an action; it is fog.

### Step 11 — Recheck and close findings

After remediation:
1. Reinspect the affected URL or sample.
2. Compare results with baseline.
3. Ensure changes did not cause regressions in other elements.
4. Update evidence and finding status.
5. Mark **Resolved**, **Partially resolved**, **Accepted risk**, **Not reproducible**, or **Escalated**.
6. Record the reason when closing without a change.

For indexing issues, use appropriate technical tools. An indexing request does not guarantee that a URL will be added immediately or at all. [7]

## 4. Severity vs. Priority: Keep Them Separate

Severity answers: **how serious is the problem?** Priority answers: **when should the team work on it?**

Examples:
- Incorrect alt text on a decorative image may have low severity or need no action.
- Missing alternative text on an important functional image control may be a more serious accessibility issue.
- A wrong title on one important page may deserve higher priority than many cosmetic problems on low-value pages.
- A template issue affecting thousands of pages may need higher priority because of scope, even if each instance looks small.

These are illustrative. Final severity depends on context, evidence, impact, and user needs.

## 5. Audit Coverage: Single Page, Template, or Site-Wide

Choose coverage according to risk and scale.

| Audit scope | Suitable for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Single-page audit | Important pages, key landing pages, specific issue investigation | Deep inspection; does not represent other pages |
| Template audit | Pages sharing a CMS/template and element patterns | Check template and data variations; do not assume every instance is identical |
| Representative sample | Large sites with many URLs | Document sampling method, strata, sample size, limitations |
| Full URL inventory review | Small sites or audits requiring full coverage | URL inventory does not mean every URL must be indexed or use identical elements |
| Remediation validation | Confirming earlier findings were fixed | Test corrected URLs and relevant regression samples |

For sampling, consider page type, template, language, device, business status, and risk patterns. Include important pages and unusual cases. W3C's WCAG-EM methodology emphasizes defining scope, exploring the product, selecting a representative sample, evaluating it, and reporting results. It is specific to accessibility evaluation, but its transparency around scope and sampling is a useful audit-process reference. [9] A sample chosen only from the easiest URLs is not defensible representation.

## 6. Audit Report: Minimum Format

The report must support decisions, not merely archive screenshots.

### 6.1 Audit summary

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Audit ID | Audit identifier |
| Audit objective | Review purpose |
| Scope | URLs, templates, or population inspected |
| Sample method | Selection method and rationale |
| Audit date | Inspection date |
| Auditor / reviewer | Person conducting and reviewing |
| Context | Available page purpose, audience, and mapping |
| Limitations | Access, data, sampling, and unverifiable items |
| Summary | Main findings, risks, and dependencies |
| Next review | Date or condition for reassessment |

### 6.2 Finding register

| ID | URL / template | Area | Observation & evidence | Severity | Priority | Recommendation | Owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F-001 | URL or template | Title | Specific evidence | High | P1 | Required change | Relevant team | Open |

This is a template, not actual audit data.

### 6.3 Finding statuses
- **Open:** not yet addressed.
- **In progress:** remediation underway.
- **Resolved:** acceptance criteria met and verified.
- **Partially resolved:** some problems fixed.
- **Accepted risk:** authorized owner accepts risk with documented rationale.
- **Not reproducible:** condition cannot be reproduced during recheck; preserve evidence.
- **Escalated:** ownership transferred to another domain/team.

## 7. Common Audit Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Better practice |
|---|---|---|
| Assuming every URL must be indexed | Duplicates, noindex, or low-value URLs may appropriately be excluded | Evaluate status against URL purpose and exclusion rationale |
| Judging titles by a rigid character limit | Display and page needs differ | Assess accuracy, specificity, and readability |
| Judging meta descriptions by SERP appearance alone | Google may select snippets from page content | Audit source metadata and SERP separately |
| Automatically flagging multiple H1s as ranking errors | No basis for a universal penalty claim | Evaluate structure and document comprehension |
| Calculating keyword density | Does not prove page quality or relevance | Check natural language, accuracy, and purpose fulfillment |
| Requiring keywords in all alt text | Ignores image function and accessibility | Write alt text for image information/function |
| Assuming semantic tags create ranking boosts | Confuses HTML semantics with ranking | Evaluate markup by meaning and accessibility |
| Giving a total SEO score without methodology | Conceals issue types and evidence strength | Report findings, severity, priority, and audit limits |
| Treating indexed status as ranking guarantee | Index eligibility does not guarantee appearance or position | Use indexing and performance data for their respective questions |
| Claiming changes caused traffic increases from simple correlation | Other factors and simultaneous changes exist | Send outcome/attribution analysis to Domain 14 |

## 8. Worked Example — Illustrative Audit

A cycling-equipment category page is inspected as part of a category template.

| Area | Observation | Assessment | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title | Says “all touring equipment,” but page displays bicycle lights only | High — inaccurate scope | Align title with actual content or reassess page purpose |
| Meta description | Identical to many other categories | Medium — insufficient specificity | Use accurate category-specific information |
| H1 | Says “Bicycle Lights” | No issue | Retain |
| URL | Descriptive and consistent with category | No issue | Retain |
| Image | Product image alt text repeats a random filename | Medium — inadequate alternative information | Write alt text based on image function/context |
| Semantic HTML | Category content is inside main with structured section headings | No issue | Retain |
| Technical | Canonical and rendering status not checked | Informational / dependency | Escalate to Domain 10 if within audit objective |

Severity labels are illustrative for this scenario, not universal defaults. The audit does not conclude that rankings rose or fell because no causal evidence is available.

## 9. Quality-Control Checklist

### Scope and evidence
- [ ] Audit objective, scope, population, and sampling method are documented.
- [ ] Page purpose and context have been reviewed.
- [ ] Baseline and inspection date are available.
- [ ] Observations are separated from interpretation and recommendations.
- [ ] Audit limitations are stated.

### Page elements
- [ ] Title is accurate, specific, and aligned with content.
- [ ] Meta description is representative and non-misleading.
- [ ] Headings help readers understand structure.
- [ ] URL wording matches page identity.
- [ ] Image alternative text matches function and context.
- [ ] Semantic HTML matches meaning and document structure.
- [ ] No material cross-element contradictions remain.

### Findings and follow-up
- [ ] Every finding has traceable evidence.
- [ ] Category, severity, and priority have rationale.
- [ ] Recommendations are specific, owned, and have acceptance criteria.
- [ ] Technical and cross-domain issues are escalated to the correct owner.
- [ ] Fixed findings are retested.
- [ ] Report does not present internal checklist or score as a ranking score.

This checklist supports process consistency. It does not replace expert judgment, page context, or actual testing.

## Key Takeaways

- On-page audits assess accuracy, clarity, structure, consistency, and element alignment with page purpose.
- Inspect actual pages and context; do not rely only on tag presence.
- Separate observation, evidence, interpretation, recommendation, and validation.
- Classify findings by issue type; distinguish severity from priority.
- Sampling must be transparent and must not be misrepresented as a full audit.
- Search Console provides indexing and performance evidence but does not prove ranking causes.
- Not every URL should be indexed and not every element needs changing.
- A checklist is not a ranking score. A good audit produces traceable decisions, not impressive-looking numbers.
