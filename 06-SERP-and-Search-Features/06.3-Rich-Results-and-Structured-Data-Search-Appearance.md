# 06.3 — Rich Results & Structured Data Search Appearance

Structured data can help Google understand what a page describes and can make that page eligible for certain enhanced search appearances. It does **not** buy a guaranteed SERP upgrade.

That distinction is the whole game: structured data is a way to describe page content in a standardized format; a rich result is a search presentation Google may choose to show when its systems, feature rules, and context allow it. Valid markup is not a display contract. Google's own documentation says so explicitly. [1][2]

This topic explains the relationship between structured data and rich results, the eligibility model, the limits of validation, and the reasons an eligible-looking page may still appear as an ordinary result. Google Search is the primary documented example. The rules below must not be silently generalized to Bing or other search products.

## 1. Scope: Markup, Eligibility, and Visible Search Appearance

Domain 06 documents what users see in search interfaces. Within that boundary, 06.3 owns the relationship between structured data and enhanced Google Search appearances commonly called **rich results**.

The topic covers:

- What structured data communicates and how Google describes its role.
- The distinction between a Schema.org type, Google's supported structured-data feature, eligibility, and actual display.
- The general conditions Google documents for rich-result eligibility.
- The broad families of rich-result appearances and how to read the feature documentation.
- Why valid markup may not produce a rich result.
- What validation and Search Console reporting can—and cannot—confirm.
- Feature retirement and availability changes that make old SEO advice stale.

This topic does **not** provide a step-by-step markup implementation guide, a Schema.org vocabulary encyclopedia, or a page-level technical SEO checklist. Those belong in the relevant implementation and semantic-architecture domains.

It also does not own the detailed presentation mechanics of every individual feature:

- Featured snippets and direct answers belong to 06.4.
- Knowledge panels and entity-oriented displays belong to 06.5.
- Local and map-based features belong to 06.6.
- News, image, video, shopping, interactive, and AI-powered experiences belong to their respective Domain 06 topics.

## 2. Structured Data as an Input to Search Appearance

Structured data is a machine-readable description of page content. Its vocabularies, serialization formats, generation, and technical validation are owned by [10.11 — Structured Data Implementation & Validation](../10-Technical-SEO/10.11-Structured-Data-Implementation-and-Validation.md). Here, it matters because Google may use it to understand a page and assess whether the page can qualify for a supported search appearance. It does not guarantee a rich result or a ranking change. [1]

Markup must accurately describe information users can access. It must not invent reviews, describe invisible content, or assign an irrelevant type. Google's content and quality policies apply independently of whether the markup is technically parseable. [2]

## 3. Google Search Supports a Selected Set of Features

Schema.org is broader than Google's documented rich-result features. The existence of a Schema.org type or property does not by itself establish Google rich-result eligibility. For the current supported feature set and its eligibility requirements, use Google's Search Gallery and the exact feature guide. [3][4]

Domain 10.11 owns the technical treatment of Schema.org vocabulary, JSON-LD/Microdata/RDFa formats, serialization, and validation. This topic focuses on which documented search appearances may be supported and what their eligibility and visible presentation mean.

## 4. What Counts as a Rich Result?

A **rich result** is an enhanced Google Search appearance supported by structured data. Depending on the feature, the result may display additional information or controls beyond a conventional text result—for example, recipe details, product information, review summaries, event details, or other feature-specific presentation. The exact appearance depends on the feature and can change over time. [3][4]

Google's Search appearance documentation groups structured-data-supported features across several content families, including articles, breadcrumbs, events, job postings, recipes, product snippets and merchant listings, review snippets, local-business and organization information, videos, image metadata, discussion forums, Q&A, profile pages, and other specialized content types. The exact list and product scope must be checked against current feature-specific documentation; a type appearing in a broad documentation list does not by itself prove that it enables a Google web Search rich result. For example, Google clarified that Dataset structured data is used by Dataset Search, not Google Search. [3][10]

This list is an orientation, not a promise that every type is available in every country, language, device, or query. Some features have additional market, content, or eligibility constraints. Others may be in an early-adopter or beta program rather than generally available. Always verify the current feature-specific page before treating a feature as active and applicable. [3]

### 4.1 Rich Result vs. Standard Organic Result

A standard organic result primarily presents the destination and a content preview. A rich result may add structured, feature-specific information to that presentation.

The distinction is about visible appearance, not a different category of organic ranking that publishers can purchase or force. A rich result can be eligible and still not appear. A normal text result may be the presentation Google considers most appropriate for the query and context. [2]

### 4.2 Rich Result vs. Featured Snippet

These terms are not interchangeable.

- **Rich results** are enhanced appearances associated with supported structured-data features and their specific eligibility rules.
- **Featured snippets** are answer-oriented presentations selected by Google's systems from page content. They are not a generic structured-data reward.

The detailed mechanics of featured snippets belong to the planned topic 06.4 — Featured Snippets & Direct Answers. Do not label every visually prominent answer box as a rich result just because it looks richer than a blue link.

### 4.3 Rich Result vs. Knowledge Panel

A knowledge panel is an entity-oriented information presentation. It should not be collapsed into the rich-result category simply because structured data may contribute to Google's understanding of entities and pages. The visible knowledge-panel experience belongs to 06.5.

## 5. The Eligibility Model: Five States That Must Not Be Collapsed

A page can move through several distinct states. Treating them as synonyms creates bad diagnosis and even worse reporting.

| State | What it means | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Markup exists | Structured data is present in the page or rendered output | That Google can access or parse it |
| Markup is parseable | The data can be read in the tested context | That it describes the page accurately or follows all policies |
| Feature requirements are met | The item appears technically eligible under the documented feature rules | That Google has crawled, indexed, or selected it |
| Google has processed the page | Google's systems have encountered and processed the relevant page/data | That a rich result will be displayed |
| Rich result is shown | The enhanced appearance is visible for a particular result context | That it will appear for every query, user, or device |

This is a conceptual diagnostic model, not an official Google status taxonomy. It separates technical validity, policy compliance, processing, eligibility, and actual display so that one green validation result does not get promoted into a claim it cannot support.

Google explicitly states that correct markup and a successful Rich Results Test do not guarantee display. Its documented reasons include context-dependent result selection, markup that does not represent the page's main content, errors the test does not catch, hidden content, and failure to meet general or feature-specific policies. [2]

## 6. General Eligibility Conditions

Google's general structured-data guidelines apply across feature types. Each feature guide can add requirements on top of them. Eligibility is therefore the intersection of the general rules and the specific feature rules—not a checklist where passing one item magically cancels the rest. [2]

### 6.1 The Markup Must Describe the Actual Page

Structured data must be relevant to the page and accurately represent its content. Do not label a woodworking tutorial as a recipe, mark up a product review that is not actually present, or describe details users cannot find on the page. [2]

### 6.2 Required Properties Must Be Present

Each supported rich-result type has its own required properties. Missing a required property can make an item ineligible. Recommended properties can add useful detail, but completeness and accuracy matter more than stuffing every optional field into the markup. [1][2]

Google specifically advises prioritizing fewer, complete, accurate recommended properties over a larger set of incomplete, malformed, or inaccurate data. More markup is not automatically better markup. [1]

### 6.3 Content and Spam Policies Still Apply

Structured data must comply with Google's Search content policies and spam policies. Examples of problematic behavior include fake reviews, irrelevant markup, misleading descriptions, impersonation, and marking up information that is not visible to users. A technically parseable payload can still violate quality requirements. [2]

### 6.4 Google Must Be Able to Access the Relevant Page and Data

Google's technical guidance says not to block structured-data pages from Googlebot using robots.txt, noindex, or other access controls. The data also needs to be available in a form Google can process. If markup is generated with JavaScript, the rendered output and actual crawl behavior matter; source-code assumptions alone are not enough. [2][5]

### 6.5 Use the Most Specific Applicable Type

Google recommends using the most specific applicable Schema.org type and property names, while following the additional guidance for the relevant rich-result feature. Specificity should reflect the content, not an attempt to force a page into a more visually attractive feature. [2]

### 6.6 Feature-Specific Rules Can Narrow Eligibility

A type may have special requirements involving the page's purpose, content, publisher, review source, product status, location, or other conditions. For example, Google's product-snippet documentation distinguishes pages focused on a single product from general product-category pages. That is a feature-specific requirement, not a universal rule for every structured-data type. [6]

## 7. Why Valid Structured Data May Not Appear

This is where unrealistic SEO expectations usually meet the actual documentation.

### 7.1 Eligibility Is Not a Display Guarantee

Google chooses how to present search results based on the query and context. Its documentation identifies variables such as search history, location, and device type. It may choose another feature or a standard text result instead. [2]

### 7.2 The Markup Does Not Match the Main Content

If the structured data describes something different from the page's primary content, or represents information that is hidden or misleading, the page may not qualify even if the syntax parses. [2]

### 7.3 The Feature Has Additional Requirements

A generic schema validator cannot decide whether every Google feature-specific requirement is met. The exact feature guide may contain mandatory properties, content restrictions, publisher qualifications, or availability conditions that a general syntax check does not evaluate. [2][3]

### 7.4 Google Has Not Processed the Latest Version Yet

Testing the current page and seeing valid markup does not prove that Google's indexed representation has already been updated. Google needs to access and process the page. Changes can take time to be reflected in Search; the current live test and indexed-page inspection answer different questions. [5][7]

### 7.5 The Feature Has Changed or Been Retired

Google periodically changes the search appearances it supports. Markup that once enabled a visible feature may become less useful, restricted, or unsupported in Search. The structured data may remain meaningful to other consumers even after Google retires the corresponding Search appearance. [8][9]

The practical rule: check the current Search Gallery and feature guide before implementing a type based on an old tutorial, plugin preset, or screenshot from a few years ago. Search features are product decisions, not permanent entitlements.

## 8. Interpreting Validation and Search Appearance

Technical structured-data implementation and validation are owned by [10.11 — Structured Data Implementation & Validation](../10-Technical-SEO/10.11-Structured-Data-Implementation-and-Validation.md). That topic covers parsing, vocabulary checks, tool selection, live URL and rendering checks, and technical interpretation of validation reports. This topic does not reproduce those procedures.

For SERP interpretation, keep three states distinct: valid markup, feature eligibility or processing, and visible rich-result display. A successful Rich Results Test does not guarantee that a rich result will appear for any query or user. Google's feature rules and actual presentation are context-dependent and can change. [2][8]

Search Console performance data can show supported search-appearance performance where available, but it is not a causal estimate of the effect of markup and does not represent every Schema.org object or every SERP appearance. [8][10]

## 9. Feature Availability Is Not Static: Documented Examples

Old screenshots and tutorials often outlive the feature they describe. These official examples show why feature status needs to be checked instead of assumed.

### 9.1 FAQ and How-To Rich Results

In August 2023, Google announced that FAQ rich results would generally be limited to well-known, authoritative government and health websites. Google also initially restricted How-To rich results to desktop, then confirmed in its September 14, 2023 update that How-To rich results were no longer shown on desktop either and the feature was deprecated. [9]

Do not present FAQ or How-To markup as a reliable way for an ordinary site to obtain those former rich-result displays. The underlying Schema.org vocabulary may still exist, but that is not evidence of current Google Search feature support.

### 9.2 Retired Structured-Data Search Appearances in 2025

On June 12, 2025, Google announced the phase-out of Book Actions, Course Info, Claim Review, Estimated Salary, Learning Video, Special Announcement, and Vehicle Listing from Google Search results. Google stated that the change would not affect page rankings and that use of those structured-data types outside Google Search would not be affected. [8]

That announcement must be read with Google's later correction: on November 5, 2025, Google removed the deprecation banner from Book Actions documentation because a Search feature still used that markup. Book Actions should therefore **not** be listed as fully retired based only on the June announcement. The other six types remained in the documented retirement and reporting changes. [10]

Google's September 8, 2025 update documented the removal of Course Info, Claim Review, Estimated Salary, Learning Video, Special Announcement, and Vehicle Listing from Search Console rich-result reporting, the Rich Results Test, and Search appearance filters beginning September 9, 2025. The Search Console API support window described in that update ran through December 2025. [8]

### 9.3 Dataset and Practice Problem Clarifications

Google's November 5, 2025 documentation update clarified that Dataset structured data is used by Dataset Search, not Google Search, and announced the phase-out of Practice Problem support in Google Search reporting and testing tools beginning January 2026. This is a useful reminder that a structured-data type can remain documented for a different product or purpose without being a Google Search rich-result feature. [10]

The examples above are dated provider announcements, not a complete list of every historical retirement. For current status, use Google's live Search appearance documentation and the specific feature guide.

## 10. A Practical Evaluation Framework

When evaluating whether a structured-data feature is relevant to a page, use this sequence:

1. **Start with the visible outcome.** Identify the specific search appearance that would add useful information for users. Do not start by choosing a schema type because a plugin happens to offer it.
2. **Check Google's current feature list.** Confirm that the feature is currently documented for Google Search and note any beta, regional, device, or publisher limitations. [3]
3. **Read the exact feature guide.** Verify the supported type, required properties, content rules, and technical requirements. [2][3]
4. **Check page-to-markup consistency.** Confirm that the data describes the main content and is visible or otherwise permitted under the feature's guidelines. [2]
5. **Validate the right layer.** Use the Rich Results Test for supported Google features, Schema Markup Validator for generic Schema.org syntax, and URL Inspection for Google's indexed/live page evidence. [5][7]
6. **Monitor after deployment.** Review relevant Search Console reports for supported features and inspect actual SERP appearance in the intended markets and devices. A report is diagnostic evidence, not a display guarantee. [2][10]
7. **Recheck feature status over time.** Follow official documentation updates and announcements before treating historical feature guidance as current. [8][10]

This is an evaluation workflow, not a markup implementation tutorial. Detailed code generation, CMS configuration, server rendering, and technical deployment belong to the implementation domains.

## 11. Common Misreadings

### 11.1 "Schema.org Supports It, So Google Supports It"

No. Schema.org defines a broad vocabulary. Google documents a narrower set of Search features and requirements. Check both layers. [3][4]

### 11.2 "Valid Markup Guarantees a Rich Result"

No. Valid markup can make a page eligible, but Google explicitly does not guarantee display. Context, content quality, feature-specific rules, processing, and search-result selection still matter. [2]

### 11.3 "More Properties Mean Better SEO"

Not automatically. Google emphasizes complete and accurate data over a larger payload full of incomplete or inaccurate properties. Add information because it accurately describes the page and serves the feature—not because more JSON looks impressive. [1][2]

### 11.4 "Structured Data Is a Direct Ranking Boost"

Do not make that claim from eligibility documentation. Structured data can enable enhanced presentation and help Google understand content, but Google's documented rich-result eligibility is not a promise of higher organic ranking. Google also stated that the 2025 retirement of selected rich-result features would not affect page rankings. [1][8]

### 11.5 "A Successful Test Means Google Has Indexed It"

No. A test checks the submitted or fetched test context. URL Inspection provides separate evidence about Google's indexed representation and live URL. Neither guarantees future display. [5][7]

### 11.6 "A Warning Means the Markup Is Useless"

Not necessarily. Interpret the warning against the relevant feature guide. Required-property errors and optional-property recommendations have different implications. [2][7]

### 11.7 "Retired Search Support Makes the Schema Type Useless Everywhere"

No. Google has explicitly said that retiring certain Search appearances does not affect the use of those structured-data types outside Google Search. Other consumers may still use the vocabulary. [8]

### 11.8 "Every Rich-Looking SERP Element Comes From Structured Data"

No. Featured snippets, knowledge panels, AI experiences, and other modules have distinct selection and presentation mechanisms. A visual enhancement alone does not prove that structured data produced it. Keep each feature in its canonical topic.

## Key Takeaways

- Structured data is a standardized description of page content; it is not a ranking shortcut or a guarantee of enhanced presentation.
- Schema.org vocabulary and Google's supported Search features are different scopes.
- A rich result is a specific enhanced Google Search appearance supported by documented feature rules.
- Markup validity, policy compliance, feature eligibility, Google processing, and actual display are separate states.
- Required properties, content accuracy, accessibility, and feature-specific rules all matter.
- The Rich Results Test, Schema Markup Validator, URL Inspection, and Search Console reports answer different questions.
- Search features can be restricted, renamed, retired, or removed from reporting. Historical implementation advice needs a freshness check.
- Google-specific guidance must remain Google-specific. Other search engines require their own current documentation.

**Bottom line:** structured data gives search engines clearer information about a page. It can open the door to certain rich results, but Google controls whether that door is used in a particular SERP. Anyone selling guaranteed stars, carousels, or ranking boosts from markup is selling certainty the documentation does not support.
