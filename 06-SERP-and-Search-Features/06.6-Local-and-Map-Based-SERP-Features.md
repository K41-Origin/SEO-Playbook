# 06.6 — Local & Map-Based SERP Features

**Domain:** [06 — SERP & Search Features](README.md)  
**Topic ID:** 06.6  
**Status:** Documented and source-checked  
**Review date:** 2026-11-19

Local search results connect a query to places or businesses in a geographic context. In Google Search, these presentations can include a map, a compact group of nearby business results, and business information that opens into a more detailed profile. Google Maps provides a location-first search interface for similar place and business discovery. [1][2]

This topic documents the visible local and map-based result formats. It does not teach local SEO strategy, Business Profile optimization, location-page implementation, or Google's undisclosed ranking systems. Those topics have other owners. We are documenting the interface, not turning this page into another local SEO checklist.

## 1. What Local Search Presentation Means

A **local search result** is a result presented in response to a query where geographic relevance matters. The query may explicitly name a place—such as a city or neighborhood—or imply proximity through wording such as "near me." A user may also search for a business or place category without naming a location; Google can use available location context to interpret that request. [1]

Local presentation is not limited to queries containing the word "near." Searches for a business name, a type of service, or a place can surface geographic information when it helps the user find or evaluate a real-world location. Google documents local results across both Search and Maps. [1][2]

The examples and behavior described here are Google-specific unless explicitly stated otherwise. Do not assume another search engine uses identical layouts, terminology, eligibility rules, or local-ranking factors.

## 2. Main Local and Map-Based Result Formats

Google's local presentation is not one fixed widget. The interface can combine map content, business listings, and place details in different arrangements depending on the query, device, and product surface. The following labels are practical descriptions used in this playbook; some are common practitioner terms rather than formal Google feature names.

### 2.1 Local Pack / Map Pack

The **local pack** or **map pack** is a common practitioner label for a compact group of local business results displayed with or near a map in Google Search. It typically helps users compare a small set of relevant nearby businesses without leaving the results page.

Depending on the current interface, the visible module may include:

- A map with location markers.
- Business names and category/context.
- Address or area information.
- Ratings and review counts, when available.
- Opening status or hours, when available.
- Other business attributes or actions, depending on the result and device.

This is a descriptive component list, not a guaranteed template. Not every field appears for every listing, and the order or design can change. Google documents local results and Business Profile information across Search and Maps, but does not publish one permanent visual specification for every local-pack variation. [1][2]

### 2.2 Expanded Local Results

Selecting a local-results link or map-oriented control can take users into a broader local-results interface, often with more businesses and a larger map. Practitioners sometimes call this expanded experience the **Local Finder**.

"Local Finder" is used here as a convenient descriptive label, not as a claim that Google publishes it as a stable product taxonomy. Its layout, entry point, and availability can change. Treat it as an expanded local discovery state rather than a separate ranking system.

### 2.3 Google Maps Search Results

Google Maps provides a map-first discovery experience. Users can search for businesses, services, and places, then inspect listings in relation to geographic features and other locations. A Maps result may expose business details, directions, calls, websites, photos, reviews, and other available actions.

Maps and Search can display related business information, but they are not the same interface. Their layouts, interaction patterns, and result context differ. Google confirms that business information is used to surface local results across Google Search and Google Maps. [2]

### 2.4 Business Profiles in Local Results

A **Google Business Profile** is the business information and management product associated with an eligible business listing. Its visible information can appear in local Search results and Google Maps. A user may open a listing to inspect more detailed business information and available actions.

A Business Profile may show details such as:

- Business name and category.
- Address or service-area context.
- Hours and temporary or special-hour information.
- Phone number, website, and directions.
- Photos, reviews, and other user-contributed content.
- Business attributes, services, or other fields relevant to the category.

The exact fields depend on the business, available information, and current interface. Google states that profile information can combine owner-provided details with public web content, licensed third-party data, user contributions, and information from Google's interactions with a local place or business. [2]

A Business Profile is not synonymous with a local pack. The profile is the business information record and management product; the local pack is a Search presentation that may show a selection of local businesses. Nor should every business-related panel be casually labeled a Knowledge Panel: see 06.5 for entity-oriented panel presentation and the distinction Google documents between general knowledge panels and Business Profiles. [3]

## 3. Where Local Features Appear

Local and map-based presentations can appear across more than one Google surface:

| Surface | Typical presentation | What the user is doing |
|---|---|---|
| Google Search | Local business group, map module, or business information within the SERP | Comparing nearby options while continuing a web search |
| Expanded local results | Broader list of local businesses alongside a map | Exploring more candidates within a local discovery task |
| Google Maps | Map-first place and business results | Finding, comparing, and navigating to places |
| Business Profile detail view | Expanded business information and available actions | Evaluating a particular business or deciding what to do next |

This is a functional comparison, not a promise that every query triggers each surface. The same business may be represented differently across Search and Maps, and not every local query produces a visible map module. Google describes local results as appearing across Search and Maps, while its documentation does not promise a universal layout for all queries or users. [1][2]

## 4. Geographic Context and Query Interpretation

Local results are shaped by the geographic context of the search. Relevant context may come from:

- An explicit location in the query, such as a city or neighborhood.
- The user's available location context.
- The location of a specific place or business being searched.
- The geographic scope implied by the service or place category.

Google states that distance considers how far a business is from the searching customer. If the user does not share a location in the query, Google uses what it knows about the user's location. [1]

This does not mean every local result is simply sorted by straight-line distance. Google documents multiple local-ranking factors, and a farther business can be a better match for a particular search. Do not read a map marker or listing order as a transparent distance ranking.

For SERP observation, record the location context as carefully as the query itself. A search for "dentist" from one neighborhood is not equivalent to the same query from another city. Location, device, language, and time can materially affect what is shown.

## 5. Local Result Ranking: What Google Actually Discloses

Google says local results are mainly based on three factors: **relevance, distance, and prominence**. Google also says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking, and that it keeps the details of its search algorithms confidential. [1]

| Published factor | Google's explanation | What can safely be concluded |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | How well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for. | The business information must be relevant to the query; completeness can help Google understand the business. |
| Distance | How far each potential result is from the customer searching. | Geographic proximity matters, with location context used when the query does not specify a location. |
| Prominence | How well-known a business is. Google mentions information such as links from websites and review quantity/ratings. | Public information and reputation-related evidence can contribute; the complete model and weights are not disclosed. |

These are Google's published high-level explanations, not a complete ranking formula. Google does not publish exact weights, thresholds, or a reproducible equation for local result ordering. [1]

Do not turn these three labels into a fake scoring formula. "Relevance + distance + prominence" is a useful conceptual summary of Google's public guidance—not permission to invent percentages, guaranteed tactics, or a universal ranking checklist.

The ranking mechanism itself belongs to Domain 03. This topic includes only the published factors needed to understand the visible local presentation.

## 6. Where Business Information Comes From

Google states that information shown in Business Profiles can come from several sources:

- Publicly available information, including content crawled from business websites.
- Licensed data from third parties.
- User contributions, including factual edits, photos, and reviews.
- Information based on Google's interactions with a local place or business.
- Information provided by business owners or representatives through Business Profile. [2]

Consequently, the visible listing is not necessarily a verbatim copy of what the business owner entered. Different fields and media may have different sources. Google provides mechanisms to suggest edits or flag information, but that does not mean every suggested change is automatically accepted or immediately displayed. [2]

When auditing a listing, distinguish between:
- Information visible in the Business Profile.
- Information supplied by the business owner.
- User-generated material such as reviews and photos.
- Information sourced or updated by Google and third parties.
- The source attribution that is actually visible to users.

Do not assume that a discrepancy proves a ranking penalty, a manual action, or a specific hidden data source. The public interface does not expose a complete provenance record for every field.

## 7. Local Business Structured Data Is Not a Local Ranking Shortcut

Google's LocalBusiness structured-data documentation describes information a site can provide about a business, including details such as hours and departments. It also describes how structured data can help Google understand page content. [4]

Keep three different things separate:

1. **Website structured data:** machine-readable information published on a website.
2. **Business Profile information:** a business listing that can combine information from owners and other sources.
3. **Local Search presentation:** the result format and listing selection displayed to users.

They can relate to the same business, but they are not interchangeable systems. The presence of LocalBusiness markup does not guarantee a local-pack placement, a particular Maps position, or even that a specific local module will appear. Google's published local-ranking guidance identifies relevance, distance, and prominence; it does not describe LocalBusiness markup as a guaranteed ranking lever. [1][4]

Detailed structured-data implementation and validation belong to Domain 10. Domain 06.3 documents structured-data-supported search appearances. This topic only explains the distinction needed to interpret local results.

## 8. Organic Local Results and Paid Placement

A local interface can contain both unpaid local results and clearly identified advertising. Do not classify every business shown near a map as organic, and do not assume that every map-related placement is an ad.

When inspecting a SERP, check the actual visual labels and interface context. Paid placements should be recorded separately from organic local listings. Google states that payment cannot be used to request a better organic local ranking. [1]

This page does not document every Google Ads local campaign format or Local Services Ads product. Those are advertising products with separate eligibility, auction, and presentation rules; they should not be conflated with organic local result features.

## 9. Feature Availability and Display Variability

Local presentation is contextual. The appearance and content of a local module may vary with:

- Query wording and specificity.
- Geographic context and the location available to Google.
- Device and screen size.
- Search surface (Google Search versus Google Maps).
- Business category and available listing information.
- Market, language, and product changes.
- Whether the query is best served by a local module at all.

The exact set of fields and controls is not a stable universal contract. Google's documentation explains the main local result concepts and information sources, but it does not guarantee that a given query will always show a local pack or a fixed number of listings. [1][2]

A screenshot proves what appeared in that observation context. It does not establish universal availability, permanent feature behavior, or the internal reason a particular business was selected.

## 10. Observing and Auditing Local SERP Features

A useful observation record should include:

1. Search engine and surface: Google Search or Google Maps.
2. Exact query.
3. Explicit location term, if present.
4. Searcher location context, if known and ethically available.
5. Device, language, market, and observation date/time.
6. Which local presentation appeared: map module, compact business group, expanded local results, Maps list, or Business Profile detail view.
7. Visible listing fields, labels, and actions.
8. Whether paid placements were present and how they were labeled.
9. The businesses shown and their visible ordering.
10. Any differences observed across repeated searches or locations.

When comparing results, change one context variable at a time where possible. Do not treat a single ranking snapshot as evidence of a stable local ranking or as proof of a specific algorithm update.

## Key Takeaways

- Local SERP features connect queries with geographically relevant businesses and places across Google Search and Google Maps.
- "Local pack," "map pack," and "Local Finder" are useful descriptive labels, but should not be mistaken for a fixed official taxonomy or layout.
- A local pack is a presentation of selected local results; a Business Profile is the business information product behind a listing.
- Google says local results are based mainly on relevance, distance, and prominence, but does not disclose exact weights or a complete formula.
- Business Profile information can come from owners, public web content, licensed sources, users, and Google's own interactions with places.
- LocalBusiness structured data does not guarantee local-pack visibility or a particular local ranking.
- Organic local results and paid placements must be identified separately.
- A SERP observation is contextual evidence, not a disclosure of Google's internal ranking or data systems.
