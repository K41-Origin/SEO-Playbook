# 03.3 — Ranking Signals & Signal Interaction

Search engines do not rank pages with one magic metric. They evaluate multiple kinds of evidence, and the relevance of that evidence can change with the query, context, and ranking system involved.

**A ranking signal is information a search engine's systems use to help evaluate a candidate result.** Signals are inputs to ranking decisions—not guaranteed standalone ranking factors, universal rules, or a public checklist of what to optimize. [1][2]

This document explains signal categories, page-level and site-wide evidence, and how signals may interact. It does not claim to reveal Google's or Bing's complete signal inventory, weights, or scoring formula.

## 1. Signal, Factor, Feature, and System Are Not Synonyms

These terms are often thrown around as if they mean the same thing. They do not.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Signal** | Evidence or information used by a system when evaluating a candidate. |
| **Feature** | A measurable or represented input available to a model or algorithm. In machine-learning contexts, a feature may encode information derived from one or more raw data sources. |
| **Ranking factor** | A commonly used SEO term for an element believed or documented to influence ranking. Its use is often imprecise; it should not imply a separately weighted, independently controllable variable. |
| **Ranking system** | A broader mechanism that processes signals and other information to perform a ranking-related task. |
| **Model** | A computational representation or learned function used by a system to make predictions, classifications, or scores. |

A signal can be transformed into one or more features. A ranking system can use many features and signals. A named ranking system is not itself a single signal. Google's documentation, for example, describes multiple ranking systems and says they use a variety of page-level signals alongside site-wide signals and classifiers. [1]

**Important:** a source saying that a system considers a category of evidence does not prove that every SEO metric associated with that category is a direct ranking factor.

## 2. Ranking Signals Are Evidence, Not a Public Checklist

Google says its ranking systems look at many factors and signals across indexed content. Its public documentation describes examples and broad categories, but it does not publish a complete list of all signals or their weights. [1][2]

Bing is more explicit about several broad ranking parameters, including relevance, quality and credibility, user engagement, freshness, location and language, and page load time. Microsoft also says their relative importance can vary by search and evolve over time. That list describes Bing's published approach; it must not be copied into a supposed Google ranking-factor checklist. [3]

Keep three evidence levels separate:

- **Documented signal or consideration:** the search provider explicitly says that a type of information is used or considered.
- **Observed association:** research or testing finds a relationship between a metric and ranking outcomes. Association alone does not establish causation or prove that the metric is used directly.
- **Speculation:** a claim about a signal, weight, threshold, or mechanism without adequate supporting evidence.

A ranking correlation is not a backstage pass to the algorithm. It may reflect causation, indirect effects, shared causes, or measurement bias.

## 3. Major Signal Categories

The categories below are a learning taxonomy synthesized from public search-engine documentation. They are not an official Google or Bing classification, and they are not a complete list of ranking inputs.

### 3.1 Query and Content Relevance

These signals help systems assess whether a candidate addresses the subject and meaning of a query.

Publicly described examples include:

- Query terms and their relationship to words in page content.
- The meaning and context of combinations of words.
- Semantic relationships, synonyms, and related concepts.
- Content that addresses the information need rather than merely repeating query terms.

Google describes query meaning and content relevance as parts of its ranking process. Its systems can use language models and synonym understanding to connect related wording, while content terms in prominent locations can provide relevance evidence. [2]

Bing likewise describes direct term matching and semantic equivalents as part of relevance assessment. [3]

The detailed mechanics of query-document relevance belong in **03.4 — Relevance & Query-Document Matching**. Keyword research and searcher intent from the SEO perspective belong in Domain 05.

### 3.2 Content Quality, Helpfulness, and Originality

Search systems may evaluate evidence associated with the usefulness, reliability, originality, and purpose of content.

Google describes systems that aim to surface reliable information, reward original content, and identify helpful results. Its guidance also explains that E-E-A-T—experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness—is not itself a specific ranking factor. Rather, Google says its systems use a mix of factors that can identify qualities associated with E-E-A-T; trust is the most important aspect. [1][4]

This distinction matters:

- E-E-A-T is a conceptual quality framework, not a single score that publishers can optimize.
- Quality rater assessments help Google evaluate whether its systems appear to produce good results; Google says rater data is not used directly in ranking algorithms. [4]
- Originality, effort, accuracy, and usefulness are meaningful content-quality considerations, but no single visible editorial attribute guarantees ranking.

Detailed content-quality systems belong in **03.6 — Content Quality, Helpfulness & Originality Systems**.

### 3.3 Link and Reference Evidence

Links can provide information about relationships between pages and the broader web.

Google confirms that it uses link-analysis systems, including PageRank, to understand how pages link to each other and which pages may be helpful for a query. [1]

That does not mean every backlink has equal value, that raw backlink counts determine rankings, or that every link metric from an SEO tool represents Google's internal link signals.

The ranking mechanics of link graphs and PageRank belong in **03.5 — Link Analysis & PageRank**. Backlink acquisition and off-site implementation belong in Domain 12.

### 3.4 Freshness and Temporal Evidence

Some queries have a stronger need for current information than others.

Google documents freshness systems designed to surface newer information when a query deserves it. Its example contrasts searches about a recent event with searches for evergreen information. [1][2]

Freshness is therefore query-dependent. A newly changed timestamp does not automatically make a page more useful or more relevant, and old content is not automatically inferior.

Detailed temporal ranking belongs in **03.8 — Freshness & Temporal Ranking**.

### 3.5 Context and User Environment

The same query can require different results depending on relevant context.

Google identifies location, language, and device among the information that can affect Search results. Bing documents the use of location, language, device characteristics, and—in permitted circumstances—search history to improve relevance and performance. [2][3]

These are not universal boosts. Context matters when it helps interpret or satisfy the request. A local business query and an abstract scientific definition do not necessarily need the same contextual treatment.

Detailed contextual ranking and personalization belong in [03.9 — Contextual Ranking & Personalization](03.9-Contextual-Ranking-and-Personalization.md).

### 3.6 Page Experience and Usability

Search systems can consider aspects of how usable a page is, but avoid turning every UX metric into a direct ranking factor.

Google says Core Web Vitals are used by its ranking systems. It also explicitly says there is no single page-experience signal, that good report scores do not guarantee top rankings, and that other page-experience aspects may improve user satisfaction without directly helping rankings. [5]

Bing's published ranking explanation also identifies page load time as a consideration, describing slow loading as a potential source of poor user experience. [3]

This does not justify claims that accessibility scores, a perfect Lighthouse score, a specific design pattern, or every usability improvement is a direct ranking signal.

Website-side performance and implementation belong in Domain 10. The ranking role of page experience is covered here only at the signal level.

### 3.7 Interaction and Feedback Evidence

Search providers may use aggregated interaction information to evaluate or improve relevance.

Google says it uses aggregated and anonymized interaction data to create signals that help machine-learned systems estimate relevance. [2] Bing explicitly describes user engagement information such as clicks, time spent after clicking, and query reformulation among its ranking parameters. [3]

Do not flatten those disclosures into "CTR is a Google ranking factor." The sources do not establish that a site's Search Console CTR is a direct, isolated, universally weighted ranking input. Interaction data can be aggregated, transformed, contextual, and used in ways that public descriptions do not fully disclose.

The safe conclusion is narrower: providers publicly acknowledge using some forms of interaction evidence, but the exact data, transformations, safeguards, and ranking impact are not fully public.

### 3.8 Policy, Spam, and Other System-Specific Evidence

Not every ranking-related input exists to reward a page. Some systems identify spam, policy violations, duplication, or other conditions that affect whether and how content appears.

Google documents spam detection systems, deduplication, and certain removal-based demotion signals. These are examples of system-specific ranking or result-treatment mechanisms, not generic quality signals that should be merged into one universal score. [1]

Spam detection and demotion mechanisms belong in **03.7 — Spam Detection & Demotion Systems**. Specialized systems belong in **03.10 — Specialized & Result-Specific Ranking Systems**.

## 4. Page-Level and Site-Wide Signals

A useful distinction is the scope at which evidence is evaluated.

### 4.1 Page-Level Signals

Page-level signals help a system understand or evaluate an individual page. Examples of publicly described page-level considerations include the page's content, relevance to a query, and aspects of its page experience. [1][5]

Page-level evaluation does not mean every signal is isolated from the rest of the site. A page exists within a wider website, link graph, and source context.

### 4.2 Site-Wide Signals and Classifiers

Google explicitly says that site-wide signals and classifiers contribute to its understanding of pages, alongside page-level signals. It also warns that good site-wide signals do not mean every page will rank highly, and poor site-wide signals do not mean every page will rank poorly. [1]

This rules out two simplistic assumptions:

- A strong domain-level reputation does not automatically make every URL a strong result.
- One weak page does not automatically condemn every other page on the domain.

The exact site-wide signals, classifier definitions, aggregation methods, and scope are not fully disclosed. Do not invent a single "domain authority score" and attribute it to Google.

### 4.3 Page-Level and Site-Wide Evidence Can Coexist

A ranking system can consider evidence about a page while also using broader information about its source or site. These scopes are not mutually exclusive.

This is an important conceptual distinction, not a claim that every query or every ranking system applies both scopes in the same way. Google's public documentation confirms that both page-level and site-wide signals exist, but does not disclose a universal weighting or combination formula. [1]

## 5. How Signals Can Interact

Signals do not have to operate as independent switches. Their usefulness can depend on other evidence, the query, the candidate set, and the ranking system.

The interaction patterns below are explanatory models. They help reason about ranking behavior, but they are not published descriptions of Google's internal scoring formula.

### 5.1 Query-Dependent Importance

The relevance of a signal can change with the query.

Google explicitly says that the weight applied to factors varies depending on the nature of the query. Freshness, for example, matters more for current news than for a dictionary definition. [2]

A practical implication: there is no defensible universal ranking-factor priority list that applies identically to every search.

### 5.2 Complementary Evidence

Different signals can support different parts of an evaluation.

For example, query-content relevance may indicate that a page addresses the subject, while link analysis or other quality-related evidence may contribute information about the page's relationships or reliability. Google publicly documents these as distinct kinds of ranking systems and considerations. [1][2]

This is a conceptual example of complementary evidence. It does not establish a fixed formula or prove that the systems always combine in the same way.

### 5.3 Conditional or Specialized Use

A signal may matter only when a particular need or context is present.

Freshness is relevant when the query expects current information. Location is relevant for local intent. Google also documents specialized systems for needs such as crisis information and local news. [1][2]

A signal can therefore be important in one search context and weak or irrelevant in another.

### 5.4 Redundant or Correlated Evidence

Two metrics may describe overlapping properties. For example, several measurements may correlate with page speed or content popularity.

A search engine does not necessarily need to treat every correlated metric as a separate independent signal. A model may transform, combine, ignore, or use only some representations. This is a general modeling possibility—not a disclosure of any specific provider's implementation.

For SEO analysis, correlated metrics should not be counted as multiple independent ranking wins without evidence.

### 5.5 Conflicting Evidence and System Constraints

Candidates can present mixed evidence. A page may be relevant but outdated, useful but duplicated, or strong on one dimension while failing a policy requirement.

Search systems also include specialized mechanisms such as deduplication, spam detection, and removal-based demotions. These can affect result treatment beyond a simplistic additive score. [1]

Do not assume that a positive signal always cancels a negative one, or that a page can compensate for a policy violation by being strong in another area.

## 6. Signal Weights, Thresholds, and the Black Box Problem

A signal's existence does not reveal its weight.

Public documentation may establish that a provider uses a category of evidence, but usually does not disclose:

- Exact numerical weights.
- Feature transformations and interactions.
- Thresholds or cutoffs.
- Whether a signal is used for every query.
- Whether it is applied at page, site, query, or system level in every context.
- How signals interact with other ranking systems.
- How much an individual change affects a specific URL.

Google's documentation describes many signals and systems, while its Search explanation says factor weights vary with the query. Bing likewise says the relative importance of its listed parameters can vary by search and evolve over time. [1][2][3]

Treat claims such as "signal X is worth 20% of ranking" as unsupported unless a credible source provides that exact information for the relevant engine, system, and period.

## 7. Common Ranking-Signal Myths

| Claim | What the evidence supports |
|---|---|
| Every SEO metric is a ranking factor. | No. A metric can be useful for diagnosis or correlated with outcomes without being a direct ranking input. |
| Google has one fixed ranking-factor checklist. | Google describes many signals and systems, not a complete public checklist or universal weighting formula. [1] |
| E-E-A-T is a direct ranking factor or score. | Google explicitly says E-E-A-T itself is not a specific ranking factor; systems use a mix of factors that can identify related qualities. [4] |
| Every page on a strong domain automatically ranks well. | Google says site-wide signals contribute, but do not guarantee high rankings for every page. [1] |
| A perfect Core Web Vitals score guarantees top rankings. | Google explicitly rejects that guarantee. [5] |
| Freshness always improves rankings. | Freshness matters when the query deserves current information; it is not a universal advantage. [1][2] |
| High CTR proves a direct Google ranking boost. | Google's public description of aggregated interaction data does not establish Search Console CTR as a direct, isolated ranking factor. [2] |
| Every signal has the same importance across queries. | Google and Bing both describe query-dependent variation in factor importance. [2][3] |

## 8. What This Means for SEO

Ranking signals are useful for understanding search-engine evaluation. They are not a shopping list of metrics to manipulate.

- Improve the actual information value and relevance of pages rather than chasing isolated proxy metrics.
- Treat technical and UX measurements as evidence about real user and system conditions, not automatic ranking guarantees.
- Build legitimate references and links because they help people discover and assess information—not because a tool reports a high backlink count.
- Keep time-sensitive information accurate, but do not refresh dates without meaningful updates.
- Interpret performance changes in context. A ranking movement does not identify which signal or system caused it.
- Avoid treating third-party scores—such as domain authority metrics, content scores, or toxicity scores—as Google's internal measurements.
- Separate documented search-engine behavior from SEO hypotheses and test results.

This is a conceptual explanation, not an optimization checklist. Website implementation belongs in the relevant SEO domains. Domain 03 explains the search-engine-side mechanisms and evidence.

## Summary

- A ranking signal is information used by a search system to help evaluate candidate results.
- Signals, features, ranking factors, models, and ranking systems are related but distinct concepts.
- Publicly documented signal categories include relevance, quality, links, freshness, context, page experience, interaction evidence, and system-specific policy or result-treatment information.
- Google confirms that page-level signals and site-wide signals/classifiers both contribute to its understanding of pages.
- Signal importance can vary by query; signals may be complementary, conditional, correlated, or in tension.
- Public documentation does not reveal a complete signal inventory, universal weights, thresholds, or exact interaction formula.
- E-E-A-T is not a specific ranking factor, and individual SEO metrics should not be promoted into direct ranking factors without evidence.

**Bottom line:** signals are evidence, not cheat codes. Understand what a search engine actually documents, question the rest, and stop treating every SEO metric as if Google personally wired it into the algorithm.
