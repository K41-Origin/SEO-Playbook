# 03.5 — Link Analysis & PageRank

Links do more than move users between pages. They create a graph of relationships across the web. Search engines can analyse that graph to understand connections, discover pages, and estimate relative importance.

One myth needs to die: **PageRank is not a public score you can inspect, and backlinks are not a simple vote counter.**

## 1. What Is Link Analysis?

**Link analysis** studies relationships represented by links between documents or entities in a graph.

For web search, pages can be modelled as nodes and hyperlinks as directed edges:

- **Node:** a page.
- **Outlink:** an edge from the linking page to another page.
- **Inlink / backlink:** an edge pointing to the destination page.
- **Anchor text:** the clickable text associated with a link.

The web's hyperlink structure provides information beyond document text. The original PageRank research used this structure to estimate the relative importance of web pages. [1]

Link analysis can provide evidence about relationships and relative prominence. It does not, by itself, establish that a page is relevant, accurate, useful, or trustworthy for every query.

## 2. Why Links Matter to Search Systems

Links can serve several functions:

1. **Navigation:** users move from one resource to another.
2. **Discovery:** crawlers can find URLs by following links.
3. **Context:** anchor text and surrounding content can describe a destination.
4. **Relationship evidence:** links connect pages, sites, topics, and sources.
5. **Importance estimation:** graph-based methods estimate relative prominence from link structure.

Google publicly confirms that it has systems that understand how pages link to one another to help determine what pages are about and which may be most helpful for a query. It identifies PageRank as one of those systems and says it remains part of Google's core ranking systems, although it has evolved substantially since launch. [2]

Bing's published explanation also identifies the reputation of a website—including the types of websites linking to it—as part of its quality and credibility assessment. [3]

These statements establish that link-related evidence matters to both engines. They do not disclose a complete link-scoring formula or prove that the engines use identical methods.

## 3. PageRank: The Core Idea

**PageRank** is a link-analysis method introduced in the original Google research to estimate the relative importance of web pages using hyperlink structure. [1]

Its central intuition: a page can be considered important when important pages link to it.

This is more sophisticated than counting backlinks. A link from a page with substantial PageRank can contribute more than a link from a page with little PageRank. The original paper describes the contribution from a linking page as being distributed across its outgoing links. [1]

### 3.1 The Random Surfer Model

The original PageRank paper explains the model through an idealized **random surfer**:

- The surfer starts on a page.
- They follow one of its outgoing links.
- They continue navigating through the web graph.
- At times, they jump to another page rather than following a link.

The probability of the surfer being on a page over the long run provides an intuitive interpretation of that page's PageRank. The model uses a damping factor to represent the probability of continuing through links rather than jumping elsewhere. [1]

This is a mathematical model, not a literal description of how real people browse the web or how modern Google Search handles every link.

### 3.2 Why Backlink Count Is Not Enough

Imagine two destination pages:

- Page A receives 100 links from pages with little graph importance.
- Page B receives 10 links from pages with much greater graph importance.

A raw backlink count says A wins. A PageRank-style model can produce a different result because it considers the importance of linking pages and how their link value is distributed. This follows the original model's recursive definition. [1]

This example is illustrative. It does not predict the ranking outcome for actual Google queries.

### 3.3 PageRank and Query Relevance Are Different

The original PageRank model estimates page importance from the link graph rather than directly matching a page to a particular search query. [1]

That distinction matters:

- **Graph importance:** how prominent a page is within a link network.
- **Query relevance:** how well a page addresses a specific query.
- **Search ranking:** evaluates multiple kinds of evidence rather than treating graph importance as the complete answer.

Google's public ranking documentation describes link analysis alongside other ranking systems, not as a replacement for relevance, quality, freshness, or other evaluations. [2]

## 4. PageRank Has Evolved

The original 1998 paper is essential for understanding the foundational idea. It is not a current implementation manual.

Google explicitly says PageRank has evolved considerably since its early form and remains part of its core ranking systems. [2] The public statement does not disclose the current formula, parameters, graph representation, update frequency, or precise role PageRank plays in individual queries.

Therefore:

- Do not assume the original equation is the exact current Google implementation.
- Do not assume PageRank is calculated or refreshed in the same way as in 1998.
- Do not infer current internal architecture from an old paper or patent.
- Do not treat a third-party PageRank-like metric as Google's internal PageRank.

The defensible conclusion is narrow but important: PageRank remains a documented part of Google's core ranking systems; its modern implementation is not publicly specified in full. [2]

## 5. Link Evidence Beyond PageRank

Link analysis is broader than one algorithm. A search engine can examine multiple properties of links and the graph they form.

### 5.1 Link Quantity

The number of incoming links can describe how widely a page is referenced. But raw volume is easy to manipulate and ignores the context and structure of those references.

The original PageRank paper discusses the limitations of simple citation counting on the web, where pages can be created cheaply and link counts can be artificially inflated. [1]

### 5.2 Linking-Page Importance

A link from a highly connected or important page can have different graph significance from a link from an obscure page. This recursive relationship is central to PageRank. [1]

Do not turn this into a universal rule that one backlink from a famous website always beats many other links. Real search systems use broader evaluation, and public sources do not expose a universal link-value formula.

### 5.3 Anchor Text

Anchor text provides descriptive context about the destination. Google's Search Essentials recommends using words people would use to look for content and placing them in descriptive locations, including link text. [4]

Anchor text is not a magic keyword-injection field. Repeating optimized commercial anchors unnaturally can become part of manipulative link patterns. Google's spam policies explicitly identify optimized anchor text in paid or distributed links as an example of link spam. [5]

### 5.4 Link Neighborhood and Graph Structure

A page's connections can be considered in relation to the wider graph: which pages point to it, which pages it points to, and how those pages are connected.

This is a general graph-analysis concept grounded in the web's hyperlink structure. The exact graph features used by current commercial search engines are not fully public. Do not present a particular neighborhood metric as a confirmed Google ranking factor without direct evidence.

### 5.5 Link Context and Destination

A hyperlink exists within a document and points to a destination. Its surrounding text and destination can help explain the relationship being expressed.

Google's public description confirms that its link-analysis systems help understand what pages are about and which may be helpful for a query. [2] The exact way current systems interpret every link's placement, surrounding text, or destination relationship is not publicly disclosed.

## 6. Link Analysis Is Not Backlink Counting

| Approach | What it considers | What it misses when used alone |
|---|---|---|
| Raw backlink count | Number of known incoming links. | Source importance, manipulation, context, and link relationships. |
| PageRank-style analysis | Recursive importance propagated through a directed link graph. | Query-specific relevance, factual quality, and complete user usefulness. |
| Broader link analysis | Multiple properties of links and graph relationships. | The complete ranking decision; link evidence is only one part of ranking. |

This is a conceptual comparison based on the original PageRank research and Google's current public description of link-analysis systems. [1][2]

A backlink tool's count, authority score, or proprietary metric is not Google's PageRank. Such metrics can be useful for that tool's own analysis, but their scales and calculations are provider-defined.

## 7. Link Spam and Manipulation

If links can influence search evaluation, people will try to manufacture them. That's not a plot twist; it's the predictable consequence of turning a web relationship into ranking evidence.

Google defines **link spam** as creating links to or from a site primarily to manipulate search rankings. Its examples include buying or selling ranking links, excessive link exchanges, automated link creation, paid advertorial links that pass ranking credit, low-quality directories, distributed widget links, and optimized forum-comment links. [5]

Google also clarifies that paid or sponsored links are acceptable when appropriately qualified with the `rel="sponsored"` or `rel="nofollow"` attribute value. [5]

### 7.1 Why Manipulation Is a Problem

Artificial links attempt to make a page appear more endorsed or prominent than genuine web linking behavior would indicate.

The original PageRank paper already identified artificial inflation of citation counts as a problem in web environments. [1] Google's current spam policies show that link manipulation remains an explicit policy concern. Google says it detects policy violations through automated systems and, where needed, human review; violations may result in lower rankings or exclusion. [5]

### 7.2 Detection and Treatment

Google's ranking systems guide identifies link analysis and PageRank separately from its spam detection systems. It also names SpamBrain as one of the systems used to address spam. [2]

Google does not publish a complete description of how every link-spam pattern is detected, how link value is discounted, or which treatment applies to every case. Avoid inventing a universal "penalty" outcome. The exact response depends on the case and system behavior. [5]

### 7.3 Not Every Unusual Link Is Spam

The existence of a backlink does not automatically mean manipulation. Links can arise naturally through citations, editorial references, recommendations, resources, community discussion, and ordinary navigation.

The policy question concerns the practice and purpose of creating or qualifying links—not merely whether a link exists. Google's examples and qualification guidance should be read in that context. [5]

## 8. Google and Bing: What Is Publicly Established?

| Search engine | Publicly documented link-related information | What remains undisclosed |
|---|---|---|
| **Google** | Link-analysis systems understand how pages link to each other; PageRank remains part of Google's core ranking systems and has evolved. [2] | Current PageRank formula, full link-signal inventory, weights, graph processing architecture, and per-query impact. |
| **Bing** | Microsoft's ranking explanation includes website reputation and the types of websites linking to a site within its quality and credibility discussion. [3] | A complete public link-analysis model, exact weights, and any claim that Bing uses Google's PageRank implementation. |

Google's PageRank disclosure is more specific by name. Bing's public material supports a broader statement about link-based reputation, not a claim that Bing uses the same PageRank algorithm.

## 9. What This Means for SEO

Link analysis explains why references from other pages can matter. It does not justify treating backlink acquisition as a volume game.

- Publish material that gives other sites a genuine reason to reference it.
- Evaluate links in context instead of relying on raw backlink totals.
- Treat anchor text as descriptive navigation, not an excuse to force exact-match keywords everywhere.
- Distinguish editorial references from paid placements and qualify sponsored links appropriately.
- Avoid automated link schemes, excessive exchanges, and other practices Google identifies as link spam.
- Treat third-party link metrics as estimates from those tools—not as Google's internal ranking data.

These are implications of documented link analysis and policy, not a guaranteed ranking formula. Backlink acquisition and off-site execution belong in Domain 12 — Off-Page SEO. Internal link architecture belongs in Domain 11 — Internal Linking. Those domains own implementation; this topic owns search-engine-side mechanics.

## Summary

- Link analysis studies the directed graph formed by hyperlinks between pages.
- Links can support navigation, discovery, context, relationship analysis, and importance estimation.
- PageRank estimates relative page importance through recursive link-based calculation, not raw backlink counting.
- The original random surfer model is a mathematical explanation, not a literal description of modern user behavior.
- Google confirms PageRank remains part of its core ranking systems, while emphasizing that it has evolved substantially.
- Bing documents link-derived website reputation as part of quality and credibility evaluation.
- Link evidence is not a substitute for relevance, quality, or the broader ranking process.
- Manipulative link practices can violate Google's spam policies; paid links should be qualified appropriately.
- Public documentation does not reveal current proprietary link formulas, weights, or complete implementations.

**Bottom line:** links are graph evidence, not votes you can mass-produce. PageRank made that distinction famous; modern search has moved far beyond the 1998 implementation.
