# 05.2 — Seed Keyword & Audience-Language Discovery

A seed keyword is a starting point for research, not a finished target. The useful part is discovering the language people use to describe a need—not inventing a list of phrases that sounds like the company's product catalogue.

Start with the audience, the problem, and the job they are trying to get done. Product names and industry terminology matter, but they are only one part of the picture. People do not owe your internal naming conventions any loyalty.

## 1. What Is a Seed Keyword?

A **seed keyword** is an initial word, phrase, or concept that represents a relevant subject, offering, problem, task, or audience need. It gives later keyword research a starting point.

A seed can be:

- A product or service name.
- A category or subject.
- A problem someone wants to solve.
- A task someone wants to complete.
- A feature, attribute, or constraint that matters to the audience.
- A phrase or question taken from the audience's own language.

For example, a business selling home coffee equipment might begin with seeds such as:

- espresso grinder
- coffee grinder for home
- espresso tastes sour
- grind size for espresso
- coffee equipment maintenance

These are illustrative starting concepts, not verified search queries or evidence of search demand.

A seed is deliberately broad enough to support further investigation. It does not need to be a polished target phrase, have known search volume, or already represent one clear intent. Those questions belong to later stages of Domain 05.

## 2. Start With the Audience and the Offering

Before collecting phrases, define the context in which a seed could be relevant.

### 2.1 Understand what the site can genuinely offer

Document the site's actual products, services, expertise, resources, and limitations.

Useful inputs include:

- Product and service catalogues.
- Product specifications and use cases.
- Support and troubleshooting documentation.
- Training materials, manuals, and FAQs.
- Existing content and site navigation.
- Subject-matter expertise and operational constraints.

This inventory provides subject matter and vocabulary. It does not prove that people search for every listed item. A company's catalogue is evidence of what the company offers—not a demand report.

Google Keyword Planner, for example, accepts words related to products or services and websites as inputs for generating keyword ideas. That is a tool-specific discovery feature, not evidence that every product label is how customers describe the same need. [1]

### 2.2 Identify the people and situations that matter

Describe the audience in terms that affect what they need to find or accomplish.

Consider:

- Their role or relationship to the product or service.
- Their level of familiarity with the subject.
- The task they are trying to complete.
- The problem, friction, or uncertainty they face.
- The context in which the need appears.
- Relevant language, location, and market differences.

Do not stop at demographic labels. "Small business owner" is not a useful seed by itself. What that person is trying to do—compare payment providers, issue an invoice, manage stock, or understand tax obligations—is much closer to a research starting point.

A useful way to frame the underlying need is:

> I need to [complete a task or solve a problem] so that [the intended outcome].

This is a user-research framing device, not a search-intent classification. GOV.UK's user-research guidance recommends investigating what people are trying to do, how they currently do it, and the problems they experience; it also recommends using language users recognize and grounding needs in research rather than assumptions. [2]

### 2.3 Separate audience language from company language

Organizations often use internal labels that customers may not know or use.

| Company or industry language | Possible audience language |
|---|---|
| Espresso extraction inconsistency | Why does my espresso taste different every time? |
| Burr calibration | How do I adjust my coffee grinder? |
| Residential water filtration system | Water filter for home |
| Customer relationship management software | Tool to keep track of customers |
| Preventive maintenance | How to stop my machine from breaking down |

These are illustrative contrasts, not claims about measured query frequency.

Do not automatically discard technical terminology. Experienced users may search with precise terms, while beginners may describe the same subject in plain language. Google's SEO Starter Guide explicitly notes that people with different levels of topic knowledge may use different terms for similar subjects. [3]

The goal is to capture both vocabularies and understand who uses them, in what context, and why.

## 3. Sources for Seed and Audience-Language Discovery

No single source represents the whole audience. Combine sources that reveal different parts of the problem, and record where each observation came from.

### 3.1 Direct audience research

When access is available, speak with actual or likely users.

Useful methods include:

- Semi-structured interviews.
- Contextual conversations about a recent task.
- Observation of how someone uses a product or service.
- Short follow-up questions after a support interaction.
- Feedback sessions with existing customers or users.

Ask about real situations rather than prompting people to invent keywords.

Useful questions include:

- What were you trying to do?
- What happened just before you needed help?
- What did you try first?
- What words did you use to describe the problem?
- What was confusing or difficult?
- How would you explain the problem to someone else?
- What would a successful outcome look like?

Avoid leading questions such as "Would you search for espresso grinder calibration?" That tests whether someone agrees with your wording after you supplied it. It does not establish that the phrase came from their own vocabulary.

Record the participant's wording separately from the researcher's interpretation. GOV.UK guidance supports interviewing and observing users, reviewing existing evidence, and treating non-user opinions as assumptions that need validation. [2]

### 3.2 Customer-facing and support data

Existing interactions can reveal recurring problems and vocabulary without requiring every discovery exercise to start from zero.

Potential sources include:

- Customer support tickets and chat transcripts.
- Sales calls and pre-sales questions.
- Frequently asked questions.
- Product reviews and feedback forms.
- Complaints, returns, and troubleshooting records.
- Onboarding questions and training sessions.
- Community support discussions hosted by the organization.

Look for repeated descriptions, questions, failed attempts, comparisons, and phrases users use to explain what they need.

Support data is especially useful for discovering friction and unmet needs. But it is not a representative sample of every customer: people who contact support have a reason to do so, and some user groups may be overrepresented.

GOV.UK's user-research guidance lists call-centre data, search logs, analytics, previous research, interviews, and observation as possible evidence sources. It also warns that opinions from people who are not users should be treated as assumptions until researched. [2]

### 3.3 Existing search and website interactions

If available, inspect:

- Internal site-search queries.
- Search terms recorded in on-site help or knowledge-base systems.
- Search Console query data for the website.
- Queries that lead users to relevant support or product pages.

These sources can expose wording already associated with the website or its users. Their coverage is limited by what the system records, which users it includes, and how the data is retained.

At this stage, use them to identify candidate language and research questions. Detailed interpretation of query datasets, reporting dimensions, and metric limitations belongs to [05.4 — Keyword Data Sources & Metrics](README.md#domain-structure).

### 3.4 Public audience language

Public discussions can reveal how people describe problems when they are not speaking directly to a company.

Possible sources include:

- Relevant forums and discussion communities.
- Public question-and-answer discussions.
- Product and marketplace reviews.
- Public comments on specialist resources.
- Competitor reviews and customer feedback where publicly accessible.

Read the surrounding discussion, not just the phrase. Context can reveal whether a term describes a problem, a solution, a product, a joke, or a meaning specific to that community.

Public discussions are self-selected and platform-dependent. A phrase appearing repeatedly in one forum does not prove that it is common across the wider market. Treat it as a discovery clue and record its source.

Respect platform rules, privacy expectations, and applicable law. Do not turn private customer records or personal information into a keyword dataset without an appropriate lawful basis and safeguards.

### 3.5 Product, service, and subject-matter knowledge

Talk to people who understand the offering and the problems it is designed to address:

- Product managers and product specialists.
- Sales and customer-success teams.
- Support and service staff.
- Technical specialists and practitioners.
- Trainers, educators, and subject-matter experts.

These people can identify terminology, product constraints, common misconceptions, and questions customers repeatedly ask.

Their knowledge is valuable, but it is not a substitute for audience evidence. An internal expert may know the correct technical term while customers use a different phrase. Keep the source and evidence type visible instead of treating every internal suggestion as an established audience query. [2]

### 3.6 Keyword discovery tools and external websites

Keyword tools can help turn initial concepts into candidate keyword ideas. Google's Keyword Planner, for example, supports starting with product/service-related words or a website. Its suggestions are generated within the tool's own system and are subject to its coverage and availability limits. [1]

Use this capability as a bridge from seed discovery to the next stage—not as a reason to skip audience research.

Competitor websites can also reveal product categories, terminology, and subjects that deserve investigation. However:

- A competitor's page structure does not prove customer demand.
- A competitor's wording may reflect its own positioning or internal vocabulary.
- A visible topic does not prove that the competitor receives meaningful organic traffic.
- Copying a competitor's terminology without checking audience context can reproduce its assumptions.

Detailed query expansion and variation-generation methods belong to [05.3 — Keyword Expansion & Query Variations](README.md#domain-structure).

## 4. A Practical Seed Discovery Workflow

Use the following process to create an initial seed inventory.

### Step 1 — Define the research boundary

Record the website or business, audience, offering, market, language, and research objective.

Keep the scope narrow enough to investigate. "All keywords for an entire ecommerce business" is not a useful starting boundary.

### Step 2 — Build an offering and expertise inventory

List the products, services, categories, tasks, problems, and areas of genuine expertise that the site can support.

Separate what the organization offers from what it merely mentions. A site should not claim relevance to a subject simply because a keyword tool suggests it.

### Step 3 — Collect audience evidence

Gather relevant phrases and needs from interviews, support interactions, customer feedback, internal search, public discussions, and existing research.

Preserve the original wording where possible. Record enough context to understand who said it, what situation they described, and where the evidence came from.

### Step 4 — Extract concepts, not just exact phrases

Identify the underlying product, problem, task, attribute, or outcome expressed by each observation.

For example, these statements may point toward related starting concepts:

- "My espresso tastes sour."
- "The coffee runs through too quickly."
- "I don't know how fine to grind the beans."

Possible seed concepts include sour espresso, espresso extraction, and espresso grind size.

This is an interpretation of illustrative statements, not a verified search-demand finding. Keep the original statements attached to the extracted concepts so the researcher can revisit the interpretation.

### Step 5 — Normalize carefully

Standardize obvious formatting differences, spelling variants, and duplicate records where doing so does not erase meaningful distinctions.

Do not aggressively rewrite audience language into polished marketing terminology. Preserve slang, abbreviations, local expressions, technical terms, and multilingual variants when they may represent meaningful differences.

Normalization rules and large-scale variation handling will be developed further in 05.3 and 05.4.

### Step 6 — Create and annotate the seed list

Turn the extracted concepts into an initial seed inventory. Add the source, audience context, and a short explanation of why each seed is relevant.

Do not assign final intent labels, search-volume estimates, opportunity scores, or page targets at this stage. Those are separate research decisions covered by 05.4–05.9.

### Step 7 — Review gaps and assumptions

Ask:

- Which audience groups have not been represented?
- Are we relying too heavily on internal terminology?
- Are some seeds based on a single anecdote?
- Are product categories represented while user problems are missing?
- Are language or market differences being flattened?
- Which ideas are assumptions rather than observed audience language?

Record unanswered questions instead of quietly promoting guesses into facts.

## 5. Evaluating Seed Quality and Evidence

A useful seed inventory is not simply a list of phrases. It is a traceable collection of starting concepts supported by evidence.

Assess each seed using the following dimensions:

| Dimension | What to check |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Does the concept relate to the site's actual audience, offering, expertise, or purpose? |
| Source | Where did the phrase or concept come from? |
| Directness | Is it the user's own wording, an observation, an internal suggestion, or a tool-generated idea? |
| Context | What task, problem, audience, or situation does it represent? |
| Recurrence | Has it appeared across multiple independent observations, or only once? |
| Coverage | Which audience groups, markets, or language communities are represented or missing? |
| Uncertainty | What is inferred, unverified, ambiguous, or potentially biased? |

These dimensions are a practical editorial framework for this playbook, not a standardized scoring model or a claim that a particular number of mentions proves demand.

### 5.1 Keep evidence and interpretation separate

A useful record distinguishes:

- **Observed wording:** what the person or source actually said.
- **Extracted concept:** the topic or need the researcher believes the wording represents.
- **Research interpretation:** why the concept may matter to the site.
- **Evidence status:** direct observation, repeated observation, internal hypothesis, or tool suggestion.
- **Open question:** what still needs validation.

This prevents a common research failure: an analyst paraphrases a customer's words, forgets that it was a paraphrase, and later cites the phrase as if customers actually used it.

### 5.2 Do not confuse recurrence with demand

A phrase appearing in multiple support tickets may signal a recurring customer problem. It does not automatically establish how frequently people search for that phrase on Google.

Likewise, one interview can reveal a useful concept without proving that it is widespread.

Seed discovery identifies what deserves further investigation. Search demand, metric interpretation, and opportunity evaluation require separate evidence and methods in later topics.

### 5.3 Preserve audience diversity

Avoid treating one customer segment, one channel, or one language community as the entire audience.

Experienced users may use technical terminology; beginners may describe symptoms. Customers may use regional expressions, abbreviations, or borrowed words. People with different accessibility needs or levels of digital confidence may describe the same task differently.

Where these distinctions matter, preserve them in the seed inventory rather than forcing every phrase into one generic label.

## 6. Example: Discovering Seeds for a Home Coffee Equipment Business

Assume a business sells espresso machines, grinders, and accessories for home use.

The following example demonstrates how raw observations can become initial seed concepts. All phrases and observations are fictional and illustrative; they are not sourced from actual customer interviews, search logs, or keyword-volume data.

| Illustrative observation | Extracted seed concept | Evidence note |
|---|---|---|
| "Why does my espresso taste sour?" | sour espresso | Problem language; hypothetical customer wording |
| "Which grinder should I get for home espresso?" | home espresso grinder | Product-selection language; hypothetical customer wording |
| "The coffee comes out too fast." | espresso extraction speed | Symptom language; underlying cause not yet established |
| "How often do I need to clean the machine?" | espresso machine cleaning | Maintenance task; hypothetical customer wording |
| Product catalogue lists replacement burrs | coffee grinder burrs | Internal product terminology; audience usage not yet verified |
| Support team reports recurring questions about descaling | espresso machine descaling | Internal support observation; source dataset should be checked |

Notice the difference between customer wording and internal terminology. Both can become useful seeds, but they do not carry the same evidence status.

The inventory is ready for the next research stage when each seed has a clear origin, context, and reason for inclusion. It is not yet a validated keyword list, a search-intent classification, or a content plan.

## 7. Common Seed Discovery Mistakes

### 7.1 Starting and stopping at product names

Product names describe the offering. They may miss the problems, tasks, comparisons, and questions that lead people to it.

Use the catalogue as one input, not the entire research strategy.

### 7.2 Treating internal jargon as audience language

A technically correct phrase can still be unfamiliar to the audience. Capture specialist terminology, but compare it with the words people actually use.

### 7.3 Asking people to invent keywords

Most people are not keyword researchers. Ask them to describe a real task or recent problem in their own words. Extract candidate concepts afterward.

### 7.4 Treating every mention as proof of demand

A phrase in a support ticket, review, or forum post is evidence that the phrase appeared in that source. It is not a search-volume measurement or a market-wide prevalence estimate.

### 7.5 Copying competitor vocabulary without context

Competitor language can suggest areas to investigate. It cannot establish that the terms are relevant to your audience or appropriate for your offering.

### 7.6 Expanding before the seed set is understood

Generating thousands of variations from a weak or irrelevant seed set only scales the original mistake. Establish relevance and context first; expansion comes next.

## 8. Key Takeaways

- Seed keywords are initial research anchors, not final SEO targets.
- Strong seed discovery starts with audience needs, real tasks, and the site's genuine offering.
- Product catalogues and internal expertise provide useful inputs, but they do not prove audience demand.
- Interviews, support data, customer feedback, internal search, public discussions, and keyword tools reveal different slices of audience language.
- Preserve original wording and record the source before interpreting it.
- Distinguish direct audience evidence, internal assumptions, and tool-generated suggestions.
- A seed inventory is ready for expansion when its relevance, context, origin, and uncertainty are documented.
- Search volume, intent classification, clustering, prioritization, and page mapping are later stages—not shortcuts to seed discovery.
