# 05.9 — Keyword-to-Page Mapping & Research Deliverables

Keyword research is not finished when the spreadsheet has clusters and priority labels. Someone still has to decide **which page should satisfy which search need**.

That decision is keyword-to-page mapping.

The goal is to create a clear, evidence-backed relationship between validated query clusters and suitable existing or proposed pages—without turning every cluster into a new URL, forcing unrelated queries onto one page, or confusing a research recommendation with an implementation instruction.

A keyword map is a decision record. It is not a ranking guarantee, a content brief, or permission to mass-produce near-identical landing pages.

## 1. What Is Keyword-to-Page Mapping?

**Keyword-to-page mapping** is the process of assigning a validated keyword cluster or search need to the most appropriate existing or proposed page on a specific website.

The mapping decision considers:

- The audience need and validated search intent.
- The cluster's priority and business relevance.
- The purpose and scope of the candidate page.
- The page's current content, usefulness, and performance evidence.
- The relationship between candidate pages and other mapped clusters.
- Whether an existing page should be retained, improved, repositioned, consolidated, or replaced by a genuinely distinct page.
- The evidence, assumptions, dependencies, and confidence behind the recommendation.

The output is a structured relationship between **query cluster → page purpose → target page candidate → decision**.

The mapping belongs to Domain 05 because it translates keyword research into a research deliverable. It does not own content planning, page writing, on-page implementation, technical URL changes, or ongoing performance reporting.

## 3. The Core Mapping Principle: Match the Need, Not Just the Keyword

A page should be mapped to a cluster when its purpose and content can satisfy the shared need represented by that cluster.

Do not assign a query to a page merely because:

- The query contains a word already present in the page title.
- The page is the closest URL available in a spreadsheet.
- The page has the most backlinks or highest current traffic.
- The site wants that page to rank for the query.
- A tool groups the query with another keyword.
- The team wants to avoid creating a new page at any cost.

Those details may be relevant evidence, but none proves that the page is the right destination.

A useful mapping test:

1. What task is the searcher trying to complete?
2. Does the candidate page's primary purpose match that task?
3. Does the page provide the information, product, service, tool, or action needed?
4. Does its scope cover the cluster without becoming unfocused?
5. Would a visitor arriving from the query reasonably feel they reached the right destination?
6. Is there another page on the site that is a materially better fit?

Google's SEO Starter Guide recommends organizing a site logically so users and search engines can understand how pages relate, while emphasizing useful, original, well-organized content. This supports evaluating page purpose and usefulness—not mechanically assigning keywords to URLs. [1]

## 4. The Mapping Unit and Relationship Model

The primary unit is usually a **prioritized keyword cluster**, but the relationship is not always one cluster to one URL.

### 4.1 One cluster → one primary page

This is the common starting pattern when the queries express a shared task and one page can satisfy that need comprehensively.

Example:

- Cluster: home espresso grinder cleaning.
- Page purpose: instructions for cleaning and maintaining a burr grinder.
- Candidate: an existing maintenance guide, if it genuinely covers the task.

The mapping records one primary target page for the cluster. This does not mean every query must appear verbatim on the page.

### 4.2 Multiple clusters → one page

Several clusters may belong to one page when they express closely related needs that can be served within a coherent page purpose.

Example:

- How to clean a burr grinder.
- How often to clean a burr grinder.
- How to remove coffee residue from a burr grinder.

These may fit one useful maintenance guide if the SERP evidence, audience task, and page scope support that grouping.

Do not merge clusters merely to reduce the number of URLs. The combined page must still answer the distinct questions without becoming an incoherent catch-all.

### 4.3 One broad topic → multiple pages

A broad topic may require multiple pages when its sub-needs differ materially in intent, task, audience, or expected destination.

Example:

- Choosing a home espresso grinder.
- Repairing a commercial grinder.
- Cleaning a specific grinder model.

The shared vocabulary does not make these the same search task. Separate pages may be appropriate if the site can provide distinct, useful destinations.

### 4.4 One query → multiple plausible destinations

A query can have mixed or ambiguous intent. Several page types may plausibly serve it, especially across different markets or contexts.

Do not force a definitive mapping when evidence is inconclusive. Record the candidate pages, the ambiguity, and the evidence needed to resolve it. Use a provisional mapping or send the issue back for intent/SERP review.

### 4.5 No suitable page

A cluster may have no appropriate existing destination.

Possible decisions:

- Propose a new page when the need is distinct, valuable, feasible, and can be served meaningfully.
- Defer the cluster when it is not currently prioritized.
- Reject it when the site cannot or should not serve the need.
- Reassess the cluster if new evidence changes its intent, priority, or business fit.

A missing URL is not automatically a content gap. Sometimes the right decision is not to publish.

## 5. Build and Review the Page Inventory

Before assigning clusters, create a reliable inventory of candidate pages. A keyword map built on stale URLs or incomplete page records is a spreadsheet-shaped guess.

### 5.1 Inventory sources

Depending on the site, use:

- The CMS or content-management export.
- XML sitemaps as one discovery source, not a complete inventory by themselves.
- A crawl of the accessible website.
- Search Console page data.
- Analytics or business landing-page records.
- Product, service, category, and support-system inventories.
- Existing editorial plans and approved page proposals.

Google's site-move guidance also describes using sitemaps, CMS exports, Search Console, analytics, and server logs to identify important URLs in the specific context of URL migrations. Those are useful inventory inputs, but a routine keyword map is not itself a site migration. [2]

### 5.2 Recommended page inventory fields

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Page ID | Stable internal identifier |
| Current URL | Exact live or known URL |
| Page title / working title | Current title or proposed label |
| Page type | Product, category, service, guide, comparison, support article, location page, tool, or other |
| Primary purpose | The main task the page is designed to satisfy |
| Audience | Intended user or segment |
| Market and language | Geographic and language scope |
| Content summary | What the page actually covers |
| Status | Published, draft, planned, redirected, unavailable, or needs verification |
| Indexability note | Known technical state, if checked; otherwise unknown |
| Existing query visibility | Relevant Search Console query/page observations, if available |
| Business role | Product, lead generation, support, education, navigation, or other |
| Owner | Responsible team or person |
| Evidence date | Date the inventory record was checked |

Do not infer indexability from a URL appearing in a sitemap. A sitemap is a discovery and canonicalization hint, not proof that a URL is indexed or eligible to appear in search. [3]

### 5.3 Verify URL and page state

Distinguish what is known from what is merely assumed:

- **Live and reviewed:** URL was accessed and the page purpose/content was inspected.
- **Live, not reviewed:** URL responds, but page fit has not been evaluated.
- **Reported in Search Console:** performance evidence exists for the property and reporting period.
- **Indexed status checked:** URL Inspection or another appropriate source was used and its observation date recorded.
- **Planned:** page does not yet exist; the proposed purpose and owner are documented.
- **Unknown:** current status has not been verified.

A URL may be live but no longer useful. It may be useful but not indexed. It may be indexed but not appropriate for the target query. Keep those states separate.

## 6. Evaluate Candidate Page Fit

For each prioritized cluster, compare the most plausible candidate pages.

### 6.1 Search-task alignment

Does the page solve the same problem or support the same decision represented by the cluster?

A product category page may fit a broad shopping task. A detailed troubleshooting guide may fit a repair question. A generic homepage usually does not become the right destination just because it mentions the product category.

### 6.2 Page purpose and scope

Check whether the page's primary purpose is clear and whether the cluster fits naturally within it.

Warning signs:

- The page would need a major change of purpose to fit the cluster.
- The cluster would occupy a small, unrelated corner of the page.
- The page would need to cover several unrelated audiences or tasks.
- The proposed target is selected only because it already exists.

### 6.3 Content coverage and usefulness

Review whether the page provides enough accurate, relevant information or functionality to satisfy the need.

Record:

- What the page already answers.
- What is missing or outdated.
- Whether the missing material is a reasonable improvement to the current page.
- Whether satisfying the cluster would require a materially different page.
- Whether the site has the expertise, evidence, inventory, or capability to deliver it.

This is a mapping assessment, not a content brief. Detailed outlines, editorial requirements, and production plans belong to Domain 08.

### 6.4 SERP and destination-page fit

Use the observations already documented in 05.6. Check whether the proposed page type is consistent with the observed result patterns and likely task.

SERP evidence is contextual and time-bound. A page-type pattern may support a mapping hypothesis, but it does not prove that Google requires one exact format or that the candidate page will rank.

### 6.5 Existing visibility and page history

Where data is available, examine the candidate URL's query and page performance over a suitable period.

Search Console lets site owners examine query and page dimensions, including clicks, impressions, CTR, and average position. It also distinguishes property-level and page-level aggregation; the same query can therefore produce different totals depending on the selected grouping. Record the report configuration and do not interpret a table as a complete inventory of all search demand. [4][5]

Useful checks include:

- Which queries already produce impressions or clicks for the URL?
- Are those queries aligned with the page's purpose?
- Do multiple URLs receive visibility for the same cluster?
- Is the observed pattern stable across the selected period?
- Are market, device, search type, and date filters appropriate?
- Are query or URL rows missing because of report limitations?

Existing visibility is evidence, not an automatic reason to preserve the current mapping. A page may rank for irrelevant queries, and a relevant page may have no reported visibility yet.

### 6.6 Business and operational fit

Confirm that the page can fulfil the audience need and the organization's role.

Examples:

- A retailer should not map a repair-service query to a product page if it does not offer repair and the page cannot help the visitor complete that task.
- A local service provider should not map a location-specific need to a page for a market it does not serve.
- A support article should not be forced into a purchase-intent role if the page has no useful commercial destination.

## 7. Choose the Mapping Decision

Every cluster should receive an explicit mapping outcome. Avoid ambiguous labels such as "optimize" without saying what is changing.

| Decision | When to use it | Typical next step |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep existing target** | The current page is the best fit and remains useful. | Preserve the mapping; pass any limited improvement notes downstream. |
| **Improve existing page** | The page is the right destination, but coverage, accuracy, clarity, or usefulness needs work. | Send the research gap and evidence to Domain 08/07. |
| **Reposition existing page** | The page has drifted from its intended purpose or the mapped cluster better reflects the need it should serve. | Review purpose and scope before implementation; avoid changing it based only on a keyword label. |
| **Consolidate candidates** | Multiple pages substantially overlap in purpose and can be replaced by one genuinely stronger destination. | Require content and URL review; document the retained destination and affected URLs. |
| **Create a new page** | A distinct, valuable need has no suitable destination and the site can serve it. | Submit a new-page proposal for editorial and business approval. |
| **Separate mappings** | A broad or mixed group contains materially different tasks or destinations. | Return for cluster review or map the distinct needs separately with evidence. |
| **Provisional / unresolved** | Evidence is insufficient or candidate pages are similarly plausible. | Record uncertainty and the exact validation needed. |
| **Do not map / defer** | The cluster is not prioritized, does not fit the site, or cannot be served responsibly. | Record the reason; do not create a URL merely to fill a cell. |

### 7.1 Existing page vs. new page

Prefer an existing page when it already serves the same need and can be improved without undermining its purpose.

Consider a new page when:

- The audience task is materially different.
- The proposed page has a distinct purpose and useful scope.
- An existing page cannot satisfy the need without becoming incoherent.
- The opportunity is prioritized and feasible under 05.8.
- The site can provide original, useful value.
- The new page will fit a logical site structure.

A new page is not justified by a keyword variation alone.

### 7.2 Consolidation is not automatic deletion

When several URLs appear to overlap, first determine whether they are genuinely redundant or serve distinct audiences, tasks, products, locations, or stages.

If content is consolidated, identify the surviving destination and the URLs affected. Redirect decisions, canonical annotations, status codes, and technical validation belong to the appropriate technical implementation workflow—not the keyword map itself.

Google documents canonicalization as selecting a representative URL among duplicate or very similar pages. Canonicalization is a search-engine indexing process; it is not a tool for telling Google that two pages target the same keyword, and it does not replace a human content-purpose decision. [3]

## 8. Keyword Cannibalization and Mapping Conflicts

"Keyword cannibalization" is a practitioner label often used for situations where multiple URLs appear to compete for the same query or overlapping search need. It is not, by itself, proof of a Google penalty, a confirmed algorithmic problem, or a diagnosis that one URL must be deleted.

Use the term carefully. Diagnose the actual conflict before prescribing a fix.

### 8.1 Types of mapping conflict

**Duplicate assignment**

The same cluster is assigned to multiple pages without a clear primary target or distinct purpose.

**Purpose overlap**

Two pages appear designed to satisfy substantially the same audience task, with no clear reason for both to exist.

**Accidental query overlap**

Several pages receive impressions for some of the same queries, but they may serve distinct purposes or audiences. Overlap alone does not prove a problem.

**Intent mismatch**

A cluster is assigned to a page whose purpose does not match the likely search task.

**Orphaned cluster**

A prioritized cluster has no suitable target page or proposed destination.

**Overloaded page**

One page is assigned too many unrelated clusters and would become a catch-all rather than a coherent destination.

**Unclear ownership**

Different teams or page owners are responsible for overlapping destinations, and no one owns the mapping decision.

### 8.2 How to investigate overlapping URLs

1. Select the query or cluster and define the relevant market, device, search type, and time range.
2. Inspect the Search Console query and page dimensions using consistent filters.
3. Identify which URLs receive impressions or clicks for the same queries.
4. Compare page purpose, content, audience, and destination type.
5. Review whether the URLs serve genuinely different needs.
6. Inspect the relevant SERPs and landing pages where the distinction remains unclear.
7. Record whether the overlap is meaningful, expected, or unresolved.
8. Recommend an action only after the page-purpose evidence is reviewed.

Search Console page-level data can help reveal which URLs receive impressions for a query, but report aggregation, anonymized queries, and available data limits mean it is not a complete record of every query-page relationship. [4][5]

### 8.3 Possible responses

Depending on the diagnosis:

- Keep both pages if they serve distinct needs.
- Clarify page purpose and scope.
- Reassign the cluster to the more suitable page.
- Improve one page and leave the other focused on its separate task.
- Consolidate genuinely overlapping content when one stronger destination can serve the need.
- Review technical canonicalization or redirects if the URLs are actual duplicates or have been intentionally replaced.
- Leave the issue unresolved if evidence does not support a safe decision.

Do not merge pages just because they share a keyword. Do not add canonical tags as a generic "cannibalization fix." Google's canonical documentation applies to duplicate or very similar URLs and treats canonical hints as signals that Google may interpret differently—not as a keyword-targeting control. [3]

### 8.4 Do not confuse query overlap with duplicate URLs

These are different problems:

- **Query overlap:** multiple pages receive visibility for some of the same queries.
- **Content duplication:** multiple URLs expose duplicate or very similar primary content.
- **Mapping conflict:** the research plan assigns overlapping or unclear page responsibilities.
- **Canonicalization:** a search engine selects a representative URL from duplicate or very similar pages.

They may occur together, but none automatically proves the others.

## 9. A Repeatable Keyword-to-Page Mapping Workflow

### Step 1 — Import prioritized clusters

Take the approved clusters and decisions from 05.8. Preserve cluster IDs, representative queries, intent evidence, priority, rationale, confidence, and constraints.

Do not map every discovered query by default. Work from the prioritized research set and explicitly record deferred or rejected clusters.

### Step 2 — Define mapping context

Record the target website, market, language, audience, business objective, and review date. A mapping is not universal across every domain, locale, or site.

### Step 3 — Build the page inventory

Collect existing URLs and proposed destinations. Verify the page status, purpose, type, owner, and relevant evidence.

### Step 4 — Generate candidate matches

Compare each cluster with plausible pages based on task, intent, purpose, scope, audience, business fit, and page type.

Use tools to surface candidates, not to make the final decision without review.

### Step 5 — Evaluate candidate fit

Review content coverage, usefulness, SERP context, existing visibility, and competing URLs. Record evidence and unresolved questions.

### Step 6 — Assign one mapping decision

Choose the best-supported outcome: keep, improve, reposition, consolidate, create, separate, provisional, or defer/no mapping.

Where one cluster has several plausible candidates, identify the primary recommendation and explain why alternatives were not selected—or mark the mapping unresolved.

### Step 7 — Check conflicts across the map

Review the entire mapping table for:

- One cluster assigned to multiple primary URLs.
- Multiple clusters assigned to one page despite unrelated tasks.
- Several pages with overlapping purpose.
- High-priority clusters without a target.
- Proposed pages that duplicate an existing destination.
- Market or language mismatches.
- Unowned pages or missing implementation dependencies.

### Step 8 — Validate the page architecture

Check whether the proposed page set makes sense as a navigable site structure. Ensure that new pages have a clear role and that consolidation does not erase useful distinctions.

This is a research-level architecture review. Technical URL structures, crawl paths, internal-link implementation, and indexation controls belong to their respective domains.

### Step 9 — Prepare the handoff

Package the mapping table, page inventory, evidence, decisions, conflicts, and outstanding questions. Make the next action clear for each row.

### Step 10 — Preserve version and review history

Record who approved the mapping, when it was reviewed, what evidence was used, and what would trigger reassessment.

Revisit the map when business priorities, page purpose, products/services, audience needs, site architecture, or search evidence materially changes.

## 10. Worked Example: Home Espresso Grinder Clusters

The following example is fictional. It does not represent live keyword data, actual Search Console performance, observed SERPs, or a real website.

Assume a specialist coffee retailer has these prioritized clusters:

| Cluster ID | Cluster | Intent/task summary | 05.8 priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| GR-01 | Best espresso grinder for home | Compare and select a home grinder | Prioritize |
| GR-02 | Home burr grinder comparison | Compare grinder models and features | Prioritize |
| GR-03 | How to clean a burr grinder | Maintain an existing grinder | Validate |
| GR-04 | Commercial grinder repair | Find professional repair | Reject: service not offered |

The site inventory includes:

| Page ID | Existing page | Purpose | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| PG-01 | Home espresso grinders category | Browse and compare available home grinders | Published |
| PG-02 | Grinder buying guide | Explain selection criteria and buying considerations | Published |
| PG-03 | Grinder cleaning guide | Explain routine cleaning and maintenance | Published |
| PG-04 | Commercial repair service | No such service page or offering | Not available |

### Candidate mapping

| Cluster | Primary target | Decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| GR-01 | PG-02 | Improve existing page | The buying guide is the best fit for comparison and selection; verify that its advice and product references remain useful. |
| GR-02 | PG-02 | Keep existing target | The comparison task fits the buying guide's purpose; do not create a second near-identical comparison page without distinct value. |
| GR-03 | PG-03 | Provisional mapping | The page purpose matches, but audience value and the retailer's ability to provide accurate model-specific guidance need validation. |
| GR-04 | None | Do not map | The business does not offer commercial repair and has no useful destination for this task. |

### What the example demonstrates

- Two related comparison clusters can map to one coherent buying guide.
- An existing page can be the right target even if it needs improvement.
- A maintenance cluster can remain provisional while evidence is incomplete.
- A rejected opportunity does not need a page.
- The category page and buying guide may have different purposes: browsing available products versus learning how to choose. Both can exist if the distinction is useful and clear.

The mapping is a recommendation for downstream review. It does not authorize automatic URL changes, redirects, canonical tags, or content publication.

## 11. Research Deliverables

A useful handoff should let another team understand what was decided, why it was decided, and what work remains—without repeating the entire research process.

### 11.1 Keyword-to-page mapping table

Recommended fields:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Mapping ID | Stable identifier for the mapping record |
| Cluster ID | Links to the 05.7 cluster |
| Representative query | Short label for the search need |
| Included queries | Query set or reference to the cluster dataset |
| Intent/task | Validated intent and underlying user task |
| Market and language | Defines the mapping context |
| 05.8 priority | Carries forward the opportunity decision |
| Primary target page ID | Identifies the recommended destination |
| Current URL | Existing URL, if applicable |
| Proposed page title | Working title for a proposed destination |
| Page type | Guide, category, product, service, tool, support, or other |
| Mapping decision | Keep, improve, reposition, consolidate, create, separate, provisional, or defer |
| Candidate alternatives | Other plausible pages considered |
| Fit rationale | Explains why the selected page matches the task |
| Coverage gap | What the existing page does not yet satisfy |
| Conflict note | Overlap, duplicate assignment, or unresolved ownership |
| Evidence references | Links to SERP notes, data, page inventory, and source records |
| Confidence | Confidence in the mapping decision, not a ranking probability |
| Owner | Person or team responsible for the next decision |
| Next action | Specific downstream action or validation request |
| Review date / trigger | When or why the mapping should be reassessed |

### 11.2 Page inventory

A separate page inventory should contain the current and proposed destinations, their status, purpose, page type, audience, market/language, owner, and verification notes.

Keep page inventory records reusable across clusters. Do not copy the same URL metadata into dozens of mapping rows and then let the values drift apart.

### 11.3 Mapping conflict register

Track issues that cannot be represented cleanly in a single mapping row:

- Conflicting cluster assignments.
- Overlapping page purpose.
- Suspected query overlap requiring investigation.
- Duplicate or near-duplicate URL candidates.
- Missing page owner.
- Unverified URL or indexation status.
- Market/language mismatch.
- Consolidation or redirect dependency.
- Decision blocked by missing business or editorial approval.

For each issue, record affected cluster/page IDs, evidence, severity or impact, owner, status, and resolution.

### 11.4 Research handoff summary

The summary should include:

- Research objective and scope.
- Site, audience, market, and language.
- Number of clusters assessed and mapped.
- High-priority mapping decisions.
- Existing pages to retain or improve.
- New-page proposals and their rationale.
- Consolidation or separation recommendations.
- Deferred and rejected opportunities.
- Open conflicts and evidence gaps.
- Dependencies, owners, and approvals required.
- Date and version of the research dataset.

### 11.5 Minimum handoff quality

A mapping is not ready for handoff if:

- The cluster cannot be traced to its source query set.
- The target URL or proposed page has no clear purpose.
- The mapping rationale is missing.
- Priority and intent evidence are absent.
- Competing URLs were ignored despite a visible conflict.
- Unknown technical status is presented as verified.
- The next team cannot tell what action is expected.

## 12. Common Mapping Mistakes

### 12.1 One Keyword, One Page

Creating one URL per query or minor wording variation creates needless fragmentation. Map by meaningful search need and page purpose, not by spreadsheet row count.

### 12.2 One Page for Everything

The opposite mistake is stuffing unrelated clusters onto one page to avoid expanding the site. A page must retain a coherent purpose and satisfy the tasks it targets.

### 12.3 Mapping by Title Similarity

A query sharing words with a page title does not establish task fit. Review the actual page and the likely need.

### 12.4 Treating Search Volume as Page Priority

Volume is one input from 05.4 and 05.8, not a page assignment rule. A high-volume cluster can be irrelevant to the page or the business.

### 12.5 Treating Search Console Overlap as Proof of Cannibalization

Multiple URLs receiving impressions for the same query is a reason to investigate, not a diagnosis. Compare page purpose, content, audience, and actual outcomes before changing anything.

### 12.6 Using Canonical Tags as Keyword Controls

Canonicalization concerns duplicate or very similar URLs. It does not assign a target keyword, merge distinct search intents, or resolve editorial ownership. [3]

### 12.7 Redirecting Everything to the Homepage

If pages are intentionally merged or moved, the destination should be relevant to the old content. Google warns against redirecting many unrelated old URLs to one irrelevant destination such as the homepage; that can confuse users and may be treated as a soft 404. [2]

### 12.8 Publishing Near-Duplicate Landing Pages

Creating substantially similar pages to rank for similar queries and funnel users elsewhere can fall within Google's doorway-abuse policy. A location, product, or modifier variation must represent a genuinely useful distinction—not just another URL in the ranking lottery. [6]

### 12.9 Skipping the Handoff

A map that says "optimize this page" without a target need, evidence, gap, or next action is not a deliverable. It is a task with missing context.

## 13. What 05.9 Does Not Own

- **It does not discover or expand queries.** Seed discovery and query expansion belong to 05.2–05.3.
- **It does not redefine metrics.** Keyword data sources and metric definitions belong to 05.4.
- **It does not classify or validate intent from scratch.** Intent and SERP evidence belong to 05.5–05.6.
- **It does not rebuild clusters by default.** Query grouping belongs to 05.7.
- **It does not decide opportunity priority.** Opportunity assessment belongs to 05.8.
- **It does not write editorial briefs or produce content.** Content strategy and production belong to Domain 08.
- **It does not implement titles, headings, copy, or other page-level optimizations.** Those belong to Domain 07.
- **It does not implement redirects, canonical tags, indexation directives, or technical URL changes.** Those belong to the relevant technical SEO and search-engine fundamentals workflows.
- **It does not own internal-link implementation.** Domain 11 owns internal-link strategy and execution.
- **It does not own ongoing performance reporting.** Domain 14 owns SEO analytics and reporting.

## Key Takeaways

- Map a validated cluster to the page that best satisfies its underlying search task.
- The relationship is not automatically one cluster to one URL.
- Multiple clusters can share a page when their needs and page purpose genuinely align.
- Distinct tasks may need separate pages; similar wording alone does not justify either merging or splitting.
- Build and verify a page inventory before mapping.
- Treat keyword cannibalization as a diagnostic label, not proof of a penalty.
- Query overlap, duplicate content, mapping conflict, and canonicalization are different concepts.
- New pages, consolidation, and redirects require evidence and downstream review.
- Preserve mapping rationale, source evidence, confidence, owners, and next actions.
- The final deliverable is a traceable research handoff—not a content production plan or implementation ticket.
