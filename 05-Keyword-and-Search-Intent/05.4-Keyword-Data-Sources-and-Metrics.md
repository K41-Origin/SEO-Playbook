# 05.4 — Keyword Data Sources & Metrics

Keyword metrics are evidence with a scope, a method, and blind spots. They are not the market itself.

This topic explains where SEO practitioners obtain keyword data, what common metrics actually measure, how those metrics are produced or constrained, and how to compare them without manufacturing certainty. The goal is not to crown one tool as the source of truth. It is to understand what each source can legitimately tell you—and what it cannot.

This topic begins after candidate discovery and expansion in 05.1–05.3. It covers data-source selection and metric interpretation. Search-intent classification, SERP validation, clustering, prioritization, and keyword-to-page decisions remain separate stages.

## 1. What Is Keyword Data?

**Keyword data** is information associated with a query, keyword, topic, or query set that helps a researcher understand search activity, website visibility, advertising context, or estimated opportunity.

The phrase "keyword data" is broad. A value labelled *search volume* in one platform may not be directly comparable with a value carrying the same label in another. Sources may differ in:

- The search engine or network represented.
- Geography, language, and device coverage.
- Date range and averaging period.
- Query matching, close-variant grouping, and normalization.
- Sampling, aggregation, privacy filtering, and rounding.
- Whether a value is observed, modeled, sampled, or forecast.
- The business purpose for which the metric was designed.

A number without its source and settings is not a complete research record. It is an orphaned number asking to be trusted.

## 2. The Main Keyword Data Sources

No source answers every keyword-research question. Select the source based on the claim you need to investigate.

### 2.1 Google Ads Keyword Planner

Google Keyword Planner is an advertising research and planning tool. It provides keyword ideas, historical search metrics, and campaign forecasts. Google documents its historical metrics and forecasts in the context of planning Google Ads campaigns—not as a complete organic SEO measurement system. [1]

Common outputs include average monthly searches, historical monthly search-volume values, advertiser competition, a competition index in the Google Ads API, top-of-page bid ranges, and forecast clicks, impressions, cost, and CTR for a proposed ad plan.

Its data is useful for understanding estimated Google Search activity under selected settings and for comparing candidate terms within a consistent research setup. But do not silently relabel advertising metrics as organic SEO metrics.

Important limitations:

- Average monthly searches are based on the selected date range, location, and Search Network settings. The default period is generally a 12-month average.
- The metric includes the keyword and its close variants; it is not necessarily a count of exact literal-string submissions.
- Google says search-volume statistics are rounded.
- Historical metrics and forecasts are different outputs. Forecasts incorporate campaign inputs such as bids, budget, seasonality, and historical ad quality.
- The Competition field describes advertiser activity relative to other keywords in the selected location and network. It is not an organic ranking difficulty score. [1]

The Google Ads API also documents that close variants can be deduplicated in historical-metric responses. For example, related singular and plural forms may be returned as one result with the close variants recorded. That is another reason not to assume every row represents an independent query count. [2]

### 2.2 Google Search Console

Google Search Console provides performance data for a verified website property. Its query dimension can show searches for which that property received visibility, alongside clicks, impressions, CTR, and position. This makes it valuable for analyzing existing organic search performance and discovering queries already associated with the site. [3]

It does **not** provide a complete market-demand database. A property report describes that property's reported performance, not every search made by everyone in the market.

Key limitations:

- Queries that are anonymized for privacy are omitted from query tables and exports, although they may remain included in chart totals unless a query filter is applied.
- Search Console applies data limits to reports and exports. The Search Analytics API and interface have different export constraints.
- Reported rows are grouped by dimensions and property context; the data is not a census of all queries in the market.
- A query with no visible row is not proof that nobody searches for it. It may have no recorded property performance, be omitted for privacy, or fall outside the available report data. [4]

Use Search Console to answer questions such as which queries already generate impressions or clicks for this property, which pages receive visibility for a query, how reported visibility changes across countries/devices/time, and whether there are relevant query themes the current site already touches.

Do not use Search Console query totals as a substitute for total search demand. Domain 14 owns ongoing site-performance analysis; Domain 05 uses the query evidence for keyword research.

### 2.3 Google Trends

Google Trends provides a sampled and normalized view of relative search interest. Google explains that its data is anonymized, categorized, aggregated, and normalized by time and geography. Values are scaled from 0 to 100 relative to the selected comparison context. [5]

Trends is useful for comparing relative interest between terms, exploring seasonality and recurring patterns, investigating regional differences, detecting changes in interest over a selected period, and discovering related and rising searches.

It is not an absolute search-volume report. A value of 100 is the peak relative interest in the selected context, not 100 searches or a universal popularity score. The same term can show a similar normalized value in two regions even when the underlying number of searches differs. [5]

Google also notes that low-volume terms may appear as zero, repeated searches may be filtered, and statistical noise can create unusual spikes—especially for terms with very little activity. A one-off spike is not proof of a real demand event. [5]

Always preserve the comparison setup: selected terms or topics, geography, time range, category, search type, and whether the input was a search term or a Trends topic.

### 2.4 Third-party SEO platforms

Third-party SEO platforms may provide keyword-volume estimates, keyword difficulty scores, CPC estimates, traffic estimates, click metrics, SERP history, and competitive datasets.

These are vendor-produced metrics. Their definitions, data coverage, models, update frequency, and confidence levels can differ. A score of 35 in one platform is not automatically equivalent to 35 in another. A platform's "keyword difficulty" is not a universal search-engine metric unless the provider explicitly documents a shared standard—which should not be assumed.

Before relying on a vendor metric, record the provider and product/plan, the metric's published definition, market/database/language/device scope, retrieval date, whether the value is measured/sampled/modeled/estimated, and any stated methodology or confidence indicators.

Use third-party data as an additional evidence layer, not as an unquestionable replacement for primary data. If a provider does not disclose enough methodology to evaluate a metric, mark that limitation rather than inventing an explanation.

### 2.5 First-party website and business data

First-party sources may include internal site-search logs, customer support questions, sales and customer-service records, product or service enquiries, on-site search and navigation behavior, conversion and lead records, and product reviews or feedback collected by the organization.

These sources can reveal language, recurring problems, product interest, and business outcomes. They do not automatically measure external search demand. An internal site search is a search performed on the website; it is not a Google search query.

Protect personal and confidential information. Use aggregated or appropriately anonymized records, follow applicable privacy requirements, and avoid copying sensitive customer data into keyword research files.

### 2.6 Other public and market sources

Industry reports, public datasets, marketplace search suggestions, forums, community discussions, and competitor pages can provide useful context. They may reveal terminology, product categories, market events, or questions that deserve investigation.

Their evidential role must remain clear:

- A public discussion is evidence that a discussion occurred, not proof of search volume.
- A competitor's page structure is evidence of that competitor's content choices, not proof of market demand.
- Marketplace search behavior may reflect that marketplace's users and ranking systems, not general web search.
- A survey measures responses from its sample under its methodology, not necessarily actual search behavior.

Treat each source according to what it observes.

## 3. Core Keyword Metrics

Metrics are useful only when their definitions are understood. The same label can hide different measurement systems.

### 3.1 Search volume

**Search volume** is an estimate or reported measure of how often a query or keyword is searched within a defined source, market, and period.

In Google Keyword Planner, **average monthly searches** represents the average number of searches for the keyword and its close variants under the selected location and Search Network settings, averaged across the chosen month range. The default is generally 12 months, and Google rounds the values. [1]

Interpretation rules:

- Record the date range and averaging period.
- Record geography, language, and network settings.
- Check whether close variants are grouped.
- Look at monthly values when seasonality matters.
- Do not assume two tools count the same query set.
- Do not sum overlapping keyword rows as if they were independent demand.

A keyword with 10,000 estimated monthly searches does not guarantee 10,000 visits, impressions, or clicks for a page. Search volume describes an estimated query activity measure; it does not describe how many users will choose a particular result.

### 3.2 Monthly search-volume history

A monthly series can reveal seasonal variation and changes hidden by a single average. Google Ads API historical metrics can provide approximate monthly search volumes for up to four years, depending on the request and available data. [2]

Use the monthly series to ask whether interest is seasonal or relatively stable, whether the average concealed a short-lived spike, whether the current period is comparable with the historical period, and whether the pattern repeats across years.

Do not extrapolate a trend from a tiny number of observations. Distinguish recurring seasonality from a one-time event.

### 3.3 Impressions

An **impression** is a recorded appearance of a result or listing under the measurement system's definition.

In Search Console, impressions describe how often a link to the property was shown in Google Search or another supported Google surface, depending on the report. The precise counting behavior can vary by result type and report. [3]

Search Console impressions are not total searches. They are property-level visibility events. One search may produce impressions for multiple properties, and a query may produce no impression for the property being analyzed.

### 3.4 Clicks

A **click** is a recorded selection of a result or listing that sends a user to the property, subject to the source's counting rules. Search Console reports clicks from Google Search results to the property. [3]

Clicks are closer to actual site acquisition than search-volume estimates, but they still describe only the measured property and report scope. They do not measure all visits from every channel or all searches in the market.

### 3.5 Click-through rate (CTR)

**CTR** is the ratio of clicks to impressions:

CTR = Clicks / Impressions × 100%

Search Console defines CTR as clicks divided by impressions. [3]

CTR helps evaluate how often recorded visibility becomes a click. It should be interpreted with query, page, device, country, date, and search-result context. A low CTR does not identify its cause by itself. A high CTR does not prove that a page satisfies the user's need after the click.

### 3.6 Average position

**Average position** in Search Console is an aggregated position metric for the property's result, query, or URL under Google's reporting methodology. It is not a promise that a user will see the page at one fixed rank for every search. [4]

Position varies across queries, URLs, locations, devices, time, and result layouts. An average can hide substantial variation. Use it as a diagnostic trend within a defined report context—not as a universal rank tracker or a direct measure of business value.

### 3.7 Cost per click (CPC)

**CPC** is an advertising cost metric. In Google Ads, average CPC describes the average amount paid or expected for an ad click under the relevant reporting or forecasting context. Keyword Planner's bid ranges describe historical advertiser bids for top-of-page ad placements, based on selected location and network settings. [1]

CPC can provide context about paid advertiser activity and commercial bidding. It does not directly measure organic ranking difficulty, organic conversion rate, or the revenue a website will earn from ranking organically.

### 3.8 Advertiser competition

Keyword Planner's Competition field represents the number of advertisers showing on a keyword relative to other keywords, within the selected location and Search Network settings. It is classified as low, medium, or high in the interface. [1]

This is **paid-search competition**, not organic SEO competition. High advertiser competition may be commercially interesting, but it does not prove that organic results are difficult to outrank. Low advertiser competition does not mean a keyword is easy to rank for organically.

### 3.9 Competition index

The Google Ads API exposes a numeric competition index from 0 to 100 alongside the competition level. It belongs to Google's advertising keyword-planning metrics. [2]

Do not confuse this with third-party organic keyword-difficulty scores. Similar numeric ranges do not imply the same formula, scale, or meaning.

### 3.10 Keyword difficulty (KD)

**Keyword difficulty** is commonly used by third-party SEO platforms as an estimate of how difficult it may be to rank for a keyword. It is not a universal Google Search metric, and the exact method is provider-specific.

A provider may incorporate link data, SERP observations, ranking-page characteristics, or other modeled inputs. Do not assume a formula unless the provider documents it. Record the provider, definition, database, and retrieval date.

KD can be used as a comparative screening signal within one provider's methodology. It should not be treated as a ranking probability, a guarantee, or a directly comparable score across vendors.

### 3.11 Traffic potential

**Traffic potential** is an estimate of the organic traffic a page or topic could attract, often based on multiple queries, rankings, and click assumptions. The exact definition varies by provider and model.

Do not confuse it with the search volume of one keyword. A page may receive visits from many related queries, while the volume of one tracked phrase may capture only a portion of the page's potential. Conversely, a large modeled traffic estimate does not guarantee the page can achieve those rankings or clicks.

When using a traffic-potential metric, document whether it refers to a single query, a query cluster, a page, a domain, or an entire topic set.

### 3.12 Trend index and relative interest

Google Trends' 0–100 scale represents normalized relative interest for the selected term or topic, geography, and time range. It is not an absolute count and should not be compared across unrelated query contexts as if the scale were universal. [5]

Use Trends to compare patterns under controlled settings, not to turn an index value into estimated monthly searches.

## 4. Metric Categories: Do Not Mix the Measurement Layers

A common research error is combining unlike metrics in one score without preserving what each value measures.

| Measurement layer | Examples | What it describes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Estimated market search activity | Average monthly searches, monthly volume estimates | Estimated query activity under a source's settings | Guaranteed clicks or traffic to a site |
| Relative interest | Google Trends index | Relative search interest in a selected context | Absolute search count |
| Property visibility | Search Console impressions, average position | How a specific property appears in Google reporting | Total market demand |
| Property acquisition | Search Console clicks, CTR | Recorded clicks from visibility to the property | All visits or conversion value |
| Paid advertising context | CPC, top-of-page bids, advertiser competition | Advertiser costs and competition | Organic difficulty or ranking likelihood |
| Vendor modeled SEO metrics | Keyword difficulty, traffic potential | Provider-specific estimates or models | A standardized search-engine measurement |
| First-party business outcomes | Leads, sales, revenue, enquiries | Outcomes recorded by the organization | External search volume without additional evidence |

The layers can complement each other, but they are not interchangeable.

## 5. Making Sources Comparable

Before comparing two values, align the measurement context. If the context differs, explain the difference rather than pretending the numbers are directly comparable.

### 5.1 Geography

Specify the country, region, or city. Search behavior and available data can vary by geography. A global estimate cannot be substituted for a country-level estimate without changing the research question.

### 5.2 Language and query interpretation

Record the selected language and how the tool handles language settings. A keyword may be used across multiple languages, and a tool's language filter may not mean that every returned query is written in that language. Review local phrasing and spelling rather than assuming literal translations are equivalent.

### 5.3 Search network and search type

Record whether a source represents Google Search, a broader Search Network, a marketplace, or another platform. Keyword Planner settings and Google Trends search-type filters affect what is represented. Do not merge datasets from different networks without a clear reason.

### 5.4 Date range and averaging

Record the start and end dates, averaging window, and retrieval date. A 12-month average, a monthly estimate, and a short-term forecast answer different questions.

### 5.5 Device and audience scope

Where available, record device segmentation and any audience or account context that changes the data. Do not assume desktop and mobile behavior are identical or that one tool's device coverage matches another's.

### 5.6 Match behavior and close variants

Check whether a source groups close variants, singular/plural forms, spelling differences, or semantically related phrases. Google Ads historical metrics may include close variants, and API responses may deduplicate them. [1][2]

If two rows share an aggregated metric, do not assign the full value to each row and then sum them. That creates artificial demand.

### 5.7 Currency and commercial context

CPC and bid ranges depend on currency, geography, network, and advertiser context. Keep currency and collection date with the value. Do not compare bids from different markets as if they represented the same economic conditions.

## 6. A Practical Keyword Data Workflow

Use this process after candidate expansion in 05.3.

### Step 1 — Define the research question

Decide what you need to know: whether there is evidence of search activity, whether interest varies by season or region, whether the site already receives impressions or clicks, whether advertiser activity is relevant, whether independent tools agree on broad patterns, and whether there is enough evidence to continue researching a candidate.

Do not collect every metric simply because the export contains it.

### Step 2 — Choose sources by purpose

| Research question | Primary source to consider | Supporting evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated Google keyword activity | Keyword Planner | Third-party estimates, Trends |
| Existing organic visibility for a property | Search Console | Analytics and landing-page data |
| Relative interest and seasonality | Google Trends | Keyword Planner monthly history |
| Paid advertiser activity | Keyword Planner / Google Ads | Campaign data where available |
| Business relevance and outcomes | First-party CRM, sales, support, analytics | Audience research and market evidence |
| Competitive keyword estimates | Third-party SEO platforms | Manual SERP research and first-party performance |

This is a selection guide, not a universal source ranking. The best source depends on the exact question and available access.

### Step 3 — Capture the settings and provenance

For every metric, store the candidate keyword or query group, source and product, metric name and source definition, value and unit, geography and language, search network or search type, date range and averaging period, retrieval date, match/close-variant behavior where documented, and notes about estimates, rounding, privacy filtering, or missing data.

Do not store a naked number with no reproducible context.

### Step 4 — Separate historical data from forecasts

Label historical metrics, sampled relative-interest data, current property performance, and future forecasts separately. A forecast is not an observation. A historical average is not a prediction. A Trends index is not a count.

### Step 5 — Compare sources without forcing agreement

If sources disagree, check whether they measure the same search engine or network; compare geography, language, date range, and device scope; inspect close-variant and aggregation behavior; check whether values are rounded, sampled, or modeled; review the provider's metric definition and methodology; and preserve the disagreement if it cannot be resolved.

Do not average unlike estimates to manufacture a more authoritative-looking number.

### Step 6 — Record missing and censored data honestly

Use distinct states such as:

- **Zero:** the source explicitly reports a numeric zero.
- **Not reported:** the source did not return a value.
- **Below threshold:** the source indicates activity is too low to display or estimate.
- **Anonymized:** a query is withheld for privacy.
- **Unavailable:** the source or account does not provide the metric.
- **Not checked:** the researcher has not evaluated it.

Do not replace missing values with zero. Zero and unknown are different facts.

### Step 7 — Hand off the evidence, not a fake verdict

The output of 05.4 is a documented keyword dataset with source definitions, settings, caveats, and comparability notes. It supports later interpretation; it does not by itself classify intent, prove ranking difficulty, establish opportunity, or select a target page.

## 7. Worked Example: Interpreting Conflicting Metrics

The following example is fictional. Values are invented to illustrate interpretation and are not actual keyword data.

A researcher evaluates the phrase **"espresso grinder"** for a home coffee equipment site:

| Source | Example output | What can reasonably be concluded |
|---|---:|---|
| Keyword Planner | 8,100 average monthly searches | The selected Google Ads settings report a rounded average that includes close variants. |
| Google Trends | 74 | Relative interest is elevated within the chosen comparison, geography, and period; this is not 74 searches. |
| Search Console | 320 impressions, 18 clicks | The property's report recorded this visibility and click activity for the selected period; it does not measure total market demand. |
| Third-party SEO platform | KD 42 | The provider's model assigns this score under its own methodology; it is not a Google ranking metric. |
| Google Ads | CPC estimate $1.20 | This is paid advertising cost context, not the expected organic click value. |

These values do not conflict simply because their numbers differ. They measure different things, over different scopes, using different methods.

A sound research record keeps each metric in its own lane. A bad record adds 8,100, 74, 320, 18, 42, and 1.20 into a "keyword score" and calls the result data-driven. That is arithmetic, not analysis.

## 8. Common Metric and Data-Source Mistakes

### 8.1 Treating search volume as guaranteed traffic

Search volume is not a traffic promise. A page's actual clicks depend on whether it appears, where and how it appears, the searcher's choice, and the measurement scope. Use property performance data to examine actual recorded clicks.

### 8.2 Calling advertiser competition organic keyword difficulty

Keyword Planner's Competition metric is about advertisers. It does not measure how difficult it is to rank an organic page. Keep paid and organic competition separate. [1]

### 8.3 Treating Trends as absolute volume

The 0–100 scale is normalized within the selected context. It cannot be read as a count or directly compared across unrelated settings. [5]

### 8.4 Treating Search Console queries as the whole market

Search Console reports performance for a property and omits anonymized queries from query tables. Its query rows are not a complete inventory of searches performed in the market. [4]

### 8.5 Treating missing data as zero demand

A blank, dash, omitted query, or unavailable metric can have several causes. Check the source's reporting rules before drawing a conclusion.

### 8.6 Adding overlapping volumes

Close variants and related terms may share or aggregate metrics. Summing every exported row can double-count the same underlying activity. Check how the source groups terms before calculating totals. [1][2]

### 8.7 Comparing scores across vendors

Third-party keyword-difficulty and traffic metrics are provider-specific. Similar names and numeric scales do not make the underlying methods equivalent.

### 8.8 Confusing CPC with organic value

CPC reflects paid advertising economics. It can be useful context, but it does not directly tell you organic conversion value, ranking difficulty, or likely SEO return.

### 8.9 Ignoring time and market context

Averages can hide seasonality, local differences, and short-lived events. Preserve monthly history and market settings where those differences affect the decision.

## 9. Output and Handoff

A completed 05.4 research pass should produce a candidate-keyword dataset with source and metric definitions; source provenance for every important value; geography, language, network, date, and match settings; separation of historical metrics, forecasts, relative indices, and property-performance observations; notes on rounding, sampling, close variants, privacy filtering, and missing values; a record of disagreements between sources and how they were investigated; and a concise statement of what the data supports—and what it cannot establish.

The next stage is **05.5 — Search Intent Concepts & Taxonomies**. Metrics can help describe query activity, but they do not reveal intent by themselves. Intent requires interpretation of the query and its context.

## Key Takeaways

- Keyword data is source-dependent; there is no single universal keyword metric.
- Keyword Planner's search volume and advertiser competition are advertising-planning metrics, not organic traffic and ranking-difficulty guarantees.
- Search Console describes reported performance for a specific property, not total market demand.
- Google Trends provides sampled, normalized relative interest—not absolute search volume.
- Third-party KD and traffic-potential scores are vendor-specific models.
- Geography, language, time, network, device, and close-variant behavior affect comparability.
- Missing, anonymized, rounded, or unavailable data must not be silently converted into zero.
- Preserve metric definitions and provenance before using values in later strategy decisions.
