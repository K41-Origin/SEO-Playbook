# 08.9 — Content Maintenance, Updating & Lifecycle Management

## 1. Purpose and Scope

Published content is not finished work. Information changes, services end, prices move, procedures are revised, sources are withdrawn, and reader needs evolve. Without maintenance, a once-helpful page can become a source of confusion.

**Content maintenance** is the ongoing management needed to keep content assets accurate, relevant, usable, and aligned with their editorial purpose. **Content lifecycle management** governs decisions throughout an asset's life: retain, review, update, revise, consolidate, archive, or retire.

GOV.UK recommends monitoring existing content to ensure it remains useful and findable; the outcome may be an update or retirement when content becomes outdated. [1] ONS notes that user needs and topic contexts can change, so content should be reviewed periodically using current user research and analytics where available. [2]

Domain 08.9 focuses on **post-publication editorial operations**. It does not take over:
- substantive revision methods owned by 08.8;
- fact-checking, editorial QA, and formal approval owned by 08.7;
- portfolio-wide inventory and audit owned by 08.10;
- organic performance measurement owned by Domain 14;
- search-engine freshness and ranking mechanisms owned by Domain 03;
- URL, redirect, canonical, metadata, markup, and crawl/index implementation owned by Domains 07 and 10.

## 2. Maintenance Is Not Merely Changing a Date

Maintenance is not a ritual of replacing a timestamp, adding a filler paragraph, or changing a few words so a page looks new.

It must answer three questions:
1. Is the information still correct?
2. Does the content still meet the reader needs that justified its publication?
3. Is the asset's current form and status still the best way to meet those needs?

Google explicitly warns against changing page dates to create a freshness impression when the substance has not changed. It also says adding or removing large amounts of content solely to make a site appear fresher is not a good reason. [3] When a substantial change occurs, displayed publication or update dates should reflect the actual change; Google's date guidance also emphasizes consistency between visible dates and structured data where used. [4]

**Principle:** record a date because a meaningful change occurred, not make a change merely to justify a new date.

## 3. Maintenance Objectives

Well-managed maintenance helps an organization:
- preserve the accuracy of facts, instructions, data, prices, policies, and references;
- ensure content continues to serve its intended reader task;
- handle changes in products, services, regulations, technology, and market conditions;
- reduce the risk of outdated or misleading information;
- prevent duplication and conflicts between pages;
- preserve historical context when older information still needs to be available;
- decide when content should be updated, consolidated, or retired;
- maintain accountability for post-publication changes.

These are editorial operating objectives. None automatically guarantees higher rankings or traffic.

## 4. Maintenance Triggers

Do not rely on calendar schedules alone. Combine planned reviews with change triggers.

| Trigger | Example | Initial response |
|---|---|---|
| Fact change | Price, specification, statistic, feature name, or availability changes | Verify current source and identify affected sections |
| Policy or rule change | Terms, eligibility, procedure, or effective date changes | Escalate to SME/policy owner; assess consequences |
| Product or service change | Feature discontinued, plan changed, UI or process replaced | Retest instructions and revise scope |
| Source change | Official documentation moved, updated, or withdrawn | Check claims that depend on it |
| Error report | Reader, staff member, or SME reports incorrect information | Triage by impact and credibility |
| User-needs change | Reader task, context, or question changes | Validate the new need before changing scope |
| Evidence of content failure | Readers cannot complete a task, instructions confuse, or information cannot be found | Investigate; do not assume the cause |
| Risk or deadline | Safety, financial, legal, or dated information approaches expiry | Accelerate review according to risk |
| Ecosystem change | A standard, platform, or technology changes materially | Check impact on accuracy and recommendations |
| Scheduled review | Review date or internal cycle becomes due | Determine whether review is needed and record the decision |

This is an SEO-Playbook operating framework, not a universal trigger list. Triggers may come from information owners, SMEs, customer support, reader feedback, user research, monitoring systems, or 08.10 audit findings.

## 5. Review Cadence: Set It by Risk

No single review interval fits every asset. Requiring every page to be reviewed every 90 days may look orderly but ignores differences in volatility, risk, and review cost.

Set cadence by considering:

1. **Volatility:** how quickly do facts or topic conditions change?
2. **Consequence:** how serious is the impact if information is wrong or late?
3. **Time sensitivity:** does the content have an effective date, expiry, or event?
4. **Source dependency:** does it rely on third-party documentation that changes often?
5. **User impact:** how important is the content to the reader's task?
6. **Change signals:** are there unresolved error reports, feedback, or evidence of change?
7. **Review effort:** does checking require an SME, testing, or special approval?

### 5.1 Risk-based cadence model

This is an internal SEO-Playbook tool, not an industry standard or Google requirement.

| Operational category | Characteristics | Review approach |
|---|---|---|
| High-risk / high-volatility | Errors may have serious consequences or information changes rapidly | Active trigger monitoring and closer scheduled review; escalate promptly when changes occur |
| Medium-risk / moderate-volatility | Information is fairly stable but depends on changing products, policies, or sources | Periodic review with change-based checkpoints |
| Low-risk / low-volatility | Relatively stable conceptual material and limited consequences of error | Less frequent review; revisit when change signals or needs appear |
| Time-bound | Applies to a particular period, event, version, or date | Set a check date before or after the relevant milestone |

Set actual intervals based on organizational context and document the rationale. A review date is a reminder to evaluate, not proof that content must change. GOV.UK's Whitehall Publisher uses review dates to flag content that has passed its scheduled review. The date prompts inspection; it does not dictate an update. [1]

## 6. Ownership and Accountability

Every active asset needs a clear owner. Without one, changes wait for someone to feel responsible—and often nobody does.

| Role | Maintenance responsibility |
|---|---|
| Content owner | Ensures the asset has a purpose, owner, and lifecycle decision |
| Subject-matter expert (SME) | Checks changes to facts and specialist information |
| Editorial/content lead | Assesses editorial impact, change scope, and revision needs |
| Maintenance coordinator | Tracks review dates, triggers, status, dependencies, and records |
| Approver | Approves according to governance and risk level |
| Technical owner | Implements technical changes such as URLs, redirects, CMS, or page delivery |
| Analytics specialist | Provides performance measurement and interpretation under Domain 14 |

One person may hold multiple roles in a small team. What matters is that responsibilities and decisions remain clear. Organizational governance, decision rights, and escalation follow 08.2.

## 7. Maintenance Workflow

This is an SEO-Playbook operating model. Teams may simplify stages for minor changes, but must not remove verification required by the risk level.

### Stage 1 — Detect
Receive a trigger from a schedule, SME, reader, official source, product change, user research, or monitoring.

Record:
- affected asset and version;
- trigger and discovery date;
- initial source or reporter;
- potential consequences;
- any deadline.

### Stage 2 — Triage
Determine whether the signal requires action and how urgent it is.

Distinguish:
- **confirmed issue:** initial evidence indicates information is wrong or outdated;
- **potential issue:** an indication exists but requires verification;
- **no issue found:** review finds no problem;
- **out of scope:** the issue belongs to another team or domain.

Escalate potential safety, financial, legal, privacy, or user-rights risks under governance. Do not wait for a routine review date.

### Stage 3 — Assess
Check purpose and reader task; affected claims, figures, instructions, and sources; degree of contextual change; dependencies on other assets; whether the same content can still meet the need; and whether a new brief or portfolio decision is required.

ONS recommends grounding user needs in evidence and checking them when content is created or updated. [2]

### Stage 4 — Decide
Choose an action:
- retain without change;
- correct or update;
- perform substantive revision through 08.8;
- restructure or reproduce through 08.5–08.6;
- consolidate with another asset;
- preserve as an archive or historical record;
- retire or unpublish under policy;
- escalate because evidence is insufficient.

### Stage 5 — Execute
Assign owner, scope, sources, reviewers, dependencies, and schedule. Use 08.8 for substantive changes. For changes affecting factual claims, perform 08.7 verification and QA appropriate to the risk.

### Stage 6 — Approve and publish
Ensure the published version has passed applicable review and approval. A content change does not automatically authorize URL, redirect, metadata, markup, or technical-control changes.

### Stage 7 — Record and monitor
Record the decision, changes, sources, reviewers, effective date, and follow-up actions. Set a future review when needed. Organic performance evaluation and attribution belong to Domain 14.

## 8. Choose a Lifecycle Action

Not every asset must be retained, and not every old asset should be deleted.

| Decision | When appropriate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Retain | Still accurate, useful, and fit for need | Record review outcome; do not change content merely to show activity |
| Correct | Factual or instructional error exists | Verify and document correction |
| Update | Specific information changed but main purpose remains | Change affected sections and check dependencies |
| Revise | Quality, structure, or explanation needs substantive improvement | Use 08.8 workflow |
| Re-scope | Audience or purpose changes materially | Return to planning and briefing through 08.3–08.4 |
| Consolidate | Assets overlap or conflict and one source is more appropriate | Preserve unique needs; coordinate technical migration |
| Archive / preserve | Historical or reference value remains although information is no longer current | Label status and date; do not present as active guidance |
| Retire / unpublish | No valid need remains, content misleads, or policy supports withdrawal | Select appropriate technical and communication action |
| Escalate / defer | Evidence, authority, or dependencies are insufficient | Record who must decide and what is needed |

GOV.UK distinguishes withdrawal and unpublishing in its own platform context and notes that guidance can often be updated rather than withdrawn. This is UK government platform policy, not a universal website rule. [5]

### 8.1 Update versus revision

An **update** usually addresses a limited change: price, date, version, link, step, or detail, while the page's main purpose remains intact.

A **revision** addresses substantive improvement: argument, completeness, explanatory structure, evidence quality, or ability to serve the reader task. Substantive revision belongs to 08.8.

This is a working classification, not an absolute technical boundary. If a small update changes the main recommendation or content scope, treat it as revision and increase review depth.

### 8.2 Consolidation

Consider consolidation when multiple assets:
- serve the same need without a clear division of purpose;
- contain conflicting instructions;
- fragment essential information and make task completion difficult;
- have one asset better suited to be the primary source.

Before consolidating:
1. identify unique needs served by each asset;
2. decide which content and evidence must be preserved;
3. select the canonical editorial destination;
4. document the decision and owner;
5. coordinate URL, redirect, canonical, and internal-link changes with the relevant technical domains.

An editorial consolidation decision does not determine redirects or canonical URLs. Technical implementation remains outside 08.9.

### 8.3 Retirement and preservation

Before retiring content, ask:
- Has the user need genuinely disappeared?
- Does the asset retain historical, legal, transparency, or reference value?
- Does another page meet the same need better?
- Would removal destroy important context?
- Does the content contain sensitive information, rights issues, or other risks requiring escalation?

Retirement does not mean delete. Options include keeping a page with an archive label, explaining that information is no longer current, moving it to an archive, or ending publication. URL choices, redirects, deletion, and access controls must be coordinated with Domain 10 and relevant policy owners.

## 9. Publication Dates, Update Dates, and Change Logs

Distinguish:
- **Published date:** when the asset was first published.
- **Updated/modified date:** when a change meeting editorial policy was made.
- **Review date:** when the asset should be checked again.

They are not interchangeable. A review date becoming due does not mean content changed. A review ending in a retain decision should not be falsified into an update.

Google explains that its systems may use several signals to estimate publication or significant update dates. It recommends clear, consistent dates when publishers display dates and use structured data. [4] Google's people-first guidance specifically discourages changing dates to make pages appear fresh without substantive changes. [3]

### 9.1 Minimum change record

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Content ID / URL | Asset identity |
| Previous version | Version before change |
| Trigger | Schedule, new source, feedback, or event |
| Decision | Retain, update, revise, consolidate, archive, retire |
| Change summary | Summary of changes |
| Evidence / source | Supporting source and evidence |
| Effective date | Date new information takes effect |
| Content owner | Decision owner |
| Reviewer / SME | Fact and substance reviewer |
| Approval | Status and approver |
| Published version | Released version |
| Next review | Next date or trigger |
| Limitations | Uncertainty and dependencies |

For a review with no change, preserve the outcome, date, reviewer, and rationale for retaining. Do not create a new version merely to make history look busy.

## 11. Maintenance Failures to Avoid

1. **Date changed, content unchanged.** Cosmetic work is not maintenance.
2. **Same interval for all content.** Asset risks and volatility differ.
3. **Review date treated as rewrite deadline.** Review may conclude with retain.
4. **Old content automatically deleted.** Age does not prove an asset is useless.
5. **Traffic decline immediately called outdated content.** Investigate; Domain 14 handles performance analysis.
6. **Small changes go unverified.** One wrong figure can affect reader decisions.
7. **No clear owner.** A trigger is found but nobody decides.
8. **Pages merged without mapping unique needs.** Important information may disappear.
9. **Historical content presented as active guidance.** Readers lose temporal context.
10. **Retirement without technical coordination.** Links, URLs, redirects, and user experience may break.
11. **Maintenance becomes a full portfolio audit.** Inventory and comprehensive audit methods belong to 08.10.
12. **Maintenance claimed to improve rankings.** Editorial decisions do not causally prove ranking impact.

## 12. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** a guide explains how to export a report from a SaaS application. The vendor changes navigation and moves the export menu.

**Trigger:** support receives several reports that readers cannot find the described menu.

**Triage and assessment:**
1. The maintenance coordinator records the URL, report dates, and guide version.
2. The content owner checks the application and current vendor documentation.
3. An SME confirms that the old steps no longer apply to the current interface.
4. The team checks whether only one step changed or whether permissions and feature availability also changed.

**Decision:** a limited update is appropriate because purpose and audience remain the same. If the export flow and prerequisites have changed substantially, escalate to substantive revision through 08.8.

**Execution:**
1. The writer updates steps and screenshots against the checked application version.
2. The SME verifies accuracy.
3. The editor performs 08.7 QA.
4. The technical team implements page changes if needed.
5. The change record captures source, application version, effective date, reviewers, and next review date.

**Outcome:** the content again matches the verified conditions. This example does not claim the update automatically increases rankings or traffic.

## Key Takeaways

- Maintenance is ongoing editorial operation after publication.
- Use triggers and scheduled reviews; do not rely on calendars alone.
- Cadence should reflect risk, volatility, time sensitivity, and reader impact.
- A review may conclude with no change.
- A limited update differs from substantive revision; 08.8 owns the revision workflow.
- Consolidation and retirement must preserve user needs and historical context.
- Publication date, update date, and review date serve different purposes.
- Material decisions need an owner, evidence, approval, and change record.
- Domain 08.10 owns portfolio inventory and audits; Domain 14 owns measurement; Domain 03 owns freshness ranking systems.
- Good maintenance preserves information reliability. Rankings are not guaranteed.
