# 08.7 — Editorial Review, Accuracy & Quality Assurance

A completed draft is not automatically ready for publication. It may look polished, sound convincing, and still be wrong. Editorial review exists to find problems before readers do.

**08.7 governs the review of substance, facts, sources, consistency, risk, and publication readiness.** Its purpose is not to make every reviewer rewrite the content. It is to ensure that production outputs meet the brief, can be substantiated, and do not mislead readers.

This document applies to drafts and assets produced through the editorial workflow. The full production workflow belongs to 08.6; this document governs the review and quality gates during and after production.

## 1. Definitions and Scope

### 1.1 Editorial review

**Editorial review** is a structured evaluation of content to determine whether its substance, presentation, and output meet the editorial purpose, reader needs, organizational standards, and agreed requirements.

Review may cover:
- alignment with the brief and acceptance criteria;
- completeness and relevance of information;
- logic, clarity, and coherence of explanations;
- factual accuracy and contextual precision;
- source quality, sufficiency, and traceability;
- attribution, quotations, permissions, and provenance;
- consistency of terminology, figures, dates, and claims;
- legal, safety, reputational, privacy, or compliance risks;
- editorial readiness before publication.

### 1.2 Fact-checking

**Fact-checking** examines factual claims against relevant evidence to determine whether they are accurate, complete, current, and presented in the right context.

It is not merely rereading or asking an SME to say “looks good.” Reviewers should identify which claims were checked, the evidence supporting or contradicting them, and what remains uncertain.

### 1.3 Quality assurance (QA)

**Editorial QA** is a set of controls used to determine whether content meets standards and acceptance criteria before it is declared ready for publication.

QA is not a single proofreading pass at the end. Complex work may require several stages: source checks, substantive review, fact-checking, risk review, proofreading, and final sign-off.

## 2. Why Review Must Be Split into Different Functions

One reviewer rarely has the expertise to check everything. An editor may assess structure and clarity but may not be authorized to confirm technical details. An SME may validate facts but is not automatically the right editor for style, flow, or reader needs.

ONS distinguishes peer review focused on structure, flow, style, and user needs from stakeholder review focused on fact-checking. GOV.UK also separates the content designer's responsibility for presentation from the SME's responsibility for facts. [1][2]

Separate at least these functions:

| Function | Core question | Suitable reviewer |
|---|---|---|
| Editorial review | Is the content clear, relevant, coherent, and aligned with the brief? | Editor or content designer |
| Fact-check | Are factual claims correct and properly contextualized? | SME or competent fact owner |
| Source verification | Do sources support the claims and can they be traced? | Editor/researcher, with SME as needed |
| Specialist/risk review | Does the content create professional, legal, safety, privacy, or compliance risk? | Reviewer with relevant competence and authority |
| Proofreading | Are there remaining language, spelling, number, formatting, or consistency errors? | Proofreader or second reviewer |
| Publication approval | Have required gates been met, and may the content be published? | Approver designated by 08.2 governance |

One person may perform multiple roles in a small team if competent. The functions and decisions must still be recorded. Do not assume one sign-off validates matters that were never checked.

## 3. Start with the Brief and Acceptance Criteria

Review needs a benchmark. Without the brief and acceptance criteria, feedback can become personal preference.

Prepare:
- approved brief and outline versions;
- acceptance criteria;
- a draft with an identifiable version;
- claim and source lists;
- assumptions and open-question notes;
- style guide and editorial standards;
- reviewer list and approval authority;
- risk classification or specialist-review requirements, where relevant.

ONS recommends checking whether content meets user needs and acceptance criteria, while asking stakeholders to identify what is wrong, misleading, or missing. [1]

If the draft changes materially after approval—for example, its conclusion, key figures, recommendation, or scope changes—the relevant review must be repeated for affected sections. Approval of an old version is not permission to publish a new one.

## 4. Claim Verification System

### 4.1 Break content into checkable claims

Do not fact-check a document by asking only “is this correct?” Break material statements into claims that can be tested.

Examples:
- “Product A supports integration X.”
- “The rate takes effect on January 1.”
- “The study included 1,200 respondents.”
- “This policy applies to all customers in the region.”
- “This method reduces processing time by 30%.”

Check each claim against its source, context, date, population, method, and scope of applicability where relevant.

### 4.2 Use a claim ledger

A claim ledger records claims and their verification status. This is an SEO-Playbook operational template, not a universal standard.

| ID | Claim | Type | Source/evidence | Date/context | Status | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-01 | [Claim under review] | Fact / figure / interpretation / recommendation | [URL, document, data] | [Date, region, version] | Verified / Qualified / Unverified / Rejected | [Retain, qualify, revise, remove] |
| C-02 | [Claim under review] | [Type] | [Evidence] | [Context] | [Status] | [Action] |

A claim ledger is most useful for content with many figures, technical claims, multiple contributors, or high risk. For simple content, well-organized source notes may be sufficient.

### 4.3 Distinguish claim statuses

Use statuses that do not conceal uncertainty:

- **Verified:** available evidence supports the claim within its stated scope and context.
- **Qualified:** the claim is usable in principle but needs a limitation, date, attribution, or additional explanation.
- **Unverified:** there is insufficient evidence to confirm it.
- **Conflicted:** relevant sources disagree and the discrepancy remains unresolved.
- **Rejected:** evidence shows that the claim is false, unsupported, or misleading.
- **Out of scope:** the claim is unnecessary for the content's purpose.

This is an internal working taxonomy, not an official ONS, GOV.UK, or Google classification.

### 4.4 Check context, not just numbers

A figure can be correct and still mislead when:
- the measurement period is omitted;
- the population or sample is unclear;
- regions or currencies differ;
- units are inconsistent;
- old data is presented as current;
- correlation is presented as causation;
- an average is used to describe every case;
- one study's result is generalized to an entire population.

For statistics, check variable definitions, methods, sample size, period, units, rounding, and limitations. For prices, features, regulations, and product policies, check versions and effective dates.

## 5. Source Verification and Evidence Quality

### 5.1 Select sources for the claim type

| Claim type | Preferred evidence |
|---|---|
| Policy or regulation | Applicable official text, regulator, or competent authority |
| Product features and specifications | Official documentation, release notes, or documented demonstration/testing |
| Statistics | Original dataset, methodology, and publication by the data-producing institution |
| Scientific/medical claims | Primary research and high-quality evidence syntheses; expert validation according to risk |
| Prices and commercial terms | Official pricing page or document with date and region |
| First-hand experience | Test records, method, conditions, sample, and checkable evidence |
| Organizational statements | Official document or statement from the organization concerned |
| Historical claims | Traceable primary archives or credible historical sources |

Source priority depends on the claim. An official source may be best for an organization's policy, but it is not automatically independent evidence that the policy is effective.

### 5.2 Test sources with concrete questions

- Does the source actually state what the claim says?
- Is it a primary source or merely repeating another source?
- Who published it, authored it, or owns the data?
- When was it published, updated, or made effective?
- Do its region, population, product, or version match?
- Are methods and limitations explained?
- Are there relevant conflicts of interest?
- Is the source still accessible and verifiable?
- Does it support the whole sentence or only part of it?

Do not cite a homepage, search snippet, or secondary article as though the primary document had been checked.

### 5.3 Handle conflicting sources

When two credible sources disagree:
1. Check their respective dates and versions.
2. Compare definitions, scope, methods, and context.
3. Look for a primary source or more specific clarification.
4. Ask an SME when the conflict concerns specialist facts.
5. Explain uncertainty if it remains unresolved.
6. Do not choose the source that best supports the preferred narrative merely because it is convenient.

If a conflict affects the main conclusion, the content must not pass the publication gate until the conflict is resolved or transparently disclosed.

## 6. Editorial Review: Substance, Clarity, and Consistency

### 6.1 Review purpose and reader needs

Check:
- whether the content meets the brief's purpose and audience;
- whether priority needs and acceptance criteria are satisfied;
- whether key information is easy to find;
- whether any section does not help the reader's task;
- whether conclusions match the evidence presented;
- whether important limitations and exceptions are explained.

Google encourages creators to assess whether content provides original information, adequate explanation, added value, accuracy, and a satisfying experience. This is creator-facing self-assessment guidance, not a ranking formula or replacement for editorial review. [4]

### 6.2 Review logic and explanations

Check:
- whether main claims have reasoning and evidence;
- whether conclusions follow from premises;
- whether facts are separated from interpretation and opinion;
- whether recommendations state their conditions of application;
- whether there are overgeneralizations;
- whether relevant trade-offs and contraindications are explained;
- whether terminology is consistent;
- whether readers can follow without unreasonable assumed knowledge.

### 6.3 Review language and editing

Check:
- sentence and paragraph clarity;
- language appropriate to the audience;
- unexplained jargon;
- repetition and sentences that add no meaning;
- tone and style-guide compliance;
- spelling, grammar, punctuation, and consistent terminology;
- readability of tables, captions, and supporting components.

ONS distinguishes editing to clarify information from final proofreading to find spelling, grammar, consistency, data, chart, and formatting errors. [3]

### 6.4 Do not turn review into a battle of preferences

Feedback should identify a problem and its reason. “I don't like this section” is not a QA finding.

Use this format:
- **Location:** section, paragraph, table, or claim.
- **Problem:** what is inaccurate, unclear, missing, or inconsistent.
- **Impact:** why it matters to readers or risk.
- **Evidence:** source or standard underlying the feedback.
- **Action:** retain, revise, verify, escalate, or remove.

## 7. Attribution, Quotations, Originality, and Provenance

### 7.1 Attribution does not replace editorial value

When content uses information from other sources, attribution must be accurate and readers must be able to find the source. However, adding many links does not automatically make content original or high quality.

Google explicitly encourages creators not to merely copy or rewrite other sources without substantial added value. It also emphasizes original information, reporting, research, or analysis. [4]

### 7.2 Check quotations and paraphrases

Ensure that:
- direct quotations are accurate and do not change meaning;
- quotations have appropriate attribution;
- paraphrases are not too close to the source's structure or wording;
- quotation context is not cut in a misleading way;
- data and visuals have appropriate permission or basis for use;
- third-party material is not presented as original work.

Copyright, licensing, privacy, and data-use requirements depend on jurisdiction and context. Escalate material risks to competent legal/compliance specialists; this document is not legal advice.

### 7.3 Verify original contributions

If content claims original research, testing, experience, or data, check:
- who actually performed the work;
- method and conditions;
- relevant sample, device, version, or period;
- checkable supporting evidence;
- limitations and potential bias;
- whether conclusions are proportionate to the evidence.

Do not claim “we tested” if the team only read specifications. Do not invent author biographies, credentials, quotations, photographs, or experience. Google explicitly warns against deceptive authorship or credential information. [4]

## 8. Review AI-Assisted or AI-Generated Content

AI does not remove publisher responsibility. Fluent output can still contain false facts, fabricated sources, nonexistent quotations, incorrect figures, or false attribution.

For AI-assisted content:
1. Record AI's role in the workflow when relevant to internal accountability.
2. Verify every material factual claim using sources that were actually opened.
3. Open and inspect every reference; do not accept generated bibliographies at face value.
4. Confirm that quotations, data, names, dates, and URLs actually exist.
5. Check whether output copies or closely tracks third-party sources.
6. Confirm that claimed analysis, demonstrations, and experiences were actually performed.
7. Ask SMEs to review technical or high-risk material.
8. Consider disclosure to readers when the production process is information they would reasonably expect to know.

Google says transparency about who, how, and why content was created can help readers understand the process, and AI disclosure may be relevant when readers would reasonably ask how content was produced. Google does not say that every use of AI must always receive the same label. [4]

Do not use AI as evidence for claims it generated itself. AI can help formulate questions or draft text, but it does not replace primary sources, expert reviewers, or publisher accountability.

## 9. Risk-Based Review and Escalation

Review depth should reflect the consequences of error, uncertainty, and required expertise. Do not apply a heavy process to every typo, but do not treat high-risk content like a promotional caption.

### 9.1 Risk factors

Consider:
- potential effects on health, safety, finances, rights, or well-being;
- legal or regulatory consequences;
- reputational impact and trust;
- privacy and security sensitivity;
- technical complexity;
- uncertainty or conflicting evidence;
- audience size and potential spread;
- how quickly the information changes.

Google identifies topics that could significantly affect health, financial stability, safety, or societal well-being as contexts requiring greater attention to reliability and accuracy. This is not a complete definition of legal or professional risk categories. [4]

### 9.2 Review-level model

The following is an illustrative operational framework, not a universal industry standard.

| Level | Example conditions | Suggested minimum controls |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Minor language change, stable facts, low consequences | Editorial review or second-person proofreading |
| Medium | Guides, comparisons, product claims, multiple sources | Editorial review, source check, fact-check by appropriate owner/SME |
| High | Medical, legal, financial, safety, regulatory, or high-impact claims | All medium controls, specialist review, contextual risk/legal/compliance review, explicit approval |

Set risk level based on content and consequences, not format or length. A short article about medication dosage may be riskier than a long report about design history.

### 9.3 When to stop publication

Hold publication if:
- a main claim remains unverified;
- a primary source cannot be checked;
- conflicting evidence changes the conclusion;
- a competent SME has not validated a critical fact;
- a high-risk recommendation has not received required specialist review;
- quotations, data, or experience lack provenance;
- potential privacy, licensing, or third-party rights violations remain unresolved;
- the latest changes have not undergone relevant re-review.

A deadline is not evidence. “It must go live today” does not turn an unverified claim into fact.

## 10. Editorial QA Workflow

The workflow can be adapted to team size, but decisions and responsibilities must be clear.

1. **Draft intake.** Record ID, version, owner, brief, outline, format, and status.
2. **Completeness check.** Ensure draft, source pack, assets, and acceptance criteria are available.
3. **Editorial review.** Check purpose, user needs, structure, explanation, style, and consistency.
4. **Claim/source verification.** Check material claims and supporting sources.
5. **SME fact-check.** Ask the fact owner to validate specialist details and identify errors specifically.
6. **Specialist/risk review.** Run it when risk level or governance requires it.
7. **Revision.** Writer/editor addresses findings and records decisions about feedback.
8. **Re-review.** Recheck claims and changed sections; material changes may trigger broader review.
9. **Final proofreading.** Check language, figures, tables, captions, and final-version consistency.
10. **Approval gate.** Approver confirms required controls and decides publish/hold/reject.
11. **Publication handoff.** Send the approved version to publication and technical implementation.

ONS describes peer and stakeholder review as part of the user-needs-to-content process. Westmorland and Furness Council's process also separates fact-checking by the content owner from review by another content designer before publication. These are organizational examples, not the only mandatory workflow. [1][5]

## 11. Feedback, Revision, and Dispute Resolution

### 11.1 Actionable feedback

Ask reviewers to identify:
- the problem location;
- the disputed claim or section;
- what is wrong or missing;
- evidence supporting the correction;
- whether the issue is factual, editorial, legal, or a preference.

GOV.UK asks fact-checkers to show what is wrong, why it is wrong, and where the error appears; the fact-check role is limited to factual accuracy, not rewriting copy. That guidance was last updated in 2017, so use it as an example of role separation rather than the sole source for all modern practice. [2]

### 11.2 When reviewers disagree

- Check whether they are discussing the same type of issue.
- Separate testable facts from editorial preferences.
- Ask each position for evidence or the relevant standard.
- Escalate factual disputes to a competent SME or source authority.
- Escalate policy disputes to the governance owner.
- Record the final decision and its rationale.

Do not settle disputes by popularity vote. A fact does not become true because five people like the same claim.

### 11.3 Keep a review record

Record:
- the draft version reviewed;
- reviewer and role;
- review date;
- findings and severity;
- evidence or correction references;
- decisions and actions;
- unresolved items;
- approver and final status.

This record makes decisions traceable and reduces repeated review of the same issues.

## 12. Publication Readiness and Release Gate

Content may be declared editorially ready when:

| Area | Passing criterion |
|---|---|
| Brief alignment | Purpose, audience, scope, and acceptance criteria are met |
| Accuracy | Material claims are verified or honestly qualified |
| Sources | Sources are relevant, traceable, and support the claims |
| Attribution | Quotations, paraphrases, data, visuals, and third-party contributions are appropriately attributed |
| Completeness | No critical gap prevents the reader's task |
| Logic | Conclusions match evidence and do not exceed its limits |
| Consistency | Terms, figures, dates, names, and claims are consistent |
| Language | Content is clear and follows editorial standards |
| Risk | Required specialist reviews and approvals are complete |
| Version | Final version is the same version that was approved |
| Open issues | No unresolved critical issue remains |

Suggested final statuses:
- **Approved:** all required gates have been met.
- **Approved with conditions:** only documented minor conditions remain and they do not affect accuracy, safety, or the primary purpose.
- **Hold:** corrections or additional evidence are required before a final decision.
- **Rejected:** the content does not meet purpose, standards, or risk requirements and must be reworked.

These are internal workflow labels. Organizations may use different labels, but must not disguise a hold as an approval.

## 13. Corrections and Post-Publication Errors

QA cannot guarantee that errors will never occur. When an issue is found after publication:
1. Record the report, URL/asset, time, and disputed section.
2. Assess severity and potential impact.
3. Verify the issue with appropriate sources or SMEs.
4. Decide whether immediate correction, a correction note, temporary withdrawal, or escalation is needed.
5. Update content through the relevant workflow and approval.
6. Record what changed, why, the evidence, and who approved it.
7. Notify affected parties when organizational policy or risk requires it.
8. Assess whether the QA process should change to prevent recurrence.

Minor corrections do not always require a public note. Corrections changing a conclusion, key figure, recommendation, or information that may affect reader decisions may require greater transparency. The decision should reflect context and publisher policy.

Ongoing maintenance, review cadence, update triggers, and lifecycle decisions are covered in 08.9. Domain 08.7 focuses on quality control and handling findings.

## 15. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** a draft guide compares three inventory software services.

The draft claims:
- Vendor A supports integration with a specific accounting system.
- Vendor B is 20% cheaper.
- Inventory software can reduce stock errors.
- The editorial team tested all three products.

QA must do more than check grammar. It examines each claim:

| Claim | Check | Illustrative finding | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vendor A supports integration X | Official documentation and feature test for the stated version | Support is available only on a particular plan | Add plan limitation and check date |
| Vendor B is 20% cheaper | Price, billing period, currency, tax, and comparable features | Initial comparison is not equivalent | Recalculate using consistent criteria or remove claim |
| Software reduces stock errors | Research source and implementation context | Evidence is insufficient for a universal claim | Qualify the statement or remove it |
| Team tested all three products | Test records, accounts, scenarios, dates, and results | No evidence of direct testing | Remove the first-hand testing claim |

This example shows why claims must be tested individually. A plausible-sounding sentence is not evidence.

## 16. Common Editorial QA Failures

1. **Self-review is treated as sufficient.** Authors are too familiar with their drafts and may miss their own errors.
2. **SMEs are asked to edit style.** Fact-checking becomes rewriting and the review purpose blurs.
3. **Editors are asked to certify facts outside their expertise.** Responsibility and competence do not match.
4. **Sources are collected but not checked.** A long bibliography does not prove a claim.
5. **Figures are checked without context.** Period, unit, population, and method disappear.
6. **Quotations are paraphrased too freely.** Meaning or attribution changes.
7. **AI output is trusted because it reads smoothly.** Fluency is not verification.
8. **Reviewers give preference-based feedback.** There is no location, rationale, or evidence.
9. **Approval remains attached to an old file.** The post-revision version is not rechecked.
10. **Deadlines override quality gates.** Unverified claims are still published.
11. **All risks receive the same treatment.** High-risk content does not get adequate review.
12. **QA is treated as a ranking guarantee.** Editorial QA improves accountability and reduces errors; it does not guarantee search performance.

## Key Takeaways

- A completed draft is not necessarily ready for publication.
- Separate editorial review, fact-checking, source verification, specialist review, proofreading, and approval.
- Verify material claims individually; check context, dates, methods, and scope.
- SMEs validate specialist facts; editors own presentation quality within their roles and governance.
- Sources must actually support claims. References are not decoration.
- AI-assisted content still requires human review and source verification.
- QA depth should reflect risk and the consequences of error.
- Approval applies only to the version actually reviewed.
- Record findings, decisions, revisions, and unresolved issues.
- Editorial QA supports accuracy and accountability, not ranking guarantees.
