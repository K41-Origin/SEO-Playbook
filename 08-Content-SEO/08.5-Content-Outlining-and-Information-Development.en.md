# 08.5 — Content Outlining & Information Development

An outline is not a list of headings designed to make a page look substantial. It is a **working model for organizing information**: what readers need to understand, in what order, how sections relate, and what evidence makes each claim credible.

If the brief answers *what must be achieved*, information development answers *how the information should be built to achieve it*.

This document covers substantive information structure developed from an approved brief. Its focus is explanation, argument, flow, and relationships between sections—not HTML heading hierarchy, semantic architecture, or keyword research.

## 1. Definitions and Scope

### 1.1 Content outlining

A **content outline** is a structured representation of the information an asset will communicate. It identifies:
- reader needs served;
- topics, questions, and information to explain;
- grouping and sequence;
- logical relationships between sections;
- required evidence, examples, data, or visuals;
- material deliberately excluded.

An outline may be a nested list, table, flowchart, storyboard, information cards, or another format suited to the asset. Its form is flexible; its purpose is to make the structure reviewable before drafting costs grow.

### 1.2 Information development

**Information development** turns raw inputs—briefs, research, sources, data, SME knowledge, and user needs—into explanations that are accurate, coherent, and sufficiently complete for the reader's task.

It includes:
- identifying core questions;
- checking causal relationships and conceptual dependencies;
- developing necessary definitions and context;
- structuring arguments or procedures;
- selecting relevant evidence and examples;
- identifying gaps, contradictions, and duplication;
- deciding what to explain, summarize, link to, or exclude.

### 1.3 Ownership boundaries

| Term | Owner |
|---|---|
| Editorial outline and information sequence | Domain 08.5 |
| HTML H1–H6 structure and hierarchy | Domain 07.5 |
| Title elements, meta descriptions, URLs, alt text, semantic HTML | Domain 07 |
| Entity modeling, semantic relationships, topical coverage, semantic architecture | Domain 09 |
| Keyword discovery, intent analysis, clustering, keyword-to-page mapping | Domain 05 |

An editorial idea may become a section or subsection without being assigned an H2 or H3 immediately. HTML element selection belongs to page-level implementation in Domain 07.5. [4]

## 2. Position in the Editorial Workflow

Information development receives a sufficiently clear brief and prepares the substantive framework for drafting.

1. **08.3 — Planning & Portfolio:** work is selected and scheduled.
2. **08.4 — Brief & Specification:** purpose, audience, scope, evidence, and acceptance criteria are established.
3. **08.5 — Outlining & Information Development:** information is grouped, sequenced, and developed into a coherent flow.
4. **08.6 — Creation & Production:** the outline guides the draft and assets.
5. **08.7 — Editorial Review & QA:** the result is checked against the brief, evidence, accuracy, and editorial standards.

Small assignments may simplify this sequence. For complex work, reviewing the outline before drafting helps catch structural problems while revisions are still relatively cheap.

The Office for National Statistics (ONS) describes a user-needs-to-content process: validate needs and acceptance criteria, create an outline from prioritized needs, write from the outline, and review. This is an institutional example, not the only valid workflow. [1]

## 3. Start with the Brief, Not a Blank Page

Before outlining, confirm that these inputs exist or explicitly record the gaps.

| Input | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| Approved brief | Purpose, audience, user needs, scope, exclusions, format, acceptance criteria |
| Prioritized questions | Most important reader questions and their relative priority |
| Domain 05 handoff | Approved keyword/page mapping and intent research—as context, not a prompt to repeat the research |
| Evidence and source pack | Facts, primary sources, data, dates, limitations, and claims still requiring verification |
| SME input | Technical or operational knowledge, including uncertainty |
| Existing content | Canonical material, duplication, conflicts, and opportunities to reference other assets |
| Semantic handoff | Relevant entities or relationships supplied by Domain 09 |
| Production constraints | Format, CMS capabilities, visual needs, accessibility, technical limits |
| Governance | Reviewers, approvals, sensitivities, escalation route |

Do not silently fill gaps with assumptions. Record open questions, unavailable sources, and decisions awaiting SMEs. If a gap materially changes purpose or scope, return the brief to its owner for revision. [1][2]

## 4. Turn User Needs into Information Structure

### 4.1 Group genuinely related needs

Start with user needs and acceptance criteria in the brief. Group them by shared task, concept, decision, or process stage.

Do not group questions merely because they use similar words. “How much does it cost?” and “How do I cancel?” may concern the same product but serve different tasks.

ONS describes grouping similar needs, labeling the groups, and prioritizing them to form a content structure. [1]

### 4.2 Distinguish core and supporting needs

Use these working categories:

- **Core need:** without this information, readers cannot complete the primary task.
- **Supporting need:** helps readers understand, compare, or apply the core information.
- **Contextual need:** provides necessary context, definitions, boundaries, or background.
- **Optional detail:** useful to some readers but not essential to the primary outcome.
- **Out of scope:** unnecessary for this asset or already handled elsewhere.

These categories are an SEO-Playbook editorial tool, not a universal official taxonomy. Adapt them to the content type and evidence.

### 4.3 Give each group a clear function

Labels should explain what readers will understand or do. Labels such as “Miscellaneous,” “Additional information,” or “Important things” often conceal an underdeveloped structure.

Illustrative outline for an inventory software guide:
1. Understand when stock-recording methods need improvement.
2. Compare manual methods, spreadsheets, and software.
3. Assess cost and integration needs.
4. Choose an approach for the business context.
5. Prepare implementation next steps.

These labels describe information functions, not final HTML headings.

## 5. Determine Information Order

Sequence is not cosmetic. It determines whether readers receive prerequisites before being asked to understand a conclusion, compare options, or act.

### 5.1 Put the most important needs first

Present the answer or most-needed information early. Do not force readers through a long history, unnecessary definitions, or generic introductions before reaching the point.

GOV.UK recommends putting the most important information first and progressively reducing detail. ONS also describes the inverted pyramid: essential information first, followed by context that deepens understanding, then details not essential to every reader. [2][3]

### 5.2 Choose a sequence based on the task and dependencies

| Pattern | Typical sequence | Useful when |
|---|---|---|
| Direct answer first | Core answer → rationale → evidence → details/conditions | Readers need a specific answer |
| Task sequence | Preparation → steps → result → troubleshooting | Readers must complete a procedure |
| Decision support | Criteria → options → trade-offs → conditional recommendation → next step | Readers must compare choices |
| Conceptual explanation | Phenomenon → definition → mechanism → implications → limitations | Readers need to understand a concept or system |
| Problem-solution | Problem → supported causes → solution options → consequences | Readers are addressing a problem |
| Chronological | Initial state → events/stages → outcome → implications | Time or event order matters |
| Evidence-led analysis | Main finding → evidence → interpretation → limitations → implications | Content presents analysis or research |

These are thinking tools, not templates to impose mechanically. One asset may combine patterns when the reader's task calls for it.

### 5.3 Check information dependencies

Before explaining concept B, does the reader need concept A? Before recommending an action, have its risks and conditions been explained?

Create a simple dependency map:
- **Prerequisite:** information that must be understood first.
- **Main point:** core answer or concept.
- **Evidence:** support for the point.
- **Implication:** what it means to the reader.
- **Next action:** subsequent step or decision.

Do not order information by the sequence in which the author discovered it. Research notes are not the reader's explanation.

## 6. Build Explanations and Arguments

Every section needs a clear job. For each information group, ask:
1. What is its main message or question?
2. What must readers know beforehand?
3. What claim or explanation will be presented?
4. What evidence supports it?
5. What example or demonstration will clarify it?
6. What limitations, exceptions, or trade-offs matter?
7. How does this section support the content's purpose?

### 6.1 Build a claim–evidence–reasoning chain

For analytical or argumentative content, use:
- **Claim:** the statement to explain or defend.
- **Evidence:** relevant sources, data, observations, or examples.
- **Reasoning:** why the evidence supports the claim.
- **Qualification:** conditions, limits, uncertainty, or alternative explanations.
- **Implication:** what it means for readers.

Do not stop at stacking quotations. Data without explanation is not analysis; it is a warehouse of numbers.

### 6.2 Separate fact, interpretation, and recommendation

Mark the type of statement expected in each section:
- verifiable fact;
- interpretation of evidence;
- contextual experience or example;
- conditional recommendation;
- hypothesis not yet adequately tested.

State the conditions behind a recommendation. Do not turn one experience into a universal law.

### 6.3 Show trade-offs and unsuitable cases

Credible explanations cover not only when something works, but also when it is unsuitable, what it costs, and what remains unknown.

For comparisons, apply consistent criteria to every option. Do not compare price for one product, ease of use for another, then declare a winner as though the measures were equivalent.

## 7. Develop Definitions, Context, and Examples

### 7.1 Definitions should serve understanding

Explain terms when readers need them. Avoid a long glossary at the beginning if new terms can be explained in context.

For complex concepts:
1. Give a concise definition.
2. Explain the function or mechanism.
3. Show an example.
4. Explain limits or exceptions.
5. Connect it to the next decision or concept.

### 7.2 Use examples to explain, not decorate

Good examples:
- are relevant to the reader's situation;
- genuinely demonstrate the concept;
- include context and assumptions;
- are not disguised as real data when illustrative;
- do not generalize beyond the evidence.

Label hypothetical examples as illustrative. For customer data, experiments, or first-hand experience, establish provenance, permissions, and context.

### 7.3 Place evidence close to claims

Plan where sources, data, quotations, tables, diagrams, or demonstrations will support the relevant section. Evidence far removed from its claim can make the relationship difficult to understand.

Primary sources are appropriate for checking policies, specifications, product documentation, or official data. For interpretations and recommendations, state the limits of inference. Final accuracy and attribution checks remain part of 08.7.

## 8. Avoid Duplication, Gaps, and Scope Creep

### 8.1 Give information one primary home

When a canonical asset already explains a definition or procedure in full, consider:
- summarizing only the minimum context needed;
- referring readers to the more complete asset;
- avoiding long copies that will quickly become inconsistent.

ONS recommends removing material unrelated to user needs or repeated across releases, and linking to related content rather than repeating it. This is that organization's guidance; apply it while ensuring readers still have enough context. [3]

### 8.2 Audit the outline with three questions

- **Gap:** Which priority need has no place?
- **Duplication:** Which sections explain the same thing without a reason?
- **Drift:** Which section no longer supports the purpose or falls outside scope?

Use a simple matrix:

| User need / acceptance criterion | Outline section | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Need A] | [Section 1] | Covered / Partial / Missing | [Evidence or gap] |
| [Need B] | [Section 2] | Covered / Partial / Missing | [Evidence or gap] |
| [Need C] | — | Missing | [Research needed or reason for exclusion] |

### 8.3 Do not include everything you know

Authors are often tempted to include every interesting detail. Content is not a storage unit for all organizational knowledge.

Keep information when it:
- meets a priority need;
- provides a prerequisite for understanding;
- reduces the risk of misunderstanding;
- supports a claim or decision;
- provides genuinely necessary context.

Move it to another asset or remove it when it has no clear function.

## 9. Choose Structures for Different Content Formats

The principles of information development remain the same, but the outline should fit how the information will be used.

| Format | Useful outline form | Main focus |
|---|---|---|
| Article / guide | Substantive sections and subsections | Need sequence, explanation, evidence |
| Tutorial | Stages, prerequisites, actions, outcomes, recovery | Task success and failure conditions |
| Comparison | Criteria, options, trade-offs, suitability | Consistent comparison |
| Case study | Context, problem, approach, outcome evidence, limitations | Provenance and outcome context |
| Video | Storyboard, beats, visuals, narration, transitions | Understandable audiovisual flow |
| Infographic | Core message, visual sequence, labels, data sources | Accurate representation and readability |
| FAQ | Real questions, concise answers, references for detail | Distinct, non-repetitive needs |
| Product page | User problem, function, specifications, limits, evidence, action | Information needed for evaluation |

Do not turn every format into a long article just because the editorial workflow is accustomed to articles. Choose a form that communicates effectively and can be maintained.

## 10. Visuals, Tables, and Supporting Components

During outlining, decide whether information would be clearer through:
- comparison tables;
- process diagrams;
- mechanism illustrations;
- charts or data visualizations;
- screenshots or demonstrations;
- video or audio;
- calculators or interactive components;
- checklists or templates.

For each component, specify:
- the reader need it serves;
- the message it must communicate;
- the data or sources used;
- accompanying caption or explanation;
- accessibility needs and text alternatives;
- owner and production requirements.

Visuals are not decoration to fill empty space. If a diagram adds no understanding, do not produce it just to make the page look busy. Implementation requirements, alt text, responsive behavior, and image delivery remain with the relevant technical owners.

## 11. Review the Outline Before Drafting

Reviewing an outline costs less than rebuilding a full draft. Use this checklist:

| Dimension | Review question |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Does the structure still serve the brief's purpose? |
| User needs | Do priority needs have a place and a sensible sequence? |
| Completeness | Are prerequisites, core answers, evidence, and important limits covered? |
| Logic | Does the reader get context before dependent concepts? |
| Flow | Are transitions natural, without unexplained jumps? |
| Evidence | Do major claims have sources or a verification plan? |
| Balance | Is section depth proportional to its importance? |
| Duplication | Can repeated information be removed or linked? |
| Scope | Does any section fall outside the brief? |
| Format | Does the structure fit the format and how readers use the information? |
| Risk | Do sensitive sections have appropriate SMEs or review? |
| Accessibility | Can the sequence and information be understood without relying on visuals alone? |

Peer review can examine structure, flow, user needs, and consistency. Stakeholder/SME review should focus on subject accuracy, missing information, and misleading claims. ONS distinguishes peer review focused on structure and flow from stakeholder review emphasizing fact-checking. [1]

## 12. Handoff from Outline to Drafting

The outline is ready for handoff when:
- purpose, audience, and scope remain clear;
- every priority need is mapped;
- the information sequence has a defensible rationale;
- major claims have sources or a research plan;
- open questions have owners;
- required examples and visuals are identified;
- duplication and out-of-scope material are addressed;
- relevant reviewers have provided input;
- material changes to the brief are approved.

The handoff may include:
1. final outline version;
2. user-need-to-outline mapping matrix;
3. source/evidence pack;
4. open questions and SME list;
5. visual/deliverable list;
6. risk notes and editorial decisions.

08.6 takes over drafting and production. 08.5 does not need to dictate every sentence or lock all wording before writing begins.

## 13. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** a guide helping a small business choose an inventory-recording method.

**User need:** the owner needs to compare stock-recording methods to choose an approach suited to the scale and operations of the business.

**Prioritized information groups:**
1. When the current recording method becomes inadequate.
2. Differences between manual records, spreadsheets, and software.
3. Decision criteria: SKU volume, number of locations, transaction frequency, integrations, cost, and control needs.
4. Trade-offs and situations where each option is unsuitable.
5. Evaluation steps before choosing.

**Substantive outline:**

| Order | Section function | Question answered | Evidence / asset |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Decision orientation | What should be considered before choosing a method? | Criteria summary |
| 2 | Understand business conditions | When does simple recordkeeping start causing problems? | Process examples and operational indicators |
| 3 | Compare approaches | How do manual methods, spreadsheets, and software differ? | Comparison table with feature/cost sources |
| 4 | Evaluate needs | Which criteria matter most for this business? | Decision checklist |
| 5 | Understand trade-offs | What limitations come with each approach? | Scenario examples and limitations |
| 6 | Take the next step | What information should be collected before choosing? | Vendor/process evaluation list |

This structure prioritizes the reader's decision, not the history of inventory technology. Vendor configuration details are excluded because they fall outside the brief's scope.

## 14. Common Outlining Mistakes

1. **Building the outline from keywords alone.** Queries provide context but do not replace needs, evidence, and purpose.
2. **Making the outline only a heading list.** No section function, message, evidence, or relationship is defined.
3. **Following the author's research sequence.** Readers are forced to follow the author's notes rather than the logic of understanding.
4. **Giving every question equal weight.** Critical information gets buried among optional details.
5. **Stacking topics without an argument.** Many subjects appear, but no explanation connects them.
6. **Claims without an evidence plan.** Drafting starts before anyone notices the proof is missing.
7. **Passing illustrative examples off as facts.** Readers cannot distinguish hypothetical scenarios from real experience.
8. **Treating duplication as coverage.** The same information is repeated in different words.
9. **Forcing every format into an article.** Production habits override communication needs.
10. **Locking the outline too early.** New evidence cannot improve the structure because the outline is treated as an inflexible contract.
11. **Turning the outline into an HTML specification.** Editorial information structure is confused with Domain 07 heading implementation.
12. **Rebuilding the semantic topic map.** The team takes over Domain 09 instead of using its handoff.

## Key Takeaways

- An outline is an information-structure model, not merely a heading list.
- Start with user needs, acceptance criteria, scope, and evidence from the brief.
- Group needs by function and prioritize them around the reader's task.
- Sequence information by importance and dependencies.
- Build explanations from claims, evidence, reasoning, limitations, and implications.
- Remove duplication and material that does not support the purpose.
- Choose a structure suited to the format; not every asset should be an article.
- Review the outline before drafting to find gaps and logic problems early.
- Domain 08.5 owns editorial structure; Domain 07 owns HTML headings; Domain 09 owns semantic architecture.
- A strong outline improves editorial clarity, not ranking guarantees.
