# 08.10 — Content Inventory & Editorial Auditing

## 1. Purpose and Scope

Established websites often face a basic problem: the organization does not really know what content it owns, where it lives, who owns it, or whether it should remain. New assets keep appearing, old assets are forgotten, similar pages accumulate, and the inventory spreadsheet has not been updated for ages—until a redesign exposes the mess.

A **content inventory** is a structured catalogue of content assets within a defined scope. It answers: *what do we have, where is it, who owns it, and what attributes describe it?*

An **editorial content audit** is a systematic evaluation of inventoried assets. It answers: *is this content accurate, relevant, useful, fit for reader needs, and still worth keeping?*

Nielsen Norman Group distinguishes an inventory—a list of assets and their attributes—from an audit, which assesses asset quality. They complement each other but are not the same activity. [2]

Domain 08.10 owns **portfolio-level inventory management and editorial evaluation**. It does not optimize one page; it structures the asset set, identifies cross-asset patterns and problems, and produces actionable recommendations.

### Ownership boundaries

- **08.9** owns operational post-publication maintenance: triggers, review cadence, updates, and lifecycle decisions for individual assets.
- **08.10** owns portfolio inventory and editorial auditing: asset coverage, catalogue structure, evaluation criteria, cross-asset findings, priorities, and recommendations.
- **08.8** owns execution of substantive revisions.
- **08.7** owns fact-checking, source verification, editorial QA, and approval of changes.
- **08.3** owns portfolio planning and priorities for new content production.
- **Domain 07** owns on-page element audits and HTML implementation.
- **Domain 10** owns technical audits and validation, including crawling, indexing, canonicalization, redirects, and delivery.
- **Domain 14** owns performance measurement methodology and outcome analysis. 08.10 may use analyzed results as inputs but does not take over the methodology.

## 2. Inventory vs Audit vs Maintenance

These activities are related, but they answer different questions and produce different outputs.

| Activity | Main question | Main output | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content inventory | What assets exist, and what are their identities and locations? | Asset catalogue and inventory metadata | 08.10 |
| Editorial content audit | What is the editorial quality, relevance, and condition of assets in scope? | Findings, diagnosis, priorities, recommendations | 08.10 |
| Content maintenance | What change should be made to an individual published asset, and when? | Per-asset lifecycle decisions and records | 08.9 |
| Content revision | How should existing content be substantively improved? | Revised content version | 08.8 |
| Editorial QA | Is a change or new version accurate and ready for release? | Review record and approval decision | 08.7 |
| Performance analysis | What happened to performance, and what interpretation is supported by data? | Performance analysis and report | Domain 14 |

An inventory is not automatically an audit. A list of 2,000 URLs does not prove that 2,000 pages are useful. Conversely, an audit without a sufficient inventory may miss assets, dependencies, or duplication outside the reviewed subset.

## 3. Inventory and Audit Objectives

Inventory and audit can help an organization:
- understand the size, types, scope, and distribution of its content assets;
- identify content without a clear owner or source;
- find duplicate, overlapping, conflicting, or fragmented content;
- assess whether assets still meet their purpose and reader needs;
- find outdated, inaccurate, incomplete, or confusing information;
- identify unmet information needs;
- support redesign, CMS migration, consolidation, restructuring, or governance changes;
- map dependencies between pages, documents, media, services, and user journeys;
- provide evidence for retain, update, revise, merge, archive, retire, or create decisions;
- allocate editorial capacity to the most important problems.

GOV.UK uses audits to discover content managed by an organization or related to its work, then monitor whether it remains useful and findable. Outcomes may include changes or retirement. [1] The Australian Public Service Academy treats auditing as collecting evidence, analyzing accuracy, relevance, quality, and performance, then turning findings into action. [3]

Inventory and audit are not administrative ends in themselves. If the result is only a beautiful spreadsheet that never informs a decision, the project has failed—albeit aesthetically.

## 4. Define Objective and Scope Before Collecting Data

Do not crawl an entire domain merely because a tool can. First decide which decisions the work should support.

### 4.1 Set the objective

Examples:
- prepare for a website migration;
- assess content in one product category;
- find assets without owners;
- identify topic duplication;
- evaluate content in one user journey;
- assess assets affected by a policy or product change;
- identify assets to retain, improve, consolidate, or retire.

The objective determines inventory attributes, audit criteria, required evidence, and stakeholders. The Australian Public Service Academy includes audit planning, stakeholder engagement, data collection, evaluation, and actioning findings in its audit process. [3]

### 4.2 Define scope boundaries

| Dimension | Example |
|---|---|
| Property | One domain, subdomain, application, or repository |
| Section | Blog, documentation, product category, help centre |
| Topic | All assets about a product, service, or problem |
| Journey | All content needed to complete a task |
| Format | HTML page, PDF, video, image, dataset, downloadable file |
| Audience | Customers, developers, partners, internal staff |
| Market/language | One locale or market group |
| Lifecycle | Active, archived, draft, or migration-bound assets |

Record what is **included and excluded**. If an audit covers only HTML pages discoverable by a crawler, do not call it an audit of all website content if PDFs, videos, gated content, applications, and microsites were excluded.

### 4.3 Choose an inventory unit

Use a consistent record unit:
- **Page-level:** one page or URL per record.
- **Asset-level:** one file or media item per record.
- **Component-level:** a reusable content module, when separately managed.
- **Content-item-level:** one content item published across multiple channels or URLs.

One content item may have multiple technical representations. Do not automatically count every URL as a unique editorial asset, or merge different assets merely because their titles look similar. Set deduplication rules before calculating totals.

## 5. Discover and Collect Assets

Use multiple sources to reduce blind spots.

| Source | Potential data | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| CMS export | ID, title, status, owner, author, created/modified dates, content type | May omit assets outside the CMS |
| Website crawler | Discoverable URLs, response status, title, canonical, links, selected structure | Does not automatically find hidden, gated, orphaned, or non-HTML content |
| XML sitemap | URLs listed in the sitemap | Not a complete inventory; includes only selected URLs |
| Search / internal site search | Pages discoverable through an index or search | Depends on search coverage and mechanism |
| File repository / DAM | PDFs, images, video, source files, and asset metadata | Naming and metadata may be inconsistent |
| Analytics/reporting exports | Usage or interaction data under system definitions | Does not independently explain quality or behavior |
| Search Console exports | Search performance data available in reports | Not an inventory of all assets; data coverage has limits |
| Editorial calendar / project records | Purpose, brief, author, owner, status, campaign context | May be out of sync with actual publication |
| SMEs / content owners | Context, dependencies, validity, ownership | Must be verified; stakeholder memory is not a substitute for evidence |
| User research / support feedback | Friction, questions, needs, pain points | Sample and context must be understood |

GOV.UK describes how search tools and CMS exports can help find content and attributes such as URL, title, organization, publication/update dates, content type, and status. Tool coverage has limits; for example, Govsearch does not show all content published through non-GOV.UK tools. [1] One source rarely supports a claim of complete coverage.

Nielsen Norman Group notes that automation can collect inventory data such as titles, dates, authors, and metadata, but audit assessment still requires human inspection of assets and their context. [2]

### 5.1 Coverage and reconciliation

After initial collection:
1. combine lists from relevant sources;
2. normalize URLs, IDs, asset types, and statuses;
3. identify duplicate records;
4. compare asset counts across sources;
5. flag assets found in only one source;
6. document inaccessible sources;
7. record estimated coverage and blind spots.

Do not call an inventory complete if important sources remain unchecked or coverage limitations are unexplained.

## 6. Content Inventory Structure

There is no mandatory schema for every organization. Choose fields based on the objective and the ability to keep data accurate.

### 6.1 Identity and location

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Content ID | Stable inventory identifier |
| Asset title/name | Team-recognized content name |
| Public URL / location | Public location or channel |
| Source system | CMS, DAM, repository, platform, or external owner |
| Asset type / format | Page, article, PDF, video, image, dataset, etc. |
| Content type | Guidance, tutorial, product page, reference, announcement, etc. |
| Language / locale | Language and market context |
| Parent / section | Relationship to a section or content group |
| Related asset IDs | Links to related or potentially duplicate assets |

### 6.2 Ownership and lifecycle

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Content owner | Party accountable for the asset |
| Author / creator | Creator or contributor |
| SME / source owner | Owner of facts or primary source |
| Editorial status | Draft, published, archived, retired, and relevant statuses |
| First published | First publication date, if known |
| Last modified | Last system-recorded change |
| Last editorial review | Last recorded editorial review |
| Next review / trigger | Review schedule or trigger under 08.9 |
| Version / record ID | Source version or record identifier |

A CMS “last modified” timestamp does not prove the content was editorially reviewed. Template, metadata, or migration changes may alter it without an editorial review. Record the meaning of each field according to its source system.

### 6.3 Editorial context

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Intended audience | Intended reader group |
| User need / task | Need or task served |
| Editorial purpose | Reason the asset is retained |
| Topic / subject | Main topic |
| Business/service owner | Unit accountable for business or service context |
| Source / evidence record | Brief, claim ledger, or primary-source location |
| Dependencies | Related products, policies, forms, tools, documents, or assets |

### 6.4 Optional diagnostic data

Add these only when the objective requires them and definitions are clear:
- traffic, clicks, impressions, conversions, engagement;
- response status, index status, canonical target;
- inbound/outbound link counts;
- accessibility findings;
- file size, media format, transcript/caption availability;
- duplication cluster or similarity reference;
- audit finding, action, priority, and confidence.

On-page metadata and technical crawl data may be stored as reference attributes, but evaluation and implementation remain with Domain 07 or Domain 10. Performance metrics retain Domain 14 definitions and analysis.

## 7. Editorial Audit: What Should Be Assessed?

Assess assets against relevant purposes and needs. Do not apply one generic checklist to every asset without considering format, audience, risk, or reader task.

### 7.1 Purpose and user need
- Is the asset's editorial purpose still clear?
- Can its audience and user task be identified?
- Does it actually help complete that task?
- Are important questions unanswered?
- Is the asset still needed, or does another resource now meet the need?

### 7.2 Accuracy and currency
- Are facts, instructions, data, and recommendations still correct?
- Are sources current and verifiable?
- Have products, services, policies, standards, or context changed?
- Are limitations, effective dates, and conditions explained?
- Do claims conflict with other assets?

The audit can flag issues and verification needs. Fact-checking material claims and approving corrections follow 08.7.

### 7.3 Completeness and usefulness
- Is the information needed for the task available?
- Are there gaps, missing steps, or unexplained context?
- Is the level of detail suitable for readers?
- Are examples, evidence, and explanations sufficient for the conclusions?
- Does the asset provide distinct value compared with other assets?

### 7.4 Clarity and editorial quality
- Is language clear for the intended audience?
- Is terminology consistent?
- Do structure and explanatory flow support understanding?
- Are title and content descriptions editorially accurate?
- Is the format suitable for the reader task?

HTML heading assessment, metadata, alt text, semantic markup, and page-element implementation belong to Domain 07. 08.10 may record indicators and route findings to the correct owner.

### 7.5 Duplication, overlap, and contradiction
- Do multiple assets serve the same need?
- Is duplication justified by different audiences or contexts?
- Do instructions or facts conflict?
- Is information fragmented so readers must navigate without a clear reason?
- Should one asset be the primary reference?

Similarity of wording or topic alone does not prove two assets should be merged. Compare user need, scope, audience, purpose, evidence, and use context.

### 7.6 Ownership and governance
- Is the owner active and appropriate?
- Are fact sources and competent validators known?
- Are publication status and review traceable?
- Does the content have dependencies or special approvals?
- Are records and access properly managed?

### 7.7 Accessibility and format suitability
- Is the content available in a format the audience can use?
- Is essential information available only in visuals or media without suitable alternatives?
- Do documents or media have required context, labels, captions, transcripts, or alternatives?
- Does the current format obstruct the reader's task?

Technical accessibility evaluation and standards implementation require competent owners. 08.10 identifies needs and routes findings; it does not replace technical or legal accessibility audits.

## 8. Evaluation Methods and Evidence

Combine quantitative and qualitative evidence. They answer different questions.

### 8.1 Quantitative evidence
Examples include asset counts by category, format, owner, or status; publication and review-date distributions; assets without owners; duplicate URLs or files; usage and performance data from analytics; and broken-reference or technical findings from related audits.

Numbers help identify patterns but do not automatically explain causes or quality.

### 8.2 Qualitative evidence
Examples include direct content inspection; user research and usability findings; customer-support questions; SME and content-owner review; user-journey analysis; and source/consistency checks across assets.

### 8.3 Do not create one magic score

There is no universal editorial score that objectively summarizes every dimension. Composite scores can conceal trade-offs: a high-traffic page containing false information is not safe because its average score looks good.

If an organization uses ratings:
- define criteria and evidence for each level;
- keep distinct dimensions separate;
- document rating rationale;
- mark unknown or not assessed;
- do not treat missing data as a poor score;
- do not compare formats without equivalent criteria;
- test reviewer consistency;
- do not describe an internal scale as an industry standard.

**Pass / Needs review / Action required / Not assessed** may be used as an internal example, not a universal standard. A critical high-risk finding must remain visible and must not be hidden by an average.

### 8.4 Confidence and limitations

Every material finding should include observed evidence; source data and collection date; reviewed scope; confidence; limitations; and the next verification action if evidence is insufficient.

Distinguish **confirmed finding**, **potential finding**, and **unknown**. Do not turn suspected duplication, inaccuracy, or uselessness into a final decision before verification.

## 9. Content Inventory and Audit Workflow

This SEO-Playbook framework can be adapted to portfolio size and complexity.

### Stage 1 — Define
- set the objective and decisions to support;
- define scope, inventory unit, and inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- identify stakeholders, owners, and reviewers;
- define fields and audit criteria;
- record source limitations early.

### Stage 2 — Discover
- collect lists from CMS, crawler, sitemap, file repository, search, and other relevant sources;
- request asset lists from content owners and SMEs;
- identify uncovered channels or systems;
- record source and collection time.

### Stage 3 — Normalize
- normalize IDs, URLs, formats, statuses, dates, and owners;
- set record-deduplication rules;
- connect editorially related assets;
- flag missing fields and conflicting data;
- reconcile sources to understand coverage.

### Stage 4 — Inventory
- populate required fields;
- mark unavailable fields as unknown rather than inventing values;
- verify owners and statuses with relevant parties;
- preserve data sources and collection dates;
- establish an inventory-update mechanism.

### Stage 5 — Audit
- select a subset based on objective and priority;
- assess it using relevant editorial criteria;
- collect quantitative and qualitative evidence;
- record findings, confidence, and limitations;
- escalate factual claims or risks requiring specialist verification.

### Stage 6 — Synthesize
Analyze asset- and portfolio-level results: individual problems; cross-asset patterns; duplication and contradiction; reader-need gaps; ownerless assets; high-risk or uncertain areas; dependencies and change impacts.

### Stage 7 — Recommend
Make recommendations for each asset or asset group. Every recommendation needs rationale, evidence, intended owner, priority, and dependencies.

### Stage 8 — Handoff and track
- send maintenance actions to 08.9;
- send substantive revisions to 08.8;
- send fact-checking and approval to 08.7;
- send new-content planning or portfolio priorities to 08.3;
- send page-element issues to Domain 07;
- send technical issues to Domain 10;
- send measurement needs to Domain 14;
- track resolution without taking over receiving domains' workflows.

The Australian Public Service Academy divides audit work into planning, stakeholder engagement, data collection, analysis/evaluation, and actioning findings. This supports the principle that an audit should lead to action, not end in a spreadsheet. [3]

## 10. Prioritization: Decide What to Review First

Not all assets can be assessed at once. Nielsen Norman Group recommends a phased approach and beginning with frequently accessed content or content supporting top tasks. [2] This is useful, but traffic is not the only priority criterion.

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| User impact | How important is the asset to the reader's task? |
| Risk | What are the consequences if information is wrong or missing? |
| Reach | How many readers may be affected? |
| Volatility | How quickly can the information change? |
| Dependency | How many assets, services, or journeys rely on it? |
| Evidence of issue | How strong is the evidence that the asset has a problem? |
| Strategic relevance | Does it still support a legitimate organizational goal? |
| Remediation effort | What capacity and expertise are needed to act? |

Internal priority model:
- **P0 — Immediate escalation:** critical risk requiring prompt action under governance.
- **P1 — High:** confirmed issue with major impact, important audience, or high dependency.
- **P2 — Medium:** worthwhile issue with more limited consequences.
- **P3 — Low / backlog:** minor improvement or low-urgency opportunity.
- **Unassessed:** insufficient evidence to assign priority.

Do not create a numeric formula that implies false precision. If scoring is used, explain weights, rationale, limitations, and critical overrides.

## 11. Editorial Recommendations and Action Taxonomy

Every audited asset should receive a clear recommendation or an unresolved status with an owner.

| Recommendation | When to use | Handoff |
|---|---|---|
| Retain | Accurate, relevant, useful, and has a clear purpose | 08.9 for review/lifecycle record if needed |
| Monitor / review | Uncertainty or a trigger needs checking | 08.9 |
| Update | Limited information needs changing without major substantive revision | 08.9 maintenance workflow |
| Revise | Explanation, evidence, scope, or structure needs substantive improvement | 08.8, then 08.7 QA |
| Merge / consolidate | Assets overlap or conflict and one editorial destination better serves the need | 08.9 lifecycle decision; 08.8 revision; Domain 10 technical migration |
| Archive / preserve | Historical, reference, or record value remains | 08.9 and governance owner |
| Retire / remove | No valid need remains or there is a strong reason to stop publication | 08.9 lifecycle decision; Domain 10 technical implementation |
| Create new content | A valid need is unmet and should not be added to an existing asset | 08.3 portfolio planning |
| Escalate / investigate | Evidence, ownership, or risk remains unclear | Relevant owner/SME/governance |

An audit recommendation is not approval to delete a page, change a URL, or merge content. Final decisions must follow ownership and governance.

## 12. Manage Duplication Findings and Content Gaps

### 12.1 Content overlap is not automatically duplication

Two pages about the same keyword or topic may serve different functions because audience, task, context, or level of detail differs.

Before declaring duplication:
1. compare intended user needs;
2. compare audience and context;
3. inspect scope and depth;
4. compare evidence and recommendations;
5. inspect relationships between pages;
6. determine whether duplication helps navigation or creates confusion;
7. ask the relevant owner to confirm each asset's function.

### 12.2 Content gaps

A gap is a reader need or question not adequately met by assets in scope. It does not automatically mean a new page is needed.

First check whether the information already exists elsewhere; whether the problem is discoverability or internal linking; whether it can be added to an existing page; whether the need is valid and evidence-based; and whether it belongs to a different audience, market, or product.

If a new asset is justified, recommend it to Domain 08.3. Keyword research and intent validation remain with Domain 05; semantic architecture remains with Domain 09.

## 14. Governance, Data Quality, and Inventory Maintenance

An inventory can become outdated too. Define:
- inventory owner and audit-process owner;
- authoritative data source for each field;
- identifier and deduplication rules;
- who may read and edit records;
- how conflicting or unknown data is handled;
- when inventory is synchronized with CMS or other sources;
- how new, changed, and retired assets are recorded;
- backup, retention, and access control under policy.

Nielsen Norman Group emphasizes ownership, stakeholder collaboration, tools accessible to the team, and ongoing inventory/audit maintenance. [2] It also describes inventory and audit as continuous work rather than a one-time activity. [2]

Do not place personal data, credentials, confidential information, or sensitive data in an inventory without a need, proper authority, and suitable controls. An inventory should support asset management, not become an uncontrolled copy of internal data.

## 15. Common Inventory and Audit Failures

1. **Treating a crawl export as a complete inventory.** A crawler only finds assets reachable under its configuration and access.
2. **Treating inventory as audit.** Knowing URLs and dates says nothing by itself about content quality.
3. **Undefined scope.** Results cannot be interpreted because coverage is unclear.
4. **Assuming one URL equals one asset.** Technical representation and editorial unit may differ.
5. **Inferring duplication from similar wording.** Audience, task, and asset function are not checked.
6. **Applying identical criteria to all assets.** Different formats and purposes need appropriate criteria.
7. **Using traffic as the only quality measure.** Important assets may have low volume; high traffic does not prove accuracy.
8. **Treating composite scores as objective.** Averages can hide critical risks.
9. **Treating missing data as a poor score.** Unknown must remain unknown.
10. **Recommendations without owners.** The audit ends in a spreadsheet and action never starts.
11. **Auditing once and abandoning the result.** Inventory and findings become stale without maintenance.
12. **Editorial team taking over technical findings.** Domains 07 and 10 have their own canonical ownership.
13. **Claiming ranking impact from an audit.** Editorial findings do not prove causal effects on rankings or traffic.
14. **Trying to audit everything at once.** Start with consequential, manageable scope and expand deliberately.

## 16. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** an organization has a help centre with 420 HTML pages, 35 PDFs, 18 tutorial videos, and product pages managed by different teams.

### Objective
Assess content supporting onboarding and account-configuration tasks before a help-centre redesign.

### Scope
- onboarding and account setup;
- related HTML pages, PDFs, and videos;
- assets from two CMSs and one file repository;
- internal-only content and training materials excluded and recorded as such.

### Inventory
The team combines CMS exports, a crawler, repository file lists, and content-owner input. Each record receives a Content ID. Duplicate URLs and asset representations are separated under the inventory-unit rules. One vendor microsite is inaccessible, so coverage is not claimed to be complete.

### Audit
Findings include:
- several guides refer to an old menu;
- two pages describe the same task, but one has different prerequisites;
- some PDFs have no active owner;
- a tutorial video remains relevant but its captions need checking;
- one onboarding question is not answered by any asset.

### Recommendations

| Finding | Recommendation | Handoff |
|---|---|---|
| Outdated UI steps | Update | 08.9, then 08.7 QA |
| Two overlapping pages | Investigate audience/task; propose consolidation if redundancy is confirmed | 08.9 + 08.8 + Domain 10 if URL changes |
| PDF without owner | Find owner and decide status | 08.2 / 08.9 |
| Inadequate video captions | Further accessibility review and asset correction | Media owner / relevant specialist |
| Onboarding need gap | Validate need and prioritize new content | Domain 05 for research input; 08.3 for planning |

### Outcome
The team gains an asset map, editorial findings, dependencies, coverage limitations, and an owner-assigned worklist. The audit does not claim that every problem is solved or rankings have increased. Audit-stage success is measured by evidence quality and actionable decisions; completion is tracked by each receiving owner.

## Key Takeaways

- A content inventory records what exists; an editorial audit evaluates quality and usefulness.
- Define objective, scope, asset unit, and exclusions before collecting data.
- Combine data sources; one crawler, CMS, or sitemap does not cover the entire digital estate automatically.
- Separate inventory attributes from editorial judgments.
- Assess purpose, user need, accuracy, completeness, clarity, duplication, ownership, and format suitability.
- Use quantitative and qualitative evidence; do not invent a magic score.
- Explicitly mark unknowns, confidence, coverage, and limitations.
- Audits must produce recommendations, owners, priorities, and handoffs.
- 08.10 diagnoses the portfolio; 08.9 manages per-asset lifecycle; 08.8 performs substantive revision.
- An inventory is a maintained working system, not a one-time spreadsheet.
- Editorial auditing does not guarantee rankings or organic growth.
