# 08.4 — Content Briefs & Editorial Specifications

A content brief is not a formality authors fill out before they start typing. It is an **editorial working agreement**: it explains the problem to solve, who the work is for, what needs to be communicated, what evidence is required, and what an acceptable result looks like.

A document containing only a keyword, target word count, and a few headings is not a mature brief. It is a list of instructions that never explains why the content needs to exist.

This document covers how to develop briefs and editorial specifications from work approved in [08.3 — Content Planning & Portfolio Management](08.3-Content-Planning-and-Portfolio-Management.en.md). Keyword research and keyword-to-page mapping remain owned by Domain 05.

## 1. What Is a Content Brief?

A **content brief** is an assignment document for one content asset or unit of editorial work. It brings together strategic context, audience needs, scope, information requirements, evidence requirements, and production constraints so everyone involved shares the same understanding.

An **editorial specification** is a more operational set of requirements for producing and evaluating that asset. It may cover format, coverage, sources that must be checked, claim boundaries, contributor or subject-matter-expert (SME) needs, accessibility, deliverables, and acceptance criteria.

They complement each other:
- The brief answers **why, for whom, and what must be achieved**.
- The specification answers **which requirements the work must satisfy**.
- Acceptance criteria answer **how the team will know the work meets the brief**.

There is no universal template. The depth of a brief should reflect the work's complexity, risk, format, and uncertainty.

## 2. Where the Brief Fits in the Editorial Workflow

The brief connects portfolio decisions to information development and production.

1. **08.2 — Strategy & Governance:** establishes direction, objectives, standards, authority, and accountability.
2. **08.3 — Planning & Portfolio:** selects editorial work and sets priorities, owners, capacity, and timing.
3. **08.4 — Brief & Specification:** turns selected work into actionable direction and acceptance criteria.
4. **08.5 — Outlining & Information Development:** develops the substantive sequence of information, explanations, and arguments.
5. **08.6 — Production Workflow:** creates the draft and assets according to the approved brief.
6. **08.7 — Editorial Review & QA:** checks accuracy, sources, consistency, risk, and publication readiness.

This sequence is not mandatory bureaucracy for every asset. Simple work can combine steps; high-risk work needs clearer separation and tighter review. What matters is that decisions and responsibilities remain explicit.

GOV.UK guidance treats user needs as the starting point for content design and asks teams to consider the most appropriate quantity, format, and publication location. This supports briefs that begin with user needs rather than a format or keyword decided in advance. [1]

## 3. Inputs a Brief Should Carry Forward

A brief should not invent strategy from scratch. It receives available inputs and identifies what still needs validation.

| Input | What is needed | Source / owner |
|---|---|---|
| Editorial mandate | Organizational rationale, objective, policy boundaries | 08.2 and content owner |
| Portfolio decision | Work type, priority, owner, timing, dependencies | 08.3 |
| Audience and user task | Intended audience, problem or task to solve | User research, first-party evidence, SME |
| Keyword and page mapping | Query cluster, intent hypothesis, target page or page recommendation | Domain 05 |
| Existing content context | Related pages, existing material, potential duplication or conflict | Content inventory / content owner |
| Semantic requirements | Relevant entities, relationships, or topical architecture, where available | [Domain 09 — Semantic SEO](../09-Semantic-SEO/README.md) |
| Technical constraints | CMS, supported formats, asset requirements, implementation limits | Technical team / Domain 10 |
| Performance evidence | Relevant performance observations, with context and limitations | Domain 14 when available |
| Governance requirements | Reviewers, approvers, standards, risks, escalation route | 08.2 |

Do not treat every input as a validated fact. Record its origin, date, context, confidence level, and who must confirm it. GOV.UK recommends deriving user needs from evidence such as research, analytics, previous reports, and service feedback—not merely internal assumptions. [2]

## 4. Core Components of a Content Brief

### 4.1 Work identity and status

Record information that identifies the work:
- Brief ID or content ID.
- Provisional working title.
- Content owner and production lead.
- Status: draft, review, approved, in production, paused, or cancelled.
- Version and update date.
- Links to the portfolio item, research documents, and existing assets.

A working title is not a final decision about the title tag or page headline. Page-element implementation belongs to Domain 07.

### 4.2 Purpose and intended outcomes

State:
- Why the asset needs to be created or revised.
- The problem or need it addresses.
- The expected audience outcome.
- Relevant organizational outcome.
- Why content is a reasonable solution compared with other options.

Separate the **audience outcome** from the **business outcome**. For example, the audience may be able to compare options independently, while the organization hopes to reduce repetitive support questions. The relationship between these outcomes is a hypothesis to evaluate, not an automatic result.

Do not make “rank number one” or “increase traffic” the sole reason content exists. Those objectives do not explain the need being served and do not guarantee search visibility.

### 4.3 Audience and user task

Describe the audience specifically enough to guide editorial decisions:
- Who they are in the context of this task.
- What they are trying to do.
- Relevant prior knowledge or context.
- Obstacles, questions, or risks of misunderstanding.
- Conditions that affect how they use the information.

Avoid decorative personas that do not change content decisions. If segmentation is not supported by research, label it as an assumption.

A user need can be framed as:
- As a [relevant audience],
- I need to [task or information],
- So that [desired outcome].

This is a practical format used in GOV.UK guidance, not a mandatory format for every organization or content type. [2]

### 4.4 Needs and acceptance criteria

Turn abstract objectives into checkable conditions.

Illustrative example:

**User need:** An online store owner needs to compare shipping methods to choose an option that fits their costs and operational requirements.

**Acceptance criteria:**
- The main differences between methods are explained.
- Relevant costs and limitations include sources and context.
- Conditions that make an option unsuitable are explained.
- Readers can find an appropriate next step.

Acceptance criteria test whether the need has been served; they should not force the author into one predetermined structure. ONS uses acceptance criteria to clarify when a user need has been met. [3]

### 4.5 Scope, boundaries, and exclusions

Explicitly define:
- Topics and questions that must be covered.
- Required depth.
- What is deliberately out of scope.
- Special conditions or cases that need mention.
- Geographic, language, time, product, or regulatory boundaries where relevant.
- Relationships with other pages or assets.

Exclusions matter as much as coverage. Without boundaries, a brief can expand into an impromptu encyclopedia.

If a need belongs in another asset, record it as a dependency or separate recommendation. Do not force every need into one page.

### 4.6 Key questions and information requirements

List the questions the content must answer. Group them around reader needs, not merely keyword order.

For each important question, specify:
- Why it matters.
- The minimum information or explanation required.
- Facts, data, or examples needed.
- Whether the answer is already known or requires research.
- Which SME or source can validate it.

This list is an input to 08.5. The brief does not need to lock every heading, argument, or final structure.

### 4.7 Research, evidence, and source requirements

Set evidence standards before production begins:
- Which claims require primary sources.
- Which data need dates, populations, methods, and context.
- Source-authority standards for sensitive or technical topics.
- Whether interviews, experiments, internal data, or first-hand experience are required.
- How attribution, quotations, and permissions will be handled.
- What to do if evidence is unavailable or conflicting.

Do not write “use reliable sources” and hope everyone shares the same definition. Specify source types and verification requirements.

For content involving legal, medical, financial, safety, or other high-risk claims, assign competent reviewers and an escalation route. A brief is not a substitute for professional advice or legal review.

### 4.8 Format and deliverables

Choose a format based on user needs and the nature of the information, for example:
- Article or guide.
- Product or category page.
- FAQ that genuinely answers distinct user needs.
- Video, diagram, calculator, dataset, or visual asset.
- A combination of formats if one format is insufficient.

Record the deliverables to be handed over: draft, supporting data, source list, visuals, transcript, working files, or validation notes. Format is not an automatic SEO decision. GOV.UK explicitly asks teams to consider the quantity, format, and best place to publish content. [1]

### 4.9 Voice, terminology, accessibility, and constraints

Connect the brief to the organization's editorial standards:
- Tone and language style.
- Terms that must be consistent or explained.
- Language and localization needs.
- Accessibility requirements relevant to the format.
- Legal, privacy, brand, security, or licensing constraints.
- CMS, design, and production limitations.

Do not copy the entire style guide into every brief. Link to the canonical standard and record work-specific exceptions.

### 4.10 Ownership, review, and approval

Specify who will:
- Write or produce the asset.
- Provide subject-matter expertise (SME).
- Verify facts and sources.
- Conduct editorial review.
- Handle legal/compliance or risk review where needed.
- Give final approval and authorize publication.

One person may hold multiple roles in a small team, but responsibilities must remain explicit. Editorial approval does not automatically replace expert validation or publication authorization.

### 4.11 Constraints, dependencies, and open questions

Record factors that may affect delivery:
- Deadlines with a genuine rationale.
- Capacity and SME availability.
- Missing data, access, permissions, or assets.
- Dependencies on products, policy, engineering, or external parties.
- Unanswered questions.
- Assumptions and risks that could change scope.

Do not disguise uncertainty as an instruction. Mark open questions with an owner and decision date.

## 5. Editorial Specification: From Brief to Work Requirements

For complex work, supplement the brief with a more detailed specification. It may include:

| Area | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Content type | What asset will be produced, and why was this format chosen? |
| Coverage | What minimum information must be included? |
| Evidence | Which claims and data must be verified? |
| Original contribution | What analysis, data, demonstration, or experience genuinely adds value? |
| Terminology | Which terms and definitions must remain consistent? |
| Assets | Which visuals, tables, data, quotations, or supporting files are needed? |
| Accessibility | Which accessibility requirements apply to this format and audience? |
| Governance | Who are the reviewers, approvers, and escalation owners? |
| Deliverables | Which files and supporting evidence must be handed over? |
| Acceptance | What conditions must be met before the work is accepted? |

A separate specification is not necessary for every assignment. Use one document for simple work; separate it when complexity, contributor count, risk, or dependencies make the brief difficult to use.

## 6. Writing Testable Acceptance Criteria

Useful acceptance criteria are:
- Specific to the needs and risks of the work.
- Checkable by a reviewer.
- Descriptions of the resulting condition, not merely an activity.
- Not prematurely locked to one solution when several approaches are valid.
- Inclusive of completeness, accuracy, evidence traceability, and relevant constraints.

Weak examples:
- “The article must be high quality.”
- “The content must be SEO-friendly.”
- “Cover everything comprehensively.”
- “At least 2,000 words.”

More operational examples:
- “Every price claim identifies its source, region, and verification date.”
- “The content distinguishes mandatory requirements from optional recommendations.”
- “Every priority question in the brief is answered or explicitly identified as currently unanswerable.”
- “Examples do not expose customer data and can be traced to an authorized source.”

Word count can be used as a production constraint when there is a clear format or editorial reason. It is not a substitute for acceptance criteria and is not an automatic measure of quality or ranking potential.

## 7. Set Brief Depth According to Risk

Not every asset needs the same process.

| Work level | Example conditions | Reasonable brief depth |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Small update, stable facts, low risk | Purpose, requested change, sources, reviewer, concise acceptance criteria |
| Medium | New guide, comparison, or content using several sources | User need, scope, key questions, evidence plan, format, dependencies, review |
| High | Sensitive claims, complex data, multiple SMEs, significant consequences if wrong | All relevant components, source protocol, risk review, expert validation, approval gates, change log |

This is an illustrative operating model, not a universal standard. Organizations should define risk categories based on consequences of error, uncertainty, sensitivity, and applicable obligations.

## 8. Brief Development and Approval Process

1. **Take an approved portfolio item.** Confirm the work, owner, priority, and capacity.
2. **Gather canonical inputs.** Bring together strategy, audience needs, Domain 05 research, existing-asset context, and governance requirements.
3. **Validate the user need.** Separate evidence, interpretation, and assumptions; conduct additional research if a material gap remains.
4. **Define purpose and outcomes.** Keep audience needs distinct from organizational objectives.
5. **Set scope and exclusions.** Prevent duplication and scope creep.
6. **Define key questions and evidence requirements.** Specify what must be answered and how claims will be verified.
7. **Choose format and deliverables.** Select based on need, not format trends.
8. **Set acceptance criteria, owners, reviewers, and dependencies.**
9. **Review and approve the brief.** Involve people with the appropriate authority under 08.2 governance.
10. **Hand off to 08.5 or 08.6.** The approved brief becomes the basis for information development and production.

ONS describes a user-needs-to-content process that includes validating needs, setting acceptance criteria, creating an outline, writing, and review. This is an example of structured content design, not an identical mandatory workflow for every organization. [3]

## 9. Managing Brief Changes

A brief may change when new research, data, or organizational conditions alter the initial understanding. Change is not automatically bad; undocumented change is what makes teams lose track.

Record:
- The changed section and previous version.
- The reason and supporting evidence.
- Impact on scope, effort, deadline, and dependencies.
- Who approved the change.
- Any acceptance criteria that changed.
- Whether the work must return to the portfolio for reprioritization.

A change that materially alters the objective, primary audience, or editorial solution should trigger a review of the 08.3 decision. Do not smuggle a new project into an old brief.

## 11. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** a software company wants to create a guide helping small businesses choose an inventory-recording system.

**Purpose:** help prospective users compare inventory-recording approaches based on operational complexity, cost, and integration needs.

**Audience:** owners or operators of small businesses currently tracking stock manually or in spreadsheets.

**User need:** “I need to compare ways of managing inventory so I can choose a process that fits the size and operations of my business.”

**Scope:**
- Differences between manual records, spreadsheets, and inventory software.
- Operating conditions suited to each approach.
- Integration needs and costs to investigate.
- Limitations and trade-offs of each option.

**Exclusions:**
- Claims that one product is always best.
- Price comparisons that cannot be verified.
- In-depth configuration tutorials for every vendor.

**Evidence requirements:**
- Vendor features and prices are checked against official documentation on the verification date.
- Claims about business practices are supported by relevant research or sources.
- Every cost estimate states its assumptions transparently.

**Acceptance criteria:**
- The three approaches are distinguished using consistent criteria.
- Advantages, limitations, and suitable use cases are explained.
- Readers can identify questions to ask before choosing.
- No vendor claim is untraceable.

This example demonstrates a brief format; it is not a product recommendation, market study, or claim that the content will improve rankings.

## 12. Common Content Briefing Mistakes

1. **Starting with the keyword.** Keyword research is an input, not a replacement for user need and editorial purpose.
2. **Choosing the format before understanding the problem.** The team decides to make a video or article before establishing whether that format fits.
3. **Using a generic audience description.** “Anyone who is interested” does not help authors make decisions.
4. **Leaving scope without exclusions.** The brief turns into a list of everything that might be relevant.
5. **Leaving evidence requirements vague.** “Find reliable sources” provides no verification standard.
6. **Using subjective acceptance criteria.** “Must be engaging and high quality” cannot be checked consistently.
7. **Treating word count as a quality target.** Length does not prove the need has been met.
8. **Asking SMEs to approve only at the end.** Important knowledge surfaces after production is finished.
9. **Leaving approval authority unclear.** There are many reviewers but no decision owner.
10. **Failing to maintain the brief.** Facts or scope change without versioning and a change log.

## Key Takeaways

- A brief translates a portfolio decision into actionable editorial direction.
- Start with audience needs and clear objectives, not keywords or format.
- Separate facts, interpretations, assumptions, and unanswered questions.
- Scope must state both what is covered and what is deliberately excluded.
- Set evidence requirements, reviewers, dependencies, deliverables, and acceptance criteria early.
- Brief depth should reflect complexity and risk, not a one-size-fits-all template.
- Material changes need a reason, owner, approval, and version history.
- A good brief reduces ambiguity; it does not guarantee rankings or organic performance.
