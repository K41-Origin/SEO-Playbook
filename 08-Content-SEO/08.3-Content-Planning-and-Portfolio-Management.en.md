# 08.3 — Content Planning & Portfolio Management

Content planning is not a contest to fill a calendar. It is the process of deciding **which editorial work is worth doing, when it should happen, who should do it, and what capacity it requires**.

When every idea goes straight into the backlog, priorities are set by whoever makes the most noise—not by audience needs or strategy. The usual result is a packed calendar, an overloaded team, and important assets left unattended.

This topic explains how to turn the strategic direction and governance established in [08.2](08.2-Content-Strategy-and-Governance.md), together with approved research inputs, into a realistic editorial work portfolio.

## 1. What Is Content Planning?

**Content planning** is the process of translating organizational goals, audience needs, organizational context, and available evidence into editorial work decisions: what should be created or addressed, why it has priority, the order of work, ownership, and estimated capacity.

A **content portfolio** is the collection of content assets that already exist, are in progress, have been approved for work, or have been proposed—managed as one decision system rather than as an unrelated list of URLs.

**Portfolio management** is the practice of keeping that portfolio relevant and balanced: selecting work, postponing or rejecting proposals, managing dependencies, adjusting resource allocation, and revisiting decisions when circumstances change.

The definitions and operating model in this document are SEO-Playbook's working framework. They are not universal standards or an official scoring methodology.

Planning decides **which work enters the portfolio and when**. A brief defines **what an individual asset must accomplish**. An outline defines **how its information will be developed**. Do not mash all three into one giant document.

## 3. Inputs: Where Should Content Plans Come From?

A sound plan combines several kinds of input. Keyword research matters, but it is not the only source of ideas.

### 3.1 Strategic direction

Use the strategy and governance established in 08.2 as decision boundaries:

- organizational goals and the outcomes the work should support;
- the audiences and needs the organization intends to serve;
- editorial scope and topics the organization will not cover;
- quality principles, risks, and standards;
- decision owners and the people who need to be involved.

A proposal does not become worthwhile merely because it looks attractive if it conflicts with the organization's mandate or capabilities.

### 3.2 Audience and user evidence

Inputs may come from user research, customer questions, support conversations, feedback, field studies, interviews, and other relevant evidence. The Office for National Statistics emphasizes that user needs should be evidence-based rather than assumed; it also recommends removing duplicate or unsupported needs before prioritization. [1]

Record the source, context, date, represented user groups, and evidence limitations. One customer comment can be a signal worth investigating, but it does not necessarily represent the entire audience.

### 3.3 Keyword and search research handoff

Use validated outputs from Domain 05, especially:

- clusters and search needs from 05.7;
- opportunity decisions, priorities, rationale, and feasibility boundaries from 05.8;
- mapping of clusters to existing or proposed pages from 05.9.

Domain 05 retains ownership of keyword discovery, intent analysis, clustering, opportunity scoring, and keyword-to-page mapping. Domain 08 does not repeat that work. Planning receives those outputs as one input, then decides whether the editorial work belongs in the portfolio, when it should happen, and whether resources are available. [2]

Do not automatically convert keyword priority into editorial priority. A cluster may rank highly in research but still be unready for production because no subject-matter expert (SME) is available, data is missing, a product has not launched, or the organization cannot provide a credible answer.

### 3.4 Existing content and portfolio evidence

Existing content also affects planning decisions. Before proposing a new asset, check whether the need is already served by an internal asset or a more appropriate source. GOV.UK's content-transition guidance combines content-use audits, user needs, and content planning to decide what should be published, omitted, or treated as a gap. [3]

Detailed portfolio inspection and editorial-audit methods belong in 08.10. In 08.3, inventory data is used only to support planning decisions: whether to create a new asset, improve, consolidate, defer, or take no action.

### 3.5 Organizational and operational constraints

Bring real constraints into the process early:

- product, service, policy, or campaign priorities;
- SME and reviewer availability;
- access to data, research, legal/compliance input, and external sources;
- budget, team capacity, vendors, and tools;
- design, engineering, translation, or approval dependencies;
- immovable external dates.

Content strategy should connect user needs with organizational goals and account for cross-functional collaborators. [4]

## 4. Content Portfolio: Managing Assets as One System

A portfolio is more than a list of articles. It is a view of editorial work and assets that helps a team understand coverage, gaps, dependencies, and trade-offs.

### 4.1 Portfolio work types

Use work categories that match the decisions the team needs to make:

| Work type | When to use it | Example decision |
|---|---|---|
| New asset | An important need is not served by a suitable asset | Propose a new guide |
| Improve existing | An asset exists but does not adequately support the reader's task | Add a substantive revision |
| Update / maintain | Facts, products, policies, or procedures have changed | Schedule a targeted update |
| Consolidate / reposition | Several assets overlap in purpose or have confusing roles | Request an evaluation of consolidation or repositioning |
| Retire / withdraw | An asset is no longer needed or cannot responsibly be maintained | Propose retirement after impact checks |
| Research / validation | A decision lacks sufficient evidence or input | Defer production and validate first |
| No editorial action | The need is already served or outside the organization's mandate | Record why no asset should be created |

These categories are planning labels, not technical instructions to change URLs, canonicals, redirects, or indexation. Technical implementation remains in Domain 10; internal-link strategy remains in Domain 11.

### 4.2 Portfolio dimensions

Each item should be reviewable against at least these dimensions:

- **Audience need:** whose task or problem is the work intended to serve?
- **Strategic fit:** which organizational goal does it support?
- **Editorial contribution:** what value or information can the organization provide?
- **Asset relationship:** is this a new asset, an existing asset, or part of a sequence of work?
- **Priority rationale:** why should this work happen before other work?
- **Readiness:** are the inputs, SME, data, and decisions available?
- **Effort and capacity:** who will do the work, and how much capacity will it require?
- **Dependencies and risk:** what must happen first, and what happens if it is late?
- **Owner and decision authority:** who is accountable, and who approves?
- **Review trigger:** what condition should cause the decision to be revisited?

Not every organization needs dozens of fields. Keep the information that improves decisions and handoffs; do not turn a spreadsheet into a monument to bureaucracy.

## 5. Prioritizing Editorial Work

Editorial priority is a capacity-allocation decision. It is not a repeat of keyword opportunity scoring in 05.8.

### 5.1 Separate value, urgency, readiness, and effort

Assess these dimensions separately.

**Value**
- How important is the audience need?
- Does the work support a strategic goal?
- What is the impact if the need remains unmet?
- Can the organization make a credible contribution?

**Urgency**
- Is there a product, policy, regulatory, or release-date change?
- Could the current information mislead or harm users?
- Is there a genuine external deadline?

**Readiness**
- Are scope and ownership clear?
- Are SMEs, evidence, data, and approvals available?
- Are the major dependencies ready?

**Effort and opportunity cost**
- How much editorial and specialist capacity is required?
- Which work must be delayed if this item is selected?
- Is there a simpler way to meet the need without creating a new asset?

High-value work is not necessarily ready. Urgent work does not necessarily require a new article. Easy work is not necessarily important.

### 5.2 A practical decision matrix

The following matrix is a discussion aid, not a universal formula:

| Value / urgency | Readiness | Suggested planning response |
|---|---|---|
| High value, high urgency | Ready | Prioritize for the nearest suitable slot |
| High value, high urgency | Not ready | Escalate dependencies; prepare work that can proceed without compromising verification |
| High value, low urgency | Ready | Place on the roadmap according to capacity |
| High value, low urgency | Not ready | Research or prepare first |
| Low value, high urgency | Ready | Handle only when risk or an actual obligation justifies it |
| Low value, low urgency | Any | Defer, reject, or retain for reconsideration if circumstances change |

Do not force every decision into a number. If the organization uses scoring, document its definitions, scale, weights, data sources, and approver. A score is a consistency aid; it does not eliminate human judgment or uncertainty.

### 5.3 Make trade-offs explicit

Every prioritization decision should state:

- the work selected;
- the reason for selecting it;
- the work deferred or rejected;
- capacity and dependencies;
- risks and assumptions;
- the conditions that would change the decision.

GOV.UK guidance notes that teams cannot do everything and must prioritize based on service conditions, external changes, and importance to users. This capacity-allocation principle is relevant as an operational analogy for editorial portfolios, not as an SEO rule. [5]

## 6. Planning Horizons: From Strategy to Working Calendar

Use multiple planning horizons so the team does not treat every plan as equally certain.

### 6.1 Strategic horizon

Covers medium- and long-term direction and priority themes. It is relatively stable but should still be reviewed when organizational goals, audiences, or market conditions change.

Example: strengthening customer support for a new product line.

### 6.2 Roadmap horizon

Covers initiatives and groups of work expected to enter upcoming work cycles. A roadmap shows sequence and dependencies; it is not a promise that dates can never change.

Example: user-needs research → technical-source preparation → setup-guide production → SME review.

### 6.3 Operational horizon

Covers work sufficiently defined for scheduling: owner, initial scope, dependencies, capacity, and target timing.

Example: a brief for a setup guide is prepared once product documentation and a technical reviewer are available.

### 6.4 Near-term commitment vs. future options

Distinguish approved, ready work from candidates still awaiting validation. Labels such as **committed**, **planned**, **proposed**, **blocked**, and **deferred** help prevent a roadmap from being mistaken for a public promise.

These horizons and labels are SEO-Playbook operational recommendations, not formal standards. Choose time ranges based on business cycles, uncertainty, and team capacity—not because a template insists on a 90-day calendar.

## 7. Editorial Calendar: A Schedule the Team Can Trust

An editorial calendar is the operational view of selected work. It helps coordination but does not replace strategy, the backlog, or prioritization decisions.

### 7.1 Minimum calendar fields

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Work / asset ID | Stable identifier for the item |
| Working title | Working name; not a final title-tag decision |
| Work type | New, improve, update, consolidate, retire, research |
| Audience need | The need the work is intended to serve |
| Strategic objective | The goal it supports |
| Status | Proposed, approved, ready, in progress, review, scheduled, published, blocked, deferred |
| Priority rationale | Reason and context for its priority |
| Owner | Person accountable for the work |
| Contributors / reviewers | SME, editor, designer, legal, or other relevant contributors |
| Dependencies | Required inputs or decisions |
| Capacity / effort estimate | Workload estimate using consistent internal units |
| Target milestone | Target for brief, draft, review, approval, or release |
| Confidence | How stable the estimate and dependencies are |
| Review trigger | Event that should prompt reconsideration |

The calendar can be simple. What matters is that each item has a clear status and owner, and that estimates are not disguised as commitments.

### 7.2 Schedule milestones, not just publication dates

A publication date alone is not enough. Schedule milestones that actually move the work forward:

- inputs and sources available;
- brief approved;
- draft ready for review;
- SME verification complete;
- approval complete;
- asset ready for publication;
- post-publication evaluation or handoff.

Production and editorial-review details are covered in 08.6 and 08.7. Here, milestones are used to test whether a schedule is feasible.

### 7.3 Avoid calendar theatre

Watch for these patterns:

- publication dates set before SMEs are available;
- every calendar slot must be filled even when no validated need exists;
- publication volume is treated as a substitute for value;
- update work is always sacrificed for new ideas;
- every item is treated as certain despite unresolved dependencies.

An empty calendar is not a failure when no worthwhile work is ready. A full calendar is not proof of a good strategy.

## 8. Capacity, Resources, and Dependencies

A plan that ignores capacity is just a wish list with dates attached.

### 8.1 Identify required capabilities

Editorial work may require a combination of:

- content strategist or planner;
- editor and writer;
- subject-matter expert;
- researcher or analyst;
- designer, photographer, videographer, or developer;
- translator and local reviewer;
- legal, compliance, privacy, or accessibility specialist;
- publisher or platform owner.

Not every assignment needs every role. Determine requirements based on format, risk, and substance.

### 8.2 Estimate effort honestly

Capacity estimates must account for more than writing time:

- understanding context and sources;
- coordinating with SMEs and stakeholders;
- collecting or validating data;
- drafting and revising;
- design or format production;
- review and approval;
- dependency management and handoffs.

Use historical team data when available. Otherwise, use estimate ranges and record assumptions. Do not manufacture false precision.

### 8.3 Manage dependencies

Examples of dependencies:

- a product launch must be confirmed before producing a product guide;
- data must be available before publishing an analysis;
- a technical reviewer must be appointed before technical claims are written;
- legal approval is required before certain material is released;
- design or engineering must provide supporting assets or functionality.

Record the dependency owner, required date, status, and impact of delay. Escalate blockers early; do not wait until the publication date has passed to announce that a required input was never available.

### 8.4 Reserve capacity for existing content

Do not allocate all capacity to new assets. Reserve room for corrections, factual changes, important updates, and unexpected needs. GOV.UK recommends planning resources for continuous improvement and directing attention according to service-health indicators, external changes, and importance to users. [5]

There is no universal percentage that must be allocated to maintenance. The appropriate share depends on information volatility, risk, portfolio size, and organizational capacity.

## 9. Balancing the Portfolio

A healthy portfolio does not chase only one kind of work.

### 9.1 New work vs. existing work

| Focus | Potential value | Risk if it dominates |
|---|---|---|
| New content | Meets needs that are not currently served | Creates duplication and increases maintenance burden |
| Improve existing | Improves assets already used by the audience | Requires diagnosis and coordination with asset owners |
| Update / maintenance | Keeps information accurate and usable | Can be repeatedly postponed because it seems less exciting |
| Consolidate / retire | Reduces confusion and unnecessary asset burden | A poor decision can remove information that is still needed |
| Research / validation | Reduces uncertainty before production | Can become indefinite delay without a defined next decision |

There is no ideal ratio that applies to every organization. Balance should reflect the actual condition of the portfolio and its needs.

### 9.2 Balance across audiences and objectives

Check whether the portfolio is disproportionately focused on:

- one audience segment while other important needs are ignored;
- acquisition content while existing customers remain underserved;
- easy-to-produce topics while important topics requiring cross-functional work are neglected;
- one format when user needs call for another;
- one objective to the point that important supporting functions are lost.

Use the strategy in 08.2 as the boundary. Do not rebuild topical maps or semantic coverage models here; Domain 09 owns semantic architecture.

## 10. Portfolio Review and Change Control

Review the portfolio when circumstances change. A review is not a monthly ritual that produces green status labels without decisions.

Review triggers may include:

- changes to organizational goals or product priorities;
- changes in audience needs or new evidence;
- changes to policies, facts, or important information;
- delayed dependencies;
- changes in team capacity;
- discovery of an existing asset that already meets the need;
- findings from editorial audits or relevant performance evaluation.

Content strategy should be iterated as understanding of users or product direction changes. [4] Existing content should also be monitored; decisions may include revision or retirement when it becomes outdated. [6]

At each review, explicitly decide whether an item should:

- remain on plan;
- be accelerated or delayed;
- have its scope changed;
- be blocked until dependencies are met;
- be replaced by higher-value work;
- be deferred or rejected;
- move into the appropriate maintenance or audit process.

Record the rationale, date, decision owner, and capacity changes. Do not silently change priorities and expect everyone to somehow keep up.

## 11. Practical Planning Workflow

The following sequence is an SEO-Playbook framework that can be adapted to team size.

1. **Set strategic boundaries.** Take goals, audiences, scope, and decision rules from 08.2.
2. **Collect work candidates.** Combine Domain 05 handoffs, audience evidence, stakeholder input, organizational changes, and existing-content needs.
3. **Check needs and duplication.** Ensure each need has adequate evidence and is not already sufficiently served by an available asset.
4. **Classify the work type.** Decide whether the item is new content, improvement, update, consolidation, retirement, or validation.
5. **Assess value, urgency, readiness, effort, and risk.** Keep dimensions separate; do not hide trade-offs in one number.
6. **Make the priority decision.** Select, defer, reject, or request more evidence. Record the rationale.
7. **Check capacity and dependencies.** Match work to available people, skills, time, and inputs.
8. **Build the roadmap and calendar.** Place items according to their certainty and milestones.
9. **Assign an owner and decision authority.** Apply governance from 08.2; no material item should lack an accountable owner.
10. **Review the portfolio periodically and when triggers occur.** Adjust decisions when evidence, strategy, capacity, or risk changes.
11. **Hand off production-ready work to 08.4.** Do not force work with inadequate scope or evidence into a brief.

## 12. Worked Example: Portfolio for a Coffee Equipment Retailer

**Illustrative example.** This is not keyword data, an analytics result, or a real business case study.

A retailer sells home espresso machines, grinders, and accessories. The team receives several work candidates:

| ID | Candidate work | Evidence / context | Initial work type |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-01 | Guide to choosing a home grinder | The 05.9 handoff indicates comparison needs, and the existing page misses several important criteria | Improve existing |
| C-02 | Guide to cleaning a grinder | Repeated support questions and product documentation are available | New asset |
| C-03 | Monthly coffee-trends article | Stakeholder idea, but audience need and unique contribution are unclear | Research / validate |
| C-04 | Accessory compatibility update | A product specification change has been confirmed | Update / maintenance |
| C-05 | Commercial grinder repair page | The company does not provide that repair service | No editorial action |

### Portfolio decision

| ID | Value / urgency | Readiness | Decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-01 | High / Medium | Ready after SME review | Prioritize next cycle | Improve the existing asset; do not create a new URL without a strong editorial reason. |
| C-02 | High / Medium | Ready | Schedule after C-04 | The need is clear and technical sources are available; a product reviewer is required. |
| C-03 | Unclear / Low | Not ready | Defer | The idea has not demonstrated sufficient need or contribution. Validate before production. |
| C-04 | High / High | Ready | Immediate priority | The specification change means existing information must be checked before it misleads customers. |
| C-05 | Not relevant | Not applicable | Reject | It does not match the services offered; there is no reason to create content merely to chase a query. |

### Capacity and calendar

The team has capacity for one major assignment and one small assignment in the nearest cycle. C-04 is scheduled first because the specification change is confirmed. C-01 enters the next major-work slot once a technical reviewer is available. C-02 remains on the roadmap, and C-03 waits for validation.

This example shows that priorities are not determined solely by search demand or production convenience. Factual urgency, readiness, organizational capability, and the cost of delay also matter.

## 13. Common Planning Failures

| Failure | Why it fails | Better response |
|---|---|---|
| Treating the editorial calendar as the strategy | A calendar shows work and timing, not the strategic rationale | Connect each item to goals, needs, and priority decisions |
| Turning every priority keyword directly into a brief | Opportunity research is not a commitment of editorial capacity | Use the Domain 05 handoff, then make a portfolio decision |
| Creating only new content | Existing assets may be outdated, inaccurate, or already sufficient | Balance new work with improvement, updates, consolidation, and retirement |
| Keeping the calendar full at all times | Encourages production without a clear need | Leave capacity available when no worthwhile work is ready |
| Setting publication dates too early | Ignores dependencies and review | Schedule milestones and confidence, not just release dates |
| Assigning no owner | Work stalls when a decision or input is needed | Assign an owner and decision authority |
| Giving every item a precise score | Scores can conceal assumptions and uncertainty | Show dimensions, evidence, and trade-offs separately |
| Allocating no capacity to maintenance | Important changes accumulate until they become risks | Reserve capacity and define escalation triggers |
| Never cleaning the backlog | Old ideas remain priorities without new evidence | Review, defer, reject, or remove candidates with recorded rationale |

## Key Takeaways

- Content planning selects editorial work; the editorial calendar is only one operational tool.
- A content portfolio must include both new assets and work on existing assets.
- Use audience evidence, strategy, validated keyword research, portfolio conditions, and operational constraints as inputs.
- Do not automatically convert keyword priority into editorial priority.
- Separate value, urgency, readiness, effort, risk, and opportunity cost.
- There is no universal scoring formula, publishing cadence, or new-versus-existing content ratio.
- Schedules must reflect capacity, SME availability, approvals, and dependencies.
- Reserve capacity for maintenance and unpredictable changes.
- Record decisions, trade-offs, owners, assumptions, and review triggers.
- Production-ready work moves to 08.4; planning does not replace the brief, outline, or production workflow.
