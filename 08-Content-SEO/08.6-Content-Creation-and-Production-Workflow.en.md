# 08.6 — Content Creation & Production Workflow

The brief is approved. The outline is ready. Now the plan must become an asset readers can actually use.

**08.6 governs drafting and content production from approved editorial inputs through handoff to editorial review.** It covers execution: writing, media creation, contributor collaboration, source-material management, version control, and production coordination.

Production is not a race to fill a page. A fast draft that loses its sources, drifts from the brief, or obscures who created it merely transfers the problem to reviewers.

## 1. Definitions and Scope

**Content creation** develops prepared information and materials into the specified content format. Activities may include drafting, rewriting, interviews, data processing, visual creation, audio/video production, and supporting assets.

**Content production** is the operational workflow governing how content is made: responsibilities, inputs, collaboration, asset management, and readiness for review.

In SEO-Playbook, production ends when the content package and production records are ready for 08.7. **08.6 does not grant final editorial approval or declare content publishable.**

| Activity | Canonical owner |

| Drafting, authoring, asset production, contributor coordination, production handoff | 08.6 |

| Portfolio planning, priority, capacity, cross-asset dependencies | 08.3 |
| Brief, scope, audience, evidence requirements, deliverables, acceptance criteria | 08.4 |

Writers may perform basic self-checks and address issues found while drafting. Final checks of claims, sources, risks, and publication readiness belong to 08.7. Do not give producers approval authority they do not hold.

## 2. Production Inputs: Do Not Start with an Immature Brief

Collect approved inputs before production, or explicitly record unresolved gaps.

| Input | Minimum content | Owner / source |
|---|---|---|
| Approved content brief | Purpose, audience, user needs, scope, exclusions, format, acceptance criteria | 08.4 |
| Approved outline | Information flow, main sections, argument, evidence needs | 08.5 |
| Research handoff | Relevant query/page mapping or intent evidence, without repeating research | Domain 05 |
| Source pack | Primary sources, data, quotations, documents, dates, context, usage limits | Researcher / content owner |
| SME input | Domain knowledge, processes, experience, open questions | Subject-matter expert |
| Existing assets | Canonical material, visuals, data, templates, reusable content | Content owner |
| Production specification | Format, deliverables, platform, media specifications, constraints | Brief / production lead |
| Governance requirements | Owner, contributors, reviewers, approver, permissions, escalation | 08.2 |
| Technical constraints | CMS, media formats, workflow tools, accessibility needs, technical handoff | Relevant team / Domain 10 |

ONS describes a user-needs workflow: validate needs and acceptance criteria, outline, write from the outline, then review with peers and stakeholders. This is an institutional example, not a mandatory template for every organization. [1]

### 2.1 Production readiness

Before starting, confirm:
- Purpose and audience are clear.
- Scope and exclusions are approved.
- The outline is sufficiently developed.
- Acceptance criteria are checkable.
- Initial sources and required SMEs are identified.
- Format and deliverables are agreed.
- Owners, contributors, reviewers, and approvers are known.
- Relevant risks, permissions, embargoes, privacy issues, and asset-use limits are recorded.

If core information is missing, do not disguise ambiguity by starting to write. Return questions to the brief or planning owner.

## 3. Choose a Production Format

Choose based on the reader's task, information complexity, available evidence, and use context—not because a format is trending.

| Format | Suitable when | Possible production needs |
|---|---|---|
| Article / guide | Readers need to understand a concept, process, or decision | Narrative draft, sources, examples, supporting diagrams |
| Tutorial / procedure | Readers must complete steps | Step sequence, prerequisites, screenshots, procedure testing |
| Comparison / product content | Readers must compare options | Criteria, comparative data, method, limitations, sources |
| Dataset / data story | Readers need to explore figures and findings | Data, definitions, methodology, tables, visualization |
| Infographic / diagram | Relationships or processes are clearer visually | Visual brief, data, labels, sources, text description |
| Audio / video | Demonstration or audiovisual explanation helps | Script, storyboard, recording, transcript, captions |
| Social / short-form | Information must fit a specific channel | Message adaptation, channel format, asset variants, approval |
| Interactive content | Readers need input, exploration, or simulation | Requirements, interaction design, data, testing, technical implementation |

ONS documents content types including articles, datasets, methodologies, static pages, diagrams, illustrations, and social content. The table is an SEO-Playbook cross-format example, not a universal taxonomy or claim that every format suits every goal. [2]

### 3.1 One idea, multiple formats?

Repurposing can help when each adaptation has a clear function and context. Cutting one article into ten posts does not automatically create ten valuable assets.

For every derivative, define its audience and task, what information changes, channel constraints, retained sources and attribution, whether it remains accurate without the original context, and who owns the derivative version. If adaptation changes meaning or claims, it needs appropriate review. Approval of one format does not automatically approve every version.

## 4. Production Roles and Collaboration

Production may involve one person or many. The requirement is clear responsibility, not a particular team structure.

| Role | Production responsibility |
|---|---|
| Content owner | Protects purpose, scope, and key editorial decisions |
| Producer / project lead | Coordinates workflow, schedule, dependencies, status, and handoff |
| Writer / creator | Develops drafts and assets against the brief and outline |
| Researcher | Collects and organizes sources, data, and research notes |
| SME | Provides subject expertise and answers technical questions; is not automatically editor or approver |
| Designer / media producer | Creates visual, audio, video, or other format assets |
| Translator / localizer | Adapts content across languages or contexts as briefed |
| Editor | May guide production and editorial work; formal QA still follows 08.7 |
| Approver | Makes approval decisions under 08.2 governance |

One person may hold several roles in a small team. But decision types remain distinct: expert contribution is not automatic publication approval, and “written” does not mean “verified.”

### 4.1 Working with SMEs and external contributors

1. Assign one content owner and one production coordinator.
2. Provide the brief, outline, style guide, and contribution boundaries.
3. Specify what is needed from the SME: facts, context, examples, data, or limited review.
4. Ask specific questions rather than “please check everything.”
5. Keep contributions and sources in traceable working documents.
6. Define who resolves disagreements and who has approval authority.

ONS notes that external collaborators need to understand the publisher's house style and writing guidance. This illustrates why editorial standards should be communicated before co-authoring begins. [3]

### 4.2 Collaboration rules

Agree on a single source of truth for drafts, comment/change procedures, file naming and versions, feedback deadlines, who may change scope, dispute resolution, contribution attribution, and who locks the review version.

Avoid editing multiple unsynchronized copies. “final-v7-newest-revised-really-final” is not version control.

## 5. Source Packs and Research Notes

Production uses sources, but 08.6 does not replace formal fact-checking in 08.7.

A source pack collects materials so creators understand context and can trace information to its origin.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Source ID | Unique project identifier |
| Title / publisher | Document, page, organization, or creator |
| Canonical URL / location | Original source location |
| Author / owner | Responsible author or organization |
| Publication / update date | Date, if available |
| Access date | Date accessed |
| Relevant section | Relevant passage or section |
| Supported point | Claim or context it may support |
| Limitations | Scope, method, conflicts, or usage restrictions |
| Rights / permission | License, permission, attribution, or usage status where relevant |

### 5.1 Separate facts, interpretations, and questions

Research notes should distinguish:
- **Source fact:** what the source actually states.
- **Observation:** what the team directly observed.
- **Interpretation:** meaning inferred from facts or observations.
- **Hypothesis:** an idea not sufficiently demonstrated.
- **Open question:** something requiring confirmation.
- **Production note:** direction or context for a writer/designer.

Do not present an interpretation as a source quotation. Do not turn SME notes into public facts without appropriate context and verification.

### 5.2 Quotations, data, images, and third-party material

Before using external assets, identify the source and owner; check relevant license or permission; record required attribution; do not assume publicly accessible material is free to reuse; preserve data context, units, periods, and definitions; avoid altering quotation meaning; and retain original files or references needed for audit.

Legal and licensing requirements vary by jurisdiction and asset type. Escalate unclear or risky uses to legal counsel or the rights owner rather than inventing a legal conclusion.

## 6. Drafting: Turn the Outline into Content

Drafting turns the outline into explanations readers can understand and use.

### 6.1 Write to complete the task

During drafting:
- start with prioritized reader needs;
- use the outline as a working structure, not handcuffs;
- answer the main question before adding supporting detail;
- explain terms, assumptions, prerequisites, and limits;
- connect claims to sources or evidence notes;
- use relevant, defensible examples;
- remove repetition that adds no understanding;
- mark gaps and uncertainty rather than hiding them behind confident wording.

ONS recommends writing from validated user needs and an agreed outline, using active voice and plain language, and checking that content does not duplicate material already available. [1]

### 6.2 Use the outline adaptively

If drafting reveals an uncovered core question, contradiction, incorrect conceptual dependency, evidence that fails to support an argument, or a material scope change, stop improvising and record the impact. Minor structural changes may be documented in the draft. Major changes to purpose, audience, or scope must return to the brief owner and 08.5.

### 6.3 Do not hide TODOs in the draft

Use explicit markers, such as:
- [SOURCE NEEDED: claim about ...]
- [SME CHECK: procedure ...]
- [DATA PENDING: period ...]
- [RIGHTS CHECK: image ...]
- [OPEN QUESTION: usage boundary ...]

Resolve these or clearly carry them forward before handoff to 08.7. Reviewers should not have to guess whether a placeholder is final copy or an oversight.

## 7. Producing Non-Text Assets

Visual, audio, video, data, and interactive assets need production briefs connected to the main brief.

### 7.1 Visuals and diagrams

Record the visual's purpose and intended understanding; data, sources, labels, units, and dates; the message and context that must remain visible; format and output needs; text descriptions or alternatives; and asset rights and attribution.

Do not make a chart more attractive by distorting scales, removing context, or hiding uncertainty.

### 7.2 Audio and video

Record purpose and audience; script or rundown; speakers and relevant consent; sources for spoken claims; transcript, captions, and supporting materials; final file version, production date, and edit notes.

Transcripts and captions provide access to audiovisual information in text form. Technical accessibility implementation must follow applicable standards and processes; 08.6 records production needs but does not take over the entire technical accessibility audit.

### 7.3 Data and interactive assets

Keep the dataset origin and version, variable definitions and units, transformation or calculation methods, assumptions and data limits, expected interaction behavior, test cases and results, and technical implementation owner.

If production requires an application or technical component, hand requirements and assets to the technical implementation owner. Domain 10 owns technical delivery and validation.

## 8. AI-Assisted Production

AI may assist production, but it is not accountable for the content. A model does not sign off claims, bear the consequences of errors, or replace required expertise.

Possible uses include brainstorming, SME interview questions, organizing notes or outlines, first drafts or wording alternatives, transcription, initial translation, format transformation, and checklists or summaries for review.

Google says generative AI can assist research and structure original content, but large-scale production without added value may fall under scaled content abuse. Google also warns that generative outputs can be inaccurate and should be manually checked before publication. [4]

### 8.1 Minimum AI controls

1. Confirm AI use is permitted by governance and organizational policy.
2. Do not enter confidential, personal, or licensed material into unapproved services.
3. Record AI use when material to provenance or process transparency.
4. Verify facts, quotations, references, figures, and transformations against original sources.
5. Check for copying, fabrication, missing context, or changed meaning.
6. Ensure a competent human owns decisions and the final result.
7. Decide whether readers reasonably need context about automation.

Google recommends explaining how content was created when that information is relevant to readers, including when automation played a substantial role. This is not a universal disclosure requirement for every use of an AI tool. [5]

### 8.2 Prohibited production shortcuts

- Fabricating sources, data, quotations, experience, or credentials.
- Treating model output as fact verification.
- Publishing large numbers of generic pages merely because production is cheap.
- Using AI to misrepresent authorship or expertise.
- Delegating editorial accountability to a model.

AI is a tool in the workflow, not a bypass around the workflow.

## 9. Production Workflow

This is an SEO-Playbook operating model. Teams may combine stages for simple work or separate them for complex and high-risk work.

| Stage | Activity | Output / gate |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Intake | Receive brief, outline, source pack, constraints | Production-ready inputs |
| 2. Kickoff | Confirm owner, roles, format, timeline, decision route | Production plan |
| 3. Research preparation | Organize materials, open questions, SME requests, rights checks | Research/source pack |
| 4. Drafting / creation | Write substance and create format-specific assets | Working draft and assets |
| 5. Internal iteration | Resolve production comments, check brief coverage, record changes | Revised production draft |
| 6. Creator self-check | Check placeholders, structure, deliverables, and traceability | Package ready for editorial review |
| 7. Handoff | Send draft, assets, source records, open issues, and version details to 08.7 | Review-ready package |

Stage 6 does not replace editorial QA. Formal fact-checking, source verification, risk review, and publication approval occur through 08.7.

## 10. Version Control and Production Records

Keep enough history to answer: who created or changed the asset; which brief and outline versions were used; which sources and data informed it; what material changed; what questions remain open; and which asset version was sent for review.

### 10.1 Production statuses

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Not started | Production has not begun |
| Ready | Required production inputs are available and approved |
| In progress | Drafting or asset creation is underway |
| Blocked | A dependency or missing input prevents progress |
| Internal iteration | Production team is revising the draft |
| Handoff ready | Package is ready for 08.7 |
| Returned for production changes | 08.7 requested changes requiring production work |
| Closed | Production work is complete under the workflow decision |

This is SEO-Playbook's operational taxonomy, not an industry standard. Handoff ready does not mean Approved or Published.

### 10.2 Change log

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Asset ID | Content identifier |
| Version | Version number or identifier |
| Date | Change date |
| Contributor | Person who made the change |
| Change summary | What changed |
| Reason | Why it changed |
| Source / dependency | Input that prompted the change |
| Open issue | Unresolved matter |
| Next owner | Person responsible for the next stage |

Do not rely on application comments as the only record if the system cannot guarantee required access and retention.

## 11. Production Handoff to 08.7

The handoff package should let reviewers understand what was created, which inputs were used, and which areas still need attention.

### 11.1 Handoff checklist

- Complete draft and agreed deliverables.
- Brief and outline versions identified.
- Source pack and research notes available.
- Provenance recorded for quotations, data, visuals, and external assets.
- Placeholders and TODOs resolved or explicitly flagged.
- Open questions and claims requiring SME checks recorded.
- Scope and structural changes explained.
- File versions and asset inventory clear.
- AI-assisted production notes available where relevant.
- Risks, rights issues, privacy concerns, and dependencies disclosed.
- Production owner available for clarification.

08.7 then performs fact-checking, source verification, editorial review, risk review, and publication-readiness assessment. If reviewers return the draft, 08.6 handles production changes; 08.7 retains its review and approval role under governance.

## 13. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** a team creates a guide comparing inventory software for small-business owners.

**Inputs:** The brief defines the audience, software-selection task, feature scope, and acceptance criteria. The outline sequences business needs, comparison criteria, costs, limitations, and evaluation steps. The source pack contains vendor documentation, prices, test notes, and SME questions.

**Production:**
1. The content owner assigns a writer and an SME to validate product details.
2. The researcher builds a source table with access dates and plan coverage.
3. The writer drafts from the outline and flags unconfirmed feature claims.
4. The designer creates a comparison table and selection-flow diagram.
5. The SME clarifies features using documentation and test accounts.
6. The writer updates the draft and records changes.
7. The producer assembles the draft, visuals, source pack, and open issues.

**Handoff:** The package goes to 08.7 for claim, source, consistency, risk, and publication-readiness review. The production team does not declare the guide approved merely because every file is complete.

## 14. Common Production Failures

1. Starting without mature inputs, forcing writers to make strategy and scope decisions.
2. Ignoring the brief and outline, then calling scope drift creativity.
3. Asking SMEs to write without direction; expertise still needs editorial guidance.
4. Failing to store sources with claims, forcing reviewers to start from zero.
5. Maintaining parallel “final” files, leaving the review version unclear.
6. Allowing unlimited stakeholder comments without a decision owner.
7. Creating visuals late without a brief, causing mismatched data and narrative or rework.
8. Treating AI output as fact; fluent language is not evidence.
9. Repurposing without adaptation, stripping context from the new channel or format.
10. Handing over only a draft link, omitting sources, versions, risks, and open issues.
11. Treating production completion as approval; a complete draft is not necessarily verified or publishable.
12. Keeping no change record, leaving reviewers unable to understand why claims or scope changed.

## Key Takeaways

- 08.6 turns briefs and outlines into drafts and production assets.
- Start from approved inputs; do not patch strategic gaps with improvisation.
- Formats should serve reader needs and asset objectives.
- Make owners, contributors, reviewers, and approvers explicit.
- Preserve source packs, research notes, provenance, versions, and change logs.
- AI may assist drafting, but output still requires human review.
- Production ends when the package is ready for 08.7—not when the writer stops typing.
- 08.7 owns fact-checking, formal QA, and publication readiness.
- Page-level implementation belongs to Domain 07; technical delivery belongs to Domain 10.
- A disciplined workflow does not guarantee rankings. It makes production more focused, traceable, and accountable.
