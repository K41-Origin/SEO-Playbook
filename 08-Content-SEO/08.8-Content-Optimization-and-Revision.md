# 08.8 — Content Optimization & Revision

Published content is not sacred. When information stops helping, explanations become confusing, or reader needs remain unmet, revision may be necessary. Changing a few sentences, adding keywords, and updating a date is not a strategy; it is cosmetic work disguised as editorial improvement.

**08.8 governs substantive improvement of existing content so that it is more accurate, clear, complete, relevant, and effective at serving the reader's task.** It owns editorial revision decisions and execution—not portfolio-wide inventory audits, ongoing maintenance policy, or page-element implementation.

## 1. Definitions and Scope

**Content optimization** in this domain evaluates and improves existing content against reader needs, editorial purpose, evidence, and current conditions. It may clarify explanations or instructions; correct facts, context, figures, and references; close information gaps; remove irrelevant, repetitive, or misleading material; strengthen analysis, evidence, examples, and original contribution; adapt to product, process, policy, or contextual changes; restructure information; or change format when the current form does not serve the reader.

Google recommends assessing whether a page provides original information, adequate explanation, added value, accuracy, and a satisfying experience. This is quality-evaluation guidance, not an optimization formula or ranking guarantee. [1]

**Content revision** is the action of changing part or all of an asset to resolve an identified issue. SEO-Playbook's operational categories are:
- **Corrective:** fix errors or inaccurate information.
- **Clarifying:** improve explanations, terms, instructions, or limitations.
- **Completeness-driven:** add important missing information.
- **Evidence-driven:** replace or strengthen claims using better evidence.
- **Structural:** change information order or grouping.
- **Consolidating:** remove repetition or overlapping sections within the asset.
- **Format revision:** change presentation to better serve the reader.

These categories are internal working taxonomy, not official Google classifications.

## 2. When Does Content Need Revision?

Start from an explainable problem, not a vague desire to “optimize” a page.

| Trigger | Example | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Inaccuracy | Price, feature, procedure, or fact is wrong | Verify and correct claims |
| Incompleteness | A critical step or decision condition is missing | Add necessary information |
| Unclear explanation | Ambiguous terms, logical jumps, confusing instructions | Rewrite or restructure |
| Weak evidence | Important claims lack adequate support | Find evidence, qualify, or remove |
| Changed reader needs | Reader task or decision has changed | Reassess purpose, scope, and content |
| Brief no longer met | Core acceptance criteria are not satisfied | Revise against criteria or create a new brief |
| Substantive duplication | Sections repeat information without purpose | Consolidate or remove repetition |
| Unsuitable format | Current table, guide, or narrative does not help the task | Consider a more suitable format |
| Verifiable feedback | Users repeatedly misunderstand a particular step | Trace the failure point and fix it |

GOV.UK recommends monitoring whether content remains useful and findable, then changing it when problems, updates, or improvements are identified. This is an example from public-sector content management, not a universal rule for every site. [3]

### 2.1 Performance signals are not editorial diagnoses

Performance data can identify assets worth investigating. Search Console reports trends by query, page, country, impressions, clicks, and other metrics. [2] But those metrics do not automatically explain why a change occurred. A click decline does not prove content is poor; more impressions do not prove it is more helpful. Search demand, SERP changes, competition, seasonality, technical changes, and page changes may all contribute.

Treat metrics as **investigation signals**, not quality verdicts or causal evidence. Domain 14 owns metric interpretation, attribution, and performance evaluation.

## 3. Diagnose Before Rewriting

State the diagnosis specifically:
- **Observed issue:** what was actually found?
- **Evidence:** what supports the finding?
- **Reader impact:** how does it affect understanding or task completion?
- **Scope:** which parts are affected?
- **Proposed intervention:** what change may solve it?
- **Uncertainty:** what remains unknown or needs verification?

Illustrative example:

| Element | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Observed issue | A data-export guide describes steps for an interface that has changed |
| Evidence | Current product documentation and retesting on the stated version |
| Reader impact | Readers cannot find the described menu |
| Scope | Export steps and related screenshots |
| Proposed intervention | Update steps, menu labels, and visuals based on testing |
| Uncertainty | Interface differences by account plan remain unverified |

“Needs freshness” is not a useful diagnosis. It does not identify a problem, evidence, or required work.

## 4. Evaluate Existing Content

Compare the current asset with its intended purpose and required conditions.

### 4.1 Purpose and reader task
Ask whether the original purpose and audience remain relevant; whether the content helps readers complete a task or make a decision; whether the main answer remains correct and sufficient; and whether readers must seek elsewhere to fill a gap this asset should address. Google recommends assessing whether readers receive enough information to achieve their goal and have a satisfying experience. [1]

### 4.2 Accuracy and evidence
Check changed facts and figures; outdated sources; changes in definitions, versions, regions, or effective dates; claims broader than their evidence; obsolete examples; and quotations, attribution, and provenance. Material factual changes must undergo 08.7 fact-checking and source verification. No revision is exempt merely because it appears small.

### 4.3 Completeness and explanatory depth
Check whether the content answers the core question directly; explains prerequisites and important limits; provides necessary steps and context; connects claims to evidence and reasoning; discusses trade-offs and conditions where recommendations do not apply; and adds genuine value beyond repeating other sources. Google encourages original information, valuable analysis, adequate description, and added value when using other sources. [1]

### 4.4 Clarity and information flow
Look for buried answers, undefined terms, ambiguous sentences, repetition, abrupt transitions, missing procedural steps, and detail that obscures the main decision. If the revision requires major rebuilding of information sequence or argument, use Domain 08.5. Drafting and asset production follow 08.6.

## 5. Choose the Right Intervention

Use the smallest intervention that genuinely solves the problem without damaging valid material.

| Intervention | When to use | Risk to control |
|---|---|---|
| Retain | Content remains accurate and fit for purpose | Do not retain only because it is old |
| Correct | A fact or detail is wrong | Verify source and impact |
| Clarify | Correct information is difficult to understand | Do not change meaning while simplifying |
| Add | An important gap exists | Avoid irrelevant additions |
| Remove | Material is irrelevant, repetitive, or misleading | Do not remove necessary context |
| Restructure | Information order obstructs understanding | Check dependencies and argument flow |
| Rewrite | A major section no longer meets its purpose | Recheck scope, evidence, and criteria |
| Reformat | Current format does not serve the task | Ensure the new format is more useful and accessible |
| Re-scope | Purpose or audience materially changes | Create a new brief or escalate to portfolio governance |
| No change | Evidence is insufficient or the issue is unproven | Record rationale and further investigation |

“No change” is a valid decision. Not every metric fluctuation requires a rewrite.

## 6. Prioritize Revisions

When many assets need work, prioritize by consequence and editorial value—not ranking alone.

Consider:
1. **Risk:** could an error harm safety, finances, rights, or important decisions?
2. **Severity:** how much does the issue obstruct the reader's goal?
3. **Evidence confidence:** how strong is the evidence that the problem exists?
4. **Audience impact:** how many or how important are the affected readers?
5. **Time sensitivity:** does information have an urgent effective date or change?
6. **Effort and dependencies:** are SMEs, research, new production, or approvals required?
7. **Strategic relevance:** does the asset still have a clear purpose and audience?

Internal prioritization template:

| Issue | Risk | Reader impact | Evidence confidence | Effort | Priority | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Issue] | High / Medium / Low | High / Medium / Low | High / Medium / Low | High / Medium / Low | [P1/P2/P3] | [Reason] |

These labels are internal management tools, not Google ranking standards or universal thresholds. High-risk content may require immediate action even with low traffic.

## 7. Execute Revisions with Control

### 7.1 Establish a baseline
Before changing the asset, preserve the current version; record the objective and issue; document evidence and sources; mark sections to retain, change, add, or remove; identify reviewers and dependencies; and, where relevant, record performance baselines through Domain 14.

### 7.2 Preserve what remains valid
Do not rewrite an entire page merely to appear busy. Retain explanations, data, examples, and structure that remain accurate and useful. Change the problematic parts, then check whether the changes affect other sections.

### 7.3 Maintain claim traceability
For each material change, record the old claim, new claim or explanation, reason, source/evidence, verifying reviewer, and affected sections. If new evidence is insufficient, qualify the statement, explain uncertainty, or hold the claim. Never fill gaps with confident-sounding assumptions.

### 7.4 Avoid optimization theater
Do not add words to hit a target length; repeat keywords without improving the answer; change publication dates without substantive updates; add generic sections that do not help readers; rewrite solely because competitors have longer pages; or mass-delete old material on the assumption that a site must look fresh.

Google states there is no preferred word count and warns against changing dates without substantive changes or adding/removing content at scale merely to create a freshness impression. [1]

## 8. Search Intent and Changing Reader Needs

08.8 may use Domain 05 research handoffs to assess whether a revision still meets the intended reader and search context. Keyword discovery, intent classification, clustering, and keyword-to-page mapping remain owned by Domain 05.

When reader needs or search context appear to change:
1. identify evidence of the change;
2. determine whether it is temporary or structural;
3. validate it using appropriate data and research;
4. decide whether the existing asset remains the right answer;
5. revise the content or scope, or recommend new portfolio work.

Do not rewrite content to chase every query variation. One page need not serve unrelated needs. If purpose or audience changes materially, escalate to 08.3 and create a new brief through 08.4.

## 9. Original Contribution and Added Value

Revision is an opportunity to improve real value, not add decoration.

Consider whether the content needs accountable testing or demonstrations; relevant data analysis; trade-off explanations; transparent contextual examples; process or method documentation; synthesis with clear interpretation; or genuine, relevant first-hand experience.

Google distinguishes source attribution from original effort: crediting a source does not automatically replace original contribution. [1]

Do not invent experience, data, tests, testimonials, or credentials to make a revision appear stronger. If no defensible new contribution exists, do not pretend otherwise.

## 10. Review, Verification, and Release Gate

After substantive revision:
1. compare the result with the original problem and objective;
2. check for new contradictions or gaps;
3. verify all changed claims and sources;
4. perform editorial review and proofreading;
5. obtain SME or specialist review when the change requires it;
6. confirm acceptance criteria;
7. record version and approval decision.

Fact-checking, source verification, risk review, and publication readiness follow 08.7. Approval of an earlier version does not automatically cover an unreviewed material revision.

Changes to page elements—title elements, meta descriptions, HTML headings, URLs, alt text, or markup—must go through Domain 07. Technical implementation and crawl/index validation belong to Domain 10.

## 11. Evaluate After Revision

Define in advance what should be learned:
- can readers complete the task more successfully?
- have the questions or confusion that triggered the work decreased?
- is information now accurate and complete?
- have unintended consequences appeared?
- have relevant performance indicators changed?

Use suitable methods: user feedback, usability testing, SME checks, support-query analysis, or relevant performance metrics. Domain 14 owns organic-performance measurement and analysis.

Do not infer that a revision caused a ranking increase merely because metrics rose after publication. Sequence does not prove causation. Record other possible influences and evaluation limits.

## 13. Worked Example

**Illustrative example:** an inventory-software guide says every product plan includes multi-location synchronization. Vendor documentation shows the feature is available only on selected plans.

**Diagnosis:** the feature claim is too broad; current plan documentation and direct verification in a test account provide evidence; readers could buy a plan that does not meet operational needs; the comparison table, plan explanation, and recommendation are affected.

**Intervention:**
1. Replace the universal claim with plan-specific information.
2. Add the check date and official source link.
3. Explain when multi-location capability is needed.
4. Make recommendations conditional on reader needs rather than endorsing a plan without context.
5. Ask an SME to check the feature interpretation and an editor to check clarity.
6. Record changes and complete 08.7 review before publication.

This revision addresses a specific claim using evidence. There is no reason to add 1,000 words or change the page date when the actual problem is an inaccurate feature statement.

## 14. Common Content Optimization Failures

1. **Optimizing without diagnosis.** The page is rebuilt without a specific problem.
2. **Treating a traffic decline as proof of poor content.** Performance data is an investigation signal, not a complete diagnosis.
3. **Rewriting everything.** Valid sections are damaged or lose context.
4. **Using length as a quality proxy.** More text does not prove more value.
5. **Keyword stuffing disguised as revision.** Wording changes but reader needs remain unmet.
6. **Changing dates without substantive work.** New appearance, old content.
7. **Adding claims without evidence.** Revision increases error risk.
8. **Removing context to be concise.** Readers lose prerequisites or important limits.
9. **Confusing revision with maintenance.** Substantive improvement differs from 08.9 lifecycle policy and review cadence.
10. **Confusing content with page elements.** Page title, meta description, HTML heading, URL, and alt text belong to Domain 07.
11. **Claiming causal impact without analysis.** Metrics after revision do not prove revision caused the change.
12. **Silently changing scope.** Audience or purpose shifts require appropriate planning and briefing.

## Key Takeaways

- Content optimization is substantive improvement of existing content.
- Start with a specific problem and checkable evidence.
- Performance data helps identify what to investigate; it is not an editorial diagnosis or causal proof.
- Choose the smallest intervention that solves the problem.
- Preserve material that remains correct and useful.
- Improve accuracy, completeness, clarity, flow, evidence, and added value.
- Do not chase length, keyword density, or an appearance of freshness.
- Material changes must pass 08.7 review and fact-checking.
- Domain 07 owns page-element implementation; 08.9 owns lifecycle maintenance; Domain 14 owns measurement.
- Good revision helps content meet reader goals. Rankings are not guaranteed.
