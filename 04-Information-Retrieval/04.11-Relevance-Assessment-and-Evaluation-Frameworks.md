# 04.11 — Relevance Assessment & Evaluation Frameworks

> Retrieval evaluation is only as credible as the task, collection, and relevance judgments behind it. A metric cannot rescue a broken test design.

## Purpose and Scope

Information retrieval (IR) evaluation needs a defensible way to decide what counts as relevant, assemble a test collection, and compare retrieval systems under controlled conditions. **Relevance assessment** is the process of judging how well an information item satisfies a defined information need. **An evaluation framework** defines how the collection, information needs, judgments, retrieval runs, and analysis fit together.

This topic covers:

- Relevance as an assessment concept, including binary and graded judgments.
- Assessors, assessment guidelines, and agreement.
- Test collections: documents, topics, and relevance judgments.
- Qrels and the distinction between relevant, non-relevant, and unjudged items.
- Pooling and other ways to select documents for assessment.
- Offline, user-centered, and benchmark-based evaluation frameworks.
- Experimental design, validity, reproducibility, and methodological limitations.

Formal metric definitions—including Precision, Recall, Average Precision, MAP, MRR, DCG, and nDCG—belong to **04.12 — Information Retrieval Evaluation Metrics**. This document establishes the assessment and test-collection foundations those metrics depend on. [1][2]

## 1. What Is Relevance Assessment?

A retrieval system receives a query, but a query is not always a complete statement of the user's information need. Assessors therefore need a task description that explains what the searcher is trying to find or accomplish.

A document is relevant when it satisfies the defined information need—not merely because it repeats the query terms. The same document may be relevant for one task and irrelevant for another. Relevance is therefore assessed in relation to a specified need, collection, and evaluation context. [1]

### 1.1 Query Is Not the Assessment Criterion

Consider the query:

`python`

Possible information needs include:

- Learn the Python programming language.
- Find information about keeping a python snake as a pet.
- Locate the official Python language documentation.
- Find a particular Python package or API.

A document about snakes may contain the exact query term but be irrelevant to a programming task. A programming tutorial may be relevant even if it does not repeat the exact query in every section.

**Assessment rule:** judge the document against the information need and the documented criteria, not against superficial term overlap. [1]

### 1.2 Relevance Is Contextual and Judgment-Based

Relevance is not a permanent property attached to a document. It can depend on:

- The information need and task objective.
- The expected audience and level of expertise.
- The document's topical coverage and specificity.
- The requested format, time period, or geographic scope.
- The assessment unit: whole document, passage, answer, or another item.
- The relevance scale and instructions used by the evaluation.

Human assessors can disagree, and the same assessor can make different judgments at different times. Relevance judgments are useful operational labels, not infallible measurements of an objective universal truth. [1][3]

Do not collapse relevance into other dimensions:

| Dimension | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Topical relevance | Does the item address the subject of the information need? |
| Task usefulness | Does it help accomplish the task described? |
| Factual accuracy | Are its claims correct? |
| Authority / trust | Is there a defensible basis for relying on the source? |
| Freshness | Is the information sufficiently current for the task? |
| Completeness | Does it cover the aspects needed to satisfy the task? |

These dimensions may influence a task-specific rubric, but they are not interchangeable. A benchmark must state which dimensions its relevance definition actually includes. [1]

## 2. Relevance Criteria and Assessment Guidelines

Before judging documents, define the criteria. Without a shared rubric, assessors may silently use different definitions of relevance and produce labels that look consistent only because they share a spreadsheet.

A usable assessment guideline should specify:

1. **Information need:** the actual task or question being evaluated.
2. **Assessment unit:** whole document, passage, page, answer, or another defined item.
3. **Relevance dimensions:** the qualities that count for this task.
4. **Judgment scale:** binary labels or clearly defined graded levels.
5. **Inclusion and exclusion rules:** how to treat partial answers, tangential coverage, outdated material, duplicates, and ambiguous cases.
6. **Evidence standard:** what information the assessor may use and what must be visible in the item itself.
7. **Edge-case handling:** how to record uncertainty, inaccessible content, language mismatch, and insufficient context.
8. **Adjudication procedure:** how disagreements are reviewed and resolved, if required.

The rubric should be written before full-scale assessment. Pilot it on a sample of items, inspect disagreements, clarify ambiguous rules, and then apply the revised instructions consistently. This reduces avoidable inconsistency; it does not eliminate subjectivity. The IR textbook describes relevance assessment as a human process and discusses both judgment variation and the need for informative test collections. [1]

### 2.1 Assessment Unit Matters

The item being judged must match the retrieval task:

- **Document-level assessment:** judge an entire article, page, paper, or record.
- **Passage-level assessment:** judge a specific section or passage.
- **Answer-level assessment:** judge whether a generated or retrieved answer satisfies a question.
- **Entity or item assessment:** judge a structured record, product, image, or another non-document item.

A passage can be relevant while the surrounding document contains substantial unrelated material. Conversely, a document may be useful overall even if one extracted passage is incomplete. Do not compare systems that retrieve different units without defining how those units are mapped to the same evaluation target. [1][2]

## 3. Binary and Graded Relevance Judgments

A relevance judgment assigns a label to a query–item pair, or more generally to an information-need–item pair.

### 3.1 Binary Relevance

Binary assessment uses two labels:

- **Relevant (1):** satisfies the defined relevance criterion.
- **Non-relevant (0):** does not satisfy that criterion.

Binary judgments are simple to explain and can support classical evaluation methods. Their weakness is that they flatten degrees of usefulness: a document that barely meets the threshold receives the same label as one that fully answers the task. [1]

### 3.2 Graded Relevance

Graded assessment uses multiple ordered levels. A generic example is:

| Grade | Illustrative interpretation |
|---:|---|
| 3 | Highly relevant: directly and substantially satisfies the information need. |
| 2 | Relevant: provides useful information that addresses the need. |
| 1 | Marginally relevant: has limited or partial value for the need. |
| 0 | Not relevant: does not meaningfully satisfy the need. |

This is an illustrative rubric, not a universal standard. Real projects must define the meaning of each grade for their task. TREC does not use one universal relevance scale. Its general English relevance-judgment guidance describes binary labels, while a TREC 2025 RAG-track paper documents a 0–4 graded scale for its justification-relevance subtask. The scale must therefore be checked at the track and task level; do not generalize one track's rubric to all of TREC. [2][6]

Graded labels preserve more information about the assessor's judgment, but they also demand clearer instructions and can increase disagreement. More labels do not automatically mean better assessment. A four-level scale with vague definitions is just four ways to be inconsistent.

### 3.3 Relevance Is Not the Same as Confidence

If an assessor is uncertain, do not silently turn uncertainty into a low relevance grade. Keep the concepts separate:

- **Relevance grade:** the judgment under the rubric.
- **Confidence / uncertainty:** how certain the assessor is about that judgment.
- **Assessment status:** whether the item was actually assessed.

An evaluation protocol may record confidence or an explicit *uncertain / needs review* state. If it does, define how those records are handled before metric calculation.

## 4. Assessors, Consistency, and Disagreement

Relevance assessment commonly relies on human assessors. Depending on the domain, they may be trained general assessors, subject-matter experts, intended users, or a combination.

### 4.1 Assessor Selection

Select assessors based on the task:

- Use subject-matter expertise when technical correctness or specialist context is essential.
- Use representative users when the task depends on ordinary user needs or language.
- Use trained general assessors when the rubric is clear and specialist knowledge is not required.
- Use multiple assessors for at least a subset of items when disagreement needs to be measured.

A domain expert is not automatically a representative user, and a representative user is not automatically qualified to assess specialist correctness. Match assessor capability to the judgment being requested. [1]

### 4.2 Independent Judgments and Adjudication

A defensible workflow may include:

1. Train assessors with the same written rubric and examples.
2. Run a calibration round on a shared sample.
3. Collect independent judgments before assessors see one another's labels.
4. Measure and inspect disagreement.
5. Adjudicate cases that meet a predefined review rule.
6. Record the final label and preserve the original judgments where auditability matters.

Independent assessment helps reveal genuine ambiguity. If assessors see earlier labels before making their own, those labels can influence subsequent judgments. Adjudication can produce a consistent final qrel set, but it does not prove that the final label is objectively correct.

### 4.3 Inter-Assessor Agreement

**Inter-assessor agreement** measures how consistently different assessors label the same items.

For binary labels, Cohen's kappa is one commonly used chance-corrected agreement statistic for two assessors. It compares observed agreement with the agreement expected from the assessors' marginal label distributions:

`κ = (P_o - P_e) / (1 - P_e)`

Where:

- `P_o` is the observed proportion of agreement.
- `P_e` is the expected agreement under the statistic's chance model.

For multiple assessors or different data types, other agreement statistics may be more appropriate. Agreement statistics must be selected to match the number of assessors, label scale, missingness, and assessment design. [1]

**Important:** agreement is not validity. Assessors can agree and still apply a flawed rubric. Low agreement may signal unclear criteria, ambiguous tasks, insufficient assessor training, or genuine subjectivity. A single kappa threshold should not be treated as a universal pass/fail rule; interpret the statistic in context. [1][3]

## 5. Test Collections: The Foundation of Offline Evaluation

A traditional IR test collection contains three core components:

1. **Document collection:** the set of items that retrieval systems can search.
2. **Topics / information needs:** the tasks or requests used to test the systems.
3. **Relevance judgments:** labels indicating which items satisfy each topic under the assessment criteria.

This three-part structure is central to the Cranfield-style evaluation methodology used in IR research and in TREC-style test collections. [1][2]

### 5.1 Documents

The collection defines the searchable universe for the experiment. Document identifiers must be stable and map to the exact content assessed.

Record relevant collection metadata, such as:

- Collection name and version.
- Document identifiers and canonicalization rules.
- Language, domain, and content type.
- Snapshot or collection date where freshness matters.
- Known exclusions, duplicates, or inaccessible documents.

A retrieval run evaluated against one collection version should not silently be judged against a different version. Otherwise, missing documents or changed content can contaminate the comparison.

### 5.2 Topics and Information Needs

A topic represents the information need used for evaluation. It may include a short title, a natural-language description, and a longer narrative that clarifies what should count as relevant.

A good topic set should reflect the target task and expected usage. Randomly generated keyword combinations are not a substitute for representative information needs. Domain experts and task owners should help ensure that the topics express meaningful requests for the chosen collection. [1][2]

A topic set should consider:

- Common and less frequent request types.
- Different levels of specificity.
- Ambiguous and underspecified queries, where relevant.
- Different languages, domains, or user groups when in scope.
- The intended mix of informational, navigational, transactional, or specialist tasks when applicable.

The correct mix depends on the application. Do not assume that one benchmark topic distribution represents every real search population.

### 5.3 Relevance Judgments and Qrels

A **qrels file** (query relevance judgments) records relevance labels for topic–document pairs. A common TREC-style representation contains:

`topic_id  iteration  document_id  relevance_grade`

The exact serialization and conventions depend on the evaluation toolkit, but the conceptual mapping is consistent: a topic, an item, and its assigned relevance grade. TREC documentation describes relevance judgments as the mapping between topics and documents in a test collection. [2]

Keep three states distinct:

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Relevant | The item was assessed and meets the rubric. |
| Non-relevant | The item was assessed and does not meet the rubric. |
| Unjudged | The item has no recorded assessment for this topic. |

**Unjudged is not automatically the same as non-relevant.** Some traditional evaluation procedures treat unjudged documents as non-relevant for particular calculations, but that is a methodological assumption, not proof that the item was examined and rejected. The distinction becomes critical when judgments are incomplete. [4][5]

## 6. Pooling: Assessing a Manageable Sample

For large collections, judging every document for every topic is usually impractical. **Pooling** reduces assessment cost by selecting a subset of documents for human review.

A common pooling workflow is:

1. Run several retrieval systems against the same collection and topics.
2. Collect the top-ranked results from each run for each topic.
3. Merge those results into a pool.
4. Deduplicate the candidate documents.
5. Have assessors judge the pooled items.
6. Publish the collection, topics, judgments, and evaluation protocol.

TREC-style test collections commonly use pooling because the full document collection is too large for exhaustive relevance assessment. [2]

### 6.1 Why Multiple Systems Contribute

If a pool is built from only one retrieval system, it may mostly contain documents that the system already knows how to find. Including runs from multiple systems can expose assessors to a wider range of candidate documents and reduce dependence on one retrieval strategy. TREC's evaluation materials describe the use of submitted runs and pooling to construct relevance judgments. [2]

### 6.2 Pooling Limitations

Pooling is efficient, but it does not guarantee complete or unbiased judgments.

- Relevant documents that no contributing run retrieves may never enter the pool.
- Shallow pools may omit relevant documents deeper in result lists.
- A small or homogeneous set of contributing systems may provide limited candidate diversity.
- Pooling can favor document characteristics shared by the systems used to construct it.
- Treating every unjudged item as non-relevant can distort comparisons when many relevant items remain unjudged.

Research by Buckley, Dimmick, Soboroff, and Voorhees showed that traditional pooling can introduce bias for very large collections when pools are too small relative to the collection. [5] Buckley and Voorhees also found that common evaluation measures are not robust to substantially incomplete relevance judgments. [4]

### 6.3 Improving Pool Quality

Possible safeguards include:

- Include diverse retrieval systems and retrieval strategies.
- Increase pool depth when the task and budget justify it.
- Add expert-found or independently sampled documents.
- Track the fraction and distribution of unjudged results.
- Reassess the pool when new retrieval methods expose new candidate populations.
- Use evaluation methods designed for incomplete judgments when appropriate.
- Document exactly how the pool was constructed.

These measures reduce specific risks; none makes incomplete judgments magically complete. Pooling assumptions must remain visible in the evaluation report. [4][5]

## 7. Evaluation Frameworks

An evaluation framework specifies how retrieval methods are tested and compared. The framework should match the question being asked.

### 7.1 Cranfield-Style Offline Evaluation

The Cranfield paradigm evaluates systems using a fixed collection, a set of information needs, and relevance judgments. Researchers run systems against the same test materials and compare their outputs using defined measures.

**Strengths:**

- Controlled comparisons under shared conditions.
- Reproducible experiments when the collection, topics, qrels, and system settings are preserved.
- Lower cost than repeated full-scale user studies.
- Useful for isolating the effect of retrieval-method changes.

**Limitations:**

- The test collection may not represent current users or current content.
- Relevance judgments simplify a complex user interaction into item-level labels.
- Incomplete judgments can distort measured effectiveness.
- Optimizing repeatedly against one test collection can overfit the experiment.

The IR textbook describes test collections as a standard foundation for systematic retrieval experiments and discusses their assumptions and limitations. [1]

### 7.2 TREC-Style Community Evaluation

The **Text REtrieval Conference (TREC)**, organized by NIST, provides shared evaluation infrastructure for IR research. It defines tasks or tracks, document collections, topics, submission procedures, relevance assessments, and evaluation methods.

TREC is not one single metric or one fixed search task. Its tracks cover different information-access problems. The TREC 2025 overview describes the conference's goal as developing evaluation infrastructure for large-scale testing of IR technology, including evaluation methods and the materials used to test them. [2]

A TREC-style framework is useful when multiple teams need to compare systems against common test materials. Its results remain specific to the track, collection, topics, judgments, and evaluation protocol.

### 7.3 User-Centered Evaluation

Offline relevance judgments are not a complete measure of user experience. User-centered evaluation may study whether people can accomplish their tasks using the retrieval system.

Possible approaches include:

- **Controlled user studies:** participants perform predefined tasks under observed conditions.
- **Task completion analysis:** measure whether users complete the intended task and how much effort it requires.
- **Qualitative interviews or observation:** identify why results help or fail users.
- **Interaction studies:** examine reformulation, result inspection, and other search behaviors.

User-centered studies can capture task context and interaction factors that static qrels do not represent. They can also be expensive, sensitive to participant selection, and harder to reproduce than fixed offline tests. The IR literature explicitly notes that user utility includes factors such as interface design and responsiveness that basic relevance-ranking evaluation does not measure. [1]

### 7.4 Online Evaluation

Online evaluation studies retrieval behavior in a live or realistic service context. It may use controlled experiments, carefully designed A/B tests, or user-interaction data.

Online observations can reflect real usage, but they introduce confounding factors: interface changes, position effects, user populations, seasonality, and concurrent system changes. Clicks and dwell behavior are not direct, unbiased relevance labels. A click can reflect position or presentation; a non-click can reflect the result snippet or the user's task being satisfied elsewhere.

Use online evaluation when the research question requires live behavior, and define safeguards for experimental validity. Do not treat raw interaction logs as a ready-made qrels set. [1]

## 8. Designing a Defensible IR Evaluation

A strong evaluation begins with a precise research question—not with a favorite metric or an available benchmark.

### 8.1 Define the Task

Specify:

- What information need is being served?
- What kind of item is retrieved?
- Which users or tasks are represented?
- What collection is searchable?
- What counts as relevant?
- Is the system evaluated for candidate retrieval, ranked retrieval, or task completion?

Different retrieval tasks require different assessment units and relevance criteria. [1][2]

### 8.2 Build Representative Test Materials

Prepare the document collection, topic set, and assessment rubric. Keep a clear record of collection versions, topic sources, document IDs, and qrels provenance.

Use a topic set that reflects the intended application. A large number of poorly designed topics does not guarantee a representative benchmark.

### 8.3 Separate Development and Test Data

Use development data to refine system parameters, query processing, assessment rules, or model choices. Reserve a test set for final comparison.

Repeatedly tuning a system against the same test collection can overstate its expected performance. The IR textbook explicitly warns against reporting test results after tuning parameters to maximize performance on that same collection. [1]

### 8.4 Control the Comparison

When comparing retrieval systems, keep the following consistent or explicitly account for differences:

- Collection and collection version.
- Topic set and topic interpretation.
- Relevance rubric and qrels.
- Candidate depth and result format.
- Preprocessing and document identity rules.
- Evaluation cutoffs and metric implementation.
- Hardware and latency measurement conditions, when efficiency is compared.

A comparison is only meaningful if the systems are evaluated under conditions that support the claim being made.

### 8.5 Report Uncertainty and Limitations

An evaluation report should describe:

- Collection and topic coverage.
- Assessor selection and training.
- Relevance scale and assessment instructions.
- Number of assessors and disagreement handling.
- Pool construction and judgment completeness.
- Unjudged-document treatment.
- Development/test separation and parameter tuning.
- Known biases, exclusions, and generalization limits.

A result without methodology is not a result that readers can independently evaluate. It is just a number asking for trust.

## 9. Worked Example: A Small Test Collection

The following is an **illustrative example**, not an empirical experiment.

### Information Need

Find documentation that explains how to reduce memory usage in a Python application.

### Assessment Guideline

- **Grade 2 — Highly relevant:** directly explains diagnosing or reducing Python application memory usage.
- **Grade 1 — Partially relevant:** provides useful Python memory concepts but does not directly address the task.
- **Grade 0 — Not relevant:** does not meaningfully help with the information need.

### Candidate Documents

| Document | Description | Judgment |
|---|---|---:|
| D1 | Python profiling guide with memory allocation analysis and optimization techniques. | 2 |
| D2 | General Python syntax tutorial with no memory discussion. | 0 |
| D3 | Article explaining garbage collection and object lifetimes in Python. | 1 |
| D4 | Guide to reducing memory use in a Java service. | 0 |
| D5 | Python package documentation describing memory profiling tools. | 2 |

The judgments follow the stated rubric. D4 contains the concept of memory optimization but targets a different language and task context, so topical overlap alone does not make it relevant.

A qrels representation for this topic could record the document IDs and grades:

`topic-01 0 D1 2`

`topic-01 0 D2 0`

`topic-01 0 D3 1`

`topic-01 0 D4 0`

`topic-01 0 D5 2`

The example illustrates how an information need, assessment guideline, and item-level judgments become reusable evaluation material. Metric calculation and interpretation are covered in 04.12.

## 10. Common Methodological Failure Modes

| Failure | Why it weakens evaluation | Better practice |
|---|---|---|
| Judging against query words only | Confuses lexical overlap with satisfaction of the information need. | Write an explicit task description and relevance rubric. |
| Vague relevance grades | Assessors interpret labels differently. | Define each grade with inclusion rules and examples. |
| One assessor for every item | Hides disagreement and assessor-specific bias. | Double-assess a meaningful sample and inspect disagreements. |
| Treating agreement as truth | Consistency does not prove that the rubric is valid. | Validate the rubric against the task and intended users. |
| Treating unjudged as known non-relevant | Confuses missing assessment with a negative judgment. | Track unjudged items and state the evaluation assumption. |
| Pooling from one system only | The pool may reflect one system's retrieval bias. | Include diverse runs and independent candidate sources. |
| Shallow or static pools | New or deep relevant items can remain unjudged. | Assess pool coverage and update judgments where feasible. |
| Tuning on the final test set | Inflates reported performance through test-set overfitting. | Tune on development data and preserve a held-out test set. |
| Unrepresentative topics | Results may not generalize to the intended user population. | Sample tasks from the target use case and report coverage. |
| Using clicks as direct relevance labels | Interaction is affected by position, interface, and user behavior. | Treat clicks as behavioral evidence requiring careful interpretation. |
| Reporting scores without protocol | Readers cannot judge validity or reproduce the result. | Publish collection, topics, qrels, assessor, and evaluation details. |

## 11. Relevance to SEO — Evidence Boundary

IR relevance assessment helps explain how researchers test retrieval systems and why relevance labels depend on explicit tasks, rubrics, collections, and assessors.

It does **not** reveal Google's private relevance judgments, evaluator instructions, quality-rating workflows, or ranking evaluation infrastructure. A public IR benchmark is evidence about its documented collection and task—not a window into proprietary Google Search operations.

For SEO-Playbook, keep these ownership boundaries clear:

- **Domain 04.11:** general IR relevance assessment, qrels, test collections, pooling, and evaluation frameworks.
- **Domain 04.12:** formal IR evaluation metrics and their mathematical interpretation.
- **Domain 03.12:** evaluation and testing of search-engine ranking systems.
- **Domain 14:** website-owner SEO analytics and observed organic search performance.
- **Domain 02:** operational search-engine architecture and retrieval pipeline.

Search Console clicks, impressions, CTR, and average position are not substitutes for controlled IR relevance judgments. They describe observed search performance under Google's product and reporting definitions, not a qrels-based experiment.

## Key Takeaways

- Relevance must be judged against a defined information need, not just query-term overlap.
- A relevance rubric must define the task, assessment unit, scale, and edge cases.
- Binary and graded judgments are both useful; neither is universally correct.
- Human assessors can disagree. Agreement measures consistency, not objective truth.
- A classic test collection combines documents, topics, and relevance judgments.
- Qrels record judgments; unjudged items must not be silently described as assessed non-relevant.
- Pooling makes large-scale assessment practical but can produce incomplete or biased judgment sets.
- Offline benchmarks, community evaluation, user studies, and online experiments answer different questions.
- Development and test data must remain separate when tuning retrieval systems.
- IR evaluation evidence must not be misrepresented as disclosure of proprietary search-engine evaluation.

**Bottom line:** Good retrieval evaluation starts before the metric. Define the task, build a defensible collection, make relevance criteria explicit, and document what the judgments can—and cannot—prove.
