# 04.2 — Information Representation & Document Modeling

A retrieval system cannot search information it has not represented in some usable form. Before choosing an index or retrieval model, it needs to decide **what counts as an information item, how that item is structured, and which attributes describe it**.

That is document modeling. It is not the same as tokenizing text, building an inverted index, or deciding which result ranks first. Those are separate problems. Mixing them together creates a conceptual mess—and then people wonder why their search architecture is difficult to reason about.

## 1. What Is Information Representation?

**Information representation** is the process of describing information items in a form that a system can store, compare, retrieve, or otherwise process.

In IR, a representation is a model of an information item for a particular retrieval task. It may preserve the item's text, structure, fields, metadata, relationships, or other attributes. The representation is not necessarily identical to the original resource. It is a task-oriented description of that resource. [1][2]

A source resource might be a web page, a research paper, an email, a product record, an image, or a video. A retrieval system may represent it as:

- A single document with an identifier and content.
- A structured record containing named fields.
- A document with separately represented regions such as title, abstract, and body.
- A collection of smaller retrievable passages.
- A multimodal item with text, image, audio, or other modality-specific information. Multimodal retrieval research studies how such modalities can be represented and combined for retrieval. [5]

These are different modeling choices. None is universally correct. The right choice depends on what users need to retrieve and what the system can reliably represent. [1]

## 2. The Document Is a Modelled Unit, Not a Sacred File Boundary

In IR, a **document** is the unit treated as an information item by a retrieval task. It does not have to correspond one-to-one with a file, URL, database row, or physical object.

For example, a research paper could be modeled as one document. The same paper could also be represented as separate sections or passages if the task requires locating specific evidence inside it. A product catalog might treat each product record as a document, while an email system might treat each message—or each message thread—as its retrievable unit.

The chosen unit affects what the system can return, how relevance is assessed, and how information is organized. Classic IR literature explicitly treats document delineation and the choice of document unit as part of the retrieval problem. [1]

### 2.1 Document Granularity

**Document granularity** describes the size or level of detail of the retrievable unit.

| Granularity | Example | Typical retrieval question |
|---|---|---|
| Collection | A digital library or product catalog | Which collection should be searched? |
| Whole document | An entire report, article, or email | Which document addresses the request? |
| Section or field | A paper's abstract or a product's specification field | Which part of the document contains the needed information? |
| Passage | A paragraph or a short text segment | Which specific passage answers the question? |
| Fine-grained element | A table cell, entity mention, or structured value | Where is this exact piece of information represented? |

This table is a conceptual taxonomy for this playbook, not a universal standard or a claim that every system supports every level.

Smaller units can make precise retrieval possible, but they can also lose context that was available in the parent document. Larger units preserve more surrounding context, but may return material that is only partly relevant. Choosing granularity is therefore a trade-off between precision of localization, context preservation, storage, and task requirements. [1]

**Do not assume passage-level retrieval is automatically better than document-level retrieval.** It is better only when the task benefits from locating smaller units and the system can preserve enough context to interpret them.

## 3. Core Components of a Document Representation

A practical document model may contain several distinct components. The exact schema depends on the collection and task.

### 3.1 Document Identifier

A **document identifier** is a value used to distinguish one retrievable item from other items in the relevant collection.

An identifier may be a database key, an internal document ID, a URI, or another stable reference. Its job is identification—not describing the document's meaning.

Identifiers should not be confused with titles, URLs, or filenames. Those values may change, may not be unique in a collection, or may describe a resource differently from the system's internal identity. A robust model keeps identity separate from descriptive attributes. [2]

### 3.2 Content

**Content** is the information carried by the item and made available to the retrieval task.

For text retrieval, this may include a title, abstract, body text, captions, or other textual regions. For non-text retrieval, it may include image, audio, video, or other modality-specific content and representations. The appropriate representation depends on the retrieval task and modality. [5]

The original resource and the content representation used by the retrieval system are related but not necessarily identical. A system may extract or transform information before it becomes part of the model. The mechanics of parsing web resources belong to Domain 02.5; text normalization and token-level transformations belong to 04.3. [1]

### 3.3 Metadata

**Metadata** is descriptive information about an information resource or item. It can describe properties such as title, creator, subject, date, language, type, format, rights, and relationships to other resources.

The Dublin Core Metadata Initiative (DCMI) defines a vocabulary of metadata terms that includes properties such as `creator`, `date`, `description`, `format`, `identifier`, `language`, `publisher`, `subject`, `title`, and `type`. DCMI also allows its terms to be combined with compatible vocabularies in application profiles. [2]

Metadata can support filtering, grouping, interpretation, and retrieval. But metadata is not automatically accurate just because it exists. Its usefulness depends on how it was created, maintained, normalized, and validated.

### 3.4 Fields and Zones

A **field** is a named attribute or value associated with a document, such as author, publication date, language, category, or document type.

A **zone** is a content region within a document that can contain text, such as the title, abstract, body, or references. The distinction is useful:

- A field often stores a value with a defined type or meaning.
- A zone identifies a region of content that may contain a variable amount of text.

IR literature discusses field- and zone-aware representations because systems may need to search or process different document parts differently. [3]

For example, a scholarly article could have:

| Component | Example value | Representation role |
|---|---|---|
| Identifier | `paper-4821` | Identifies the item |
| Title | `Retrieval Models for Scientific Search` | Text zone and descriptive metadata |
| Author | `A. Researcher` | Metadata field |
| Publication date | `2024-05-12` | Typed metadata field |
| Abstract | A short summary | Text zone |
| Body | Full article text | Text zone |
| Language | `en` | Metadata field |
| Document type | `research-article` | Metadata field |

This is an illustrative schema, not a prescribed DCMI profile or a claim about a production search engine's internal record format.

## 4. Common Document Modeling Approaches

Different collections require different representations. These approaches can also be combined.

### 4.1 Flat Document Model

A flat model treats each item primarily as one content unit, often associated with an identifier and a limited set of attributes.

**Useful when:** documents are relatively uniform and the retrieval task mainly concerns whole-item matching.

**Trade-off:** internal structure may be ignored or underrepresented. A long document with distinct sections can be treated as one undifferentiated block.

### 4.2 Fielded Document Model

A fielded model represents an item as a set of named fields, such as title, author, date, category, or body.

**Useful when:** users need field-specific search, filtering, or interpretation.

**Trade-off:** fields require consistent definitions and data quality. A field named `author` is not useful if one record stores a person's name, another stores an organization, and a third stores an unparsed string with several contributors.

### 4.3 Structured and Hierarchical Model

A structured model preserves relationships between components. A document may contain sections, nested elements, repeated fields, or links to related resources.

XML retrieval is one established IR context in which structure affects retrieval: the system may need to consider both textual content and the structural position of that content. [4]

**Useful when:** the structure itself carries meaning—for example, legal documents, technical manuals, scholarly articles, or structured records.

**Trade-off:** structural complexity can make representation, matching, and result presentation more demanding. Flattening the structure may discard useful context; preserving every detail may add unnecessary complexity.

### 4.4 Passage-Based Model

A passage-based model treats selected segments of a larger document as independently retrievable units, often while retaining a relationship to the parent document.

**Useful when:** the task asks for a specific answer, statement, or evidence located inside a longer resource.

**Trade-off:** a passage can become misleading when separated from its heading, neighboring text, qualifications, or parent document. A passage model should preserve enough parent context to support interpretation and attribution.

### 4.5 Multimodal Model

A multimodal model represents items that contain more than one information modality, such as text and images, audio and transcripts, or video and temporal segments. Research surveys document retrieval methods that combine text, image, audio, and other modalities. [5]

The representation may combine modality-specific content with shared metadata and links between related components. DCMI's type vocabulary, for example, includes resource types such as image, sound, moving image, text, and dataset. That vocabulary describes resource types; it does not prescribe a particular multimodal retrieval architecture. [2]

**Useful when:** the retrieval task must search or connect information across different media.

**Trade-off:** different modalities require different representation methods and evaluation choices. A text-only representation may omit information that is central to an image, audio clip, or video.

## 5. Metadata, Content, and Structure Are Not Interchangeable

These components often work together, but they answer different questions.

| Component | Main question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Content | What information does the item contain? | The body of an article |
| Metadata | What describes the item? | Author, date, language, type |
| Structure | How are the item's parts organized? | Sections, headings, nested elements |
| Identifier | Which item is this? | Internal document ID or URI |
| Retrieval unit | What unit can the system return? | Whole article or one passage |

A title can function as both content and metadata depending on the model. The categories are conceptual roles, not mutually exclusive storage containers.

A common modeling mistake is to assume that metadata automatically communicates the full meaning of a resource. It does not. Metadata provides descriptions and attributes; the content and structure may carry additional information that the metadata does not capture. [2]

Another mistake is to assume that every available attribute should become a retrieval feature. A field should be included because it serves a defined task or system requirement—not because the schema has room for another column.

## 6. Document Representation Is Task-Dependent

There is no single document representation that is optimal for every retrieval problem.

Consider the same technical manual:

- A user searching for the manual may need whole-document retrieval.
- A user looking for a specific troubleshooting procedure may need section or passage retrieval.
- A support team filtering manuals by product model may need structured metadata fields.
- A system searching diagrams may need image representations linked to the relevant sections.

The source resource is the same. The retrieval task changes the useful representation.

This is why document modeling should begin with the retrieval task, the information unit users expect, and the attributes needed to identify or interpret that unit. The representation should preserve information that matters to the task without pretending every possible attribute is equally useful. [1][2]

## 7. What Document Modeling Does Not Decide

Document representation establishes the units and attributes available to later retrieval operations. It does not, by itself, decide how every system processes or ranks those representations.

| Concern | Canonical topic |
|---|---|
| Fetching, parsing, and rendering acquired web resources | 02.5 — Document Processing & Rendering |
| Operational storage and maintenance of a search engine's index | 02.6 — Search Indexing |
| Operational retrieval in a search-engine pipeline | 02.8 — Search Retrieval |
| Tokenization, normalization, stemming, and term-level representation | 04.3 — Text Processing & Term Representation |
| Retrieval-oriented index structures and postings | 04.4 — Information Retrieval Indexing & Data Structures |
| Retrieval models and scoring | 04.5–04.7 |
| Search-engine ranking and ordering | Domain 03 — Search Engine Algorithms |

A document model may define a title field, but it does not establish how much weight a search engine assigns to that field. A representation may expose passages, but it does not prove that a particular production search engine retrieves or ranks passages in a specific way.

Keep representation, processing, indexing, retrieval, and ranking conceptually distinct. They interact, but they are not synonyms.

## 8. Why This Matters to SEO

SEO content exists inside resources that search systems may process and represent. Understanding document modeling helps explain why a page is not necessarily the only meaningful unit of information and why metadata, content regions, and structure serve different roles.

It also helps avoid several unsupported assumptions:

- A visible page is not proof of the exact internal document representation used by a search engine.
- A structured field in a website's markup does not guarantee that a search engine stores or uses it in a particular way.
- A heading or content section may help describe a document's organization, but that alone does not prove a specific ranking effect.
- A general IR model can explain representation choices without revealing Google's or Bing's proprietary document schemas.

For Google-specific crawling, rendering, and processing behavior, use the relevant official documentation and Domain 02.5. For structured data implementation and search appearance, use Domain 10 and Domain 06 respectively. Domain 04 stays focused on the general IR concepts.

## Key Takeaways

- Information representation describes information items in a form that a retrieval task can use.
- A document is a task-defined retrievable unit, not necessarily a file, URL, or database row.
- Document granularity ranges from whole resources to passages and smaller elements.
- A useful document model can separate identifiers, content, metadata, fields, and structure.
- Flat, fielded, hierarchical, passage-based, and multimodal models address different retrieval needs.
- Metadata describes a resource; it does not automatically capture all of its content or meaning.
- Document modeling is task-dependent and involves trade-offs between context, precision, structure, and complexity.
- Representation does not determine tokenization, index construction, retrieval scoring, or ranking behavior.

**Bottom line:** decide what the information item is and what must be preserved before arguing about how to retrieve it. A retrieval model cannot rescue a document representation that discarded the information the task needed.
