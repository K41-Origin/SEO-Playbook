# 04.6 — Vector Space Model & Term Weighting

## 1. What the Vector Space Model Solves

Boolean retrieval answers a binary question: does a document satisfy the query expression or not? That is useful when constraints are explicit, but it does not distinguish a document that matches one query term from a document that matches several important terms.

The **Vector Space Model (VSM)** represents documents and queries as weighted vectors in a shared term space. A retrieval system can then calculate a score for each query-document pair and order documents by that score. The model therefore supports ranked retrieval rather than only set membership. [1]

The central idea is simple:

- Each distinct term in the vocabulary defines a dimension.
- Each document receives a numeric weight for each term.
- A query is represented in the same dimensions.
- A similarity or scoring function compares the query vector with document vectors.
- Documents with higher scores are placed earlier in the ranked result set.

This is a classical lexical retrieval model. It is not a complete model of human relevance, and it is not a description of Google's proprietary ranking system.

## 2. From Terms to Vectors

Assume a collection has a vocabulary:

`V = {t_1, t_2, ..., t_m}`

Each term is one dimension in an `m`-dimensional vector space. A document can be represented as:

`d = (w_{1,d}, w_{2,d}, ..., w_{m,d})`

where `w_{i,d}` is the weight assigned to term `t_i` in document `d`.

A query uses the same vocabulary and dimensional structure:

`q = (w_{1,q}, w_{2,q}, ..., w_{m,q})`

The shared space matters. A document vector and query vector can only be compared meaningfully when their dimensions refer to the same term representation and vocabulary. The term vocabulary and text-analysis choices established in [04.3 — Text Processing & Term Representation](04.3-Text-Processing-and-Term-Representation.md) therefore affect the vectors built by the model. [1]

### 2.1 Bag-of-Words Representation

The classical VSM commonly uses a **bag-of-words** representation. It records which terms occur and how often or how strongly they are weighted, but it does not preserve their original order or grammatical relationships.

For example, under a basic bag-of-words representation:

- `"Mary is quicker than John"`
- `"John is quicker than Mary"`

can produce the same term-frequency vector.

That is a real limitation, not a minor implementation detail. A bag-of-words vector can capture lexical overlap and term distribution, but it does not inherently understand word order, syntax, negation, or the semantic relationship between terms. Phrase and positional retrieval require additional representation and matching mechanisms. [1]

### 2.2 Sparse Vectors

A document's vocabulary may contain millions of possible terms, while any one document contains only a small fraction of them. Most vector dimensions are therefore zero. Such representations are **sparse vectors**.

An implementation usually stores non-zero term-weight pairs rather than allocating a dense array for every document. Inverted indexes and postings lists provide structures that can support efficient accumulation of scores for documents containing query terms; see [04.4 — Information Retrieval Indexing & Data Structures](04.4-Information-Retrieval-Indexing-and-Data-Structures.md). [1]

## 3. Term Frequency (TF)

**Term frequency (TF)** measures how often a term occurs in a document. The simplest form is raw frequency:

`tf(t,d) = f_{t,d}`

where `f_{t,d}` is the number of occurrences of term `t` in document `d`.

The basic intuition is that a term appearing repeatedly may be more representative of the document's subject than a term appearing once. But raw frequency has a weakness: it grows with repetition and can over-reward long documents or repeated wording. [1]

TF is not restricted to one formula. Common weighting choices include:

| TF scheme | Example definition | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Binary | `tf = 1` if the term occurs, otherwise `0` | Records presence, ignores repetition. |
| Raw frequency | `tf = f_{t,d}` | Increases linearly with every occurrence. |
| Logarithmic | `tf = 1 + log(f_{t,d})` for `f_{t,d} > 0`, otherwise `0` | Gives diminishing additional weight to repeated occurrences. |
| Normalized frequency | Frequency divided or adjusted by a document-length statistic | Reduces some effects of document length; exact normalization varies. |

These are alternative design choices, not interchangeable formulas with identical behavior. A model must define which TF function it uses. [1]

**Important:** more repetitions do not imply an unlimited increase in relevance. TF is a representation weight, not a quality judgment or a recommendation to repeat keywords.

## 4. Document Frequency (DF) and Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

TF looks inside one document. **Document frequency (DF)** looks across the collection.

`df_t =` number of documents in the collection that contain term `t`.

Let `N` be the number of documents in the collection. The classical inverse document frequency formula is:

`idf(t) = log(N / df_t)`

A term appearing in many documents has a lower IDF. A term appearing in relatively few documents has a higher IDF. IDF therefore reduces the influence of terms that are common across the collection and gives more weight to terms with greater collection-level discriminating power. [1]

### 4.1 Why DF, Not Just Total Term Occurrences?

Collection frequency counts every occurrence of a term across the entire collection. Document frequency counts how many distinct documents contain it.

Those statistics answer different questions. A term can occur many times in a small number of documents, while another term can occur once in a very large number of documents. For distinguishing documents, DF directly measures how widely the term is distributed across documents. [1]

### 4.2 IDF Is Collection-Dependent

IDF depends on the collection and its document statistics. If the collection changes, a term's DF and IDF can change too.

The textbook formula above is a classical baseline, not the only possible implementation. Systems may use smoothing, offsets, different logarithm bases, field-specific statistics, or other variants. For example, Lucene's documented TF-IDF implementation uses a smoothed IDF expression based on `docFreq` and `docCount`, rather than the bare textbook expression. [1][2]

Do not compare raw IDF values from different systems without checking their formulas, collection definitions, and statistics.

## 5. TF-IDF Weighting

**TF-IDF** combines within-document frequency with collection-level rarity:

`tfidf(t,d) = tf(t,d) × idf(t)`

The weight tends to be higher when a term occurs relatively often in a document but appears in relatively few documents across the collection. It tends to be lower when the term is rare in the document or common throughout the collection. [1]

The two components contribute different information:

- **TF:** How strongly is the term represented in this document?
- **IDF:** How useful is the term for distinguishing documents in this collection?
- **TF-IDF:** What weight results when those two signals are combined?

### 5.1 A Small Illustrative Example

Assume a collection contains four documents. The term `solar` occurs in three documents, while `photovoltaic` occurs in one.

Using the classical formula with base-2 logarithms:

- `idf(solar) = log₂(4/3) ≈ 0.415`
- `idf(photovoltaic) = log₂(4/1) = 2`

If each term occurs twice in a particular document and raw TF is used:

- `tfidf(solar,d) = 2 × 0.415 = 0.83`
- `tfidf(photovoltaic,d) = 2 × 2 = 4`

The rarer term receives a larger weight in this illustrative collection. These values are not universal constants: changing the collection, TF function, smoothing method, or IDF formula changes the result. The example demonstrates the weighting mechanism, not an empirical claim about search-engine behavior. [1]

### 5.2 Query-Term Weights

A query can also be represented with term weights. One simple scheme assigns a query term a binary weight or a TF-based weight; another uses IDF or TF-IDF-like weights. The query and document weighting schemes do not have to be identical, but both vectors must use compatible dimensions and a clearly defined scoring method. The textbook illustrates a VSM configuration in which query terms are weighted using IDF while document terms use normalized TF. [1]

There is no single mandatory query-weight formula shared by every VSM implementation.

## 6. Document Length Normalization

Raw term counts are influenced by document length. A longer document has more opportunities to contain a term and accumulate a larger raw TF score.

A common response is to normalize document vectors by their Euclidean length:

`||d||₂ = sqrt(Σᵢ wᵢ,d²)`

The normalized vector is:

`d̂ = d / ||d||₂`

This scales a non-zero vector to unit length. It reduces the influence of vector magnitude so comparisons focus more on the relative distribution of term weights. [1]

Normalization is a modeling choice with trade-offs. It can reduce document-length effects, but it can also remove information about absolute term evidence. Practical systems may use different length-normalization functions rather than strict unit-vector normalization. Lucene's TF-IDF documentation explicitly describes its own field-length normalization as a refinement of the textbook cosine formulation. [2]

A zero vector has no defined unit direction because its norm is zero. An implementation must handle that case explicitly rather than divide by zero.

## 7. Similarity and Scoring

### 7.1 Dot Product

For query vector `q` and document vector `d`, the dot product is:

`q · d = Σᵢ wᵢ,q × wᵢ,d`

Only dimensions where both vectors have non-zero weights contribute to the sum. With non-negative term weights, larger shared weighted components generally produce a larger dot product. [1]

However, a raw dot product is affected by vector magnitudes. Longer documents or larger overall weights can receive larger values even when their term distributions are similar.

### 7.2 Cosine Similarity

Cosine similarity compares the angle between two vectors:

`cosine(q,d) = (q · d) / (||q||₂ × ||d||₂)`

For non-zero vectors with non-negative term weights, cosine similarity ranges from 0 to 1:

- **1:** vectors point in the same direction.
- **0:** vectors are orthogonal; they share no positive-weight dimensions.
- **Between 0 and 1:** vectors share some weighted dimensions, with similarity depending on their relative distribution.

The denominator normalizes vector lengths. This makes cosine similarity less sensitive to overall magnitude than an unnormalized dot product. It does not make document length, term weighting, or representation choices irrelevant; those choices still determine the vectors being compared. [1]

### 7.3 From Score to Ranked Retrieval

A basic VSM retrieval procedure can be described as:

1. Analyze the query using a representation compatible with the indexed documents.
2. Build the query vector and determine its term weights.
3. Identify candidate documents with non-zero contributions for query dimensions.
4. Accumulate query-document dot products or another defined similarity score.
5. Apply the chosen normalization.
6. Sort documents by descending score and return the top results. [1]

The inverted index can support this process by providing postings for query terms and allowing score contributions to be accumulated without scanning every term in every document. Efficient top-`K` scoring and implementation optimizations are developed further in the textbook's Chapter 7; this topic focuses on the model and weighting foundations. [1]

A high VSM score means a stronger match under the chosen representation and scoring function. It does **not** prove that a document is objectively relevant, accurate, trustworthy, useful, or best for the user's information need. Relevance remains task-dependent and requires evaluation against appropriate judgments.

## 8. Worked Conceptual Flow

Consider the query:

`q = "solar panel efficiency"`

A basic lexical VSM process might:

1. Use the text-analysis rules from 04.3 to derive the query terms.
2. Map those terms into the collection vocabulary.
3. Assign query weights using the selected query-weighting scheme.
4. Retrieve the postings for the query terms from the inverted index.
5. Build or accumulate weighted document representations for candidate documents.
6. Calculate each candidate's score using dot product or cosine similarity.
7. Return candidates in descending score order.

A document containing all three query terms may receive a high score, but a document containing only two terms can still score well if those terms have substantial weights and align strongly with the query vector. Unlike strict Boolean AND, the basic vector-space scoring approach does not inherently require every query term to appear in a document. [1]

This is the key conceptual transition from 04.5: **Boolean retrieval tests logical membership; VSM estimates a degree of lexical match.**

## 9. What the Classical VSM Does Not Capture

The classical bag-of-words VSM is useful, but it has clear limits:

| Limitation | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Word order is discarded | Different word sequences can produce the same vector. |
| Syntax and relations are not modeled | Negation, roles, and grammatical relationships may be lost. |
| Vocabulary mismatch | Synonyms and semantically related terms do not automatically share a dimension. |
| Collection dependence | IDF weights change when collection statistics change. |
| Term repetition can distort weights | Raw TF can overemphasize repeated terms without suitable weighting or normalization. |
| Score is model-relative | A higher score is not a universal measure of human relevance or content quality. |
| Sparse lexical matching | Documents with no shared represented terms may receive no lexical similarity despite being conceptually related. |

These limitations motivate other retrieval approaches, including probabilistic models, relevance feedback, query expansion, semantic retrieval, and hybrid methods. Those methods have their own assumptions and failure modes; they are not automatic upgrades in every task.

## 10. VSM, Boolean Retrieval, and Probabilistic Retrieval

| Dimension | Boolean retrieval | Vector Space Model | Probabilistic retrieval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core representation | Term presence and set membership | Weighted term vectors | Probability-based relevance or term evidence |
| Matching/scoring | Logical conditions | Similarity or vector score | Probabilistic scoring under model assumptions |
| Result behavior | Matching set; no inherent relevance order | Ranked by vector-space score | Ranked by probabilistic score |
| Partial lexical match | Only if query logic allows it | Can receive a non-zero score | Depends on the probabilistic model |
| Main assumptions | Explicit Boolean expression | Shared term dimensions and weighting scheme | Defined probabilistic assumptions and statistics |

This table is a conceptual comparison, not a claim that production systems must choose only one model. Systems can combine retrieval methods, filters, and scoring stages. [1]

The next topic, 04.7 — Probabilistic Information Retrieval Models, covers probabilistic relevance estimation and BM25. Do not label BM25 as TF-IDF or treat it as simply another cosine formula.

## 11. Implementation Example: Apache Lucene

Apache Lucene documents `TFIDFSimilarity` as an implementation of the Vector Space Model and describes how its scoring formula relates to TF, IDF, vector similarity, field-length normalization, and query boosts. Its `ClassicSimilarity` is a concrete implementation choice, not the definition of every VSM system. [2]

This distinction matters:

- The textbook model defines the conceptual representation and scoring framework.
- A library chooses specific TF, IDF, normalization, and query-weight functions.
- A production search engine may use different or additional models and features.

Lucene's documented scoring behavior should therefore be cited as Lucene behavior, not generalized to Google, Bing, or all retrieval systems. [2]

## 12. Why This Matters to SEO

VSM provides a useful theoretical explanation of lexical retrieval: terms can contribute different weights, collection statistics can affect their importance, and documents can be scored by their weighted overlap with a query.

But the evidence boundary matters:

- TF-IDF is a classical IR weighting scheme, not a verified Google ranking formula.
- Keyword repetition does not guarantee better rankings. TF is only one component of a retrieval representation and can be transformed or normalized.
- A high lexical similarity score does not establish content quality, factual accuracy, authority, usefulness, or search intent satisfaction.
- The classical bag-of-words model does not fully represent semantic meaning, word order, or context.
- Do not infer Google's internal query-document scoring from a textbook, a Lucene implementation, or a third-party tool's similarity score.

Use Domain 03 for search-engine ranking systems and query-document relevance behavior. Use Domain 05 for SEO keyword research and search intent. Domain 04.6 explains the general vector-space retrieval model—not a shortcut for reverse-engineering proprietary ranking systems.

## Key Takeaways

- The Vector Space Model represents queries and documents as weighted vectors in a shared term space.
- TF captures within-document term frequency; IDF captures how widely a term appears across the collection.
- TF-IDF combines those two signals, but its exact formula depends on the selected weighting scheme.
- Document-length normalization can reduce magnitude effects, but normalization choices have trade-offs.
- Dot product measures weighted overlap; cosine similarity normalizes by vector lengths and compares vector direction.
- VSM supports ranked retrieval because partial matches can receive different scores.
- A VSM score measures match strength under a specific representation and scoring function—not objective relevance or content quality.
- Classical bag-of-words VSM loses word order, syntax, and semantic relationships.
- Textbook VSM theory and a library's scoring implementation must not be presented as proof of a proprietary search engine's internal formula.

**Bottom line:** VSM moves retrieval beyond “match or no match” by assigning weighted scores to query-document pairs. Useful? Absolutely. A complete model of meaning or Google's ranking system? No. Let's not confuse a foundational model with a magic SEO formula.
