# 04.7 — Probabilistic Information Retrieval Models

## 1. Purpose and Scope

Boolean retrieval determines whether a document satisfies a logical expression. The Vector Space Model (VSM) assigns a score based on weighted term vectors. Probabilistic Information Retrieval (PIR) models uncertainty about whether a document is relevant to a query and uses the resulting estimates to rank documents.

For query \(q\) and document \(d\), the central target is:

\[
P(R=1 \mid d,q)
\]

where \(R=1\) denotes that \(d\) is relevant to the information need represented by \(q\). This is a model-based estimate, not a guarantee of relevance or automatically a calibrated real-world probability. [1]

Probabilistic IR is a family of models, not one algorithm. This topic focuses on the Probability Ranking Principle (PRP), Binary Independence Model (BIM), and BM25. [1]

## 2. Probability Foundations

Bayes' rule connects prior belief, observed evidence, and updated probability:

\[
P(R=1 \mid E)=\frac{P(E \mid R=1)P(R=1)}{P(E)}
\]

Here, \(E\) is evidence about a document-query pair. A prior is the probability before considering that evidence; a likelihood describes how probable the evidence is under a relevance assumption; and a posterior is the updated probability after considering it. [1]

The odds of relevance are:

\[
O(R=1)=\frac{P(R=1)}{P(R=0)}
\]

Odds are a monotonic transformation of probability. Ranking by posterior odds therefore preserves the same order as ranking by posterior probability for the same query and relevance event. This is useful because a model may be able to calculate a ranking-equivalent quantity more easily than a fully normalized posterior. [1]

## 3. Probability Ranking Principle (PRP)

The **Probability Ranking Principle** says that, under its basic assumptions, documents should be ordered by decreasing estimated probability of relevance:

\[
score(d,q) \propto P(R=1 \mid d,q)
\]

The classical optimality result assumes that relevance probabilities are estimated correctly, document relevance events are independent, and a simple 1/0 loss applies: retrieving a non-relevant document and failing to retrieve a relevant document each incur the same unit cost. Under those assumptions, sorting by decreasing relevance probability minimizes expected loss. The independence assumption matters when a result set contains duplicates or near-duplicates, because relevance among those documents is not independent. [1]

PRP is a decision principle, not a complete retrieval algorithm. It does not prescribe how to estimate the probabilities; BIM is one model that provides a way to derive a ranking function.

The assumptions matter. True relevance probabilities are unknown in practice, judgments are incomplete, relevance varies by user and task, document relevance can be dependent (for example, near-duplicate results), and retrieval errors may have different costs. The textbook explicitly notes that the optimality result depends on probabilities being known correctly and that document-independence is a simplifying assumption that can fail in practice. [1]

When false positives and false negatives have different costs, the decision rule must account for those costs or utilities. A medical evidence search and a casual product search should not automatically treat every retrieval error as equally costly. [1]

## 4. Binary Independence Model (BIM)

The **Binary Independence Model** is a classical probabilistic retrieval model. It represents documents and queries as binary term-incidence vectors:

\[
x_t =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if term }t\text{ occurs in the document}\\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

The model assumes term occurrences are conditionally independent given the relevance class and query. This lets it approximate a joint term probability as a product of term-level probabilities. [1]

That assumption is mathematically convenient, but natural-language terms are often dependent. For example, "New" and "York" or "credit" and "card" are not independent in ordinary text. BIM's independence is a modeling simplification, not a claim about language. [1]

### 4.1 Term Probabilities and Weights

For each query term \(t\), define:

- \(p_t=P(x_t=1 \mid R=1,q)\): probability that the term appears in a relevant document.
- \(u_t=P(x_t=1 \mid R=0,q)\): probability that the term appears in a non-relevant document.

A term is useful evidence when it is more likely to occur in relevant than in non-relevant documents. Its log-odds contribution is:

\[
c_t=\log\frac{p_t(1-u_t)}{u_t(1-p_t)}
\]

The BIM Retrieval Status Value (RSV), ignoring terms constant for a particular query, is:

\[
RSV_d=\sum_{t\in q,\ x_t=1}c_t
\]

The model adds the evidence weights of query terms present in the document. [1]

### 4.2 Estimation and Smoothing

In principle, \(p_t\) and \(u_t\) can be estimated from relevance judgments by counting term occurrences in relevant and non-relevant documents. In practice, judgments are often sparse. A term may occur in every known relevant document or none, creating zero or one estimates that make log odds undefined.

**Smoothing** addresses this by adding pseudocounts to observed counts. The classical BIM treatment describes adding \(0.5\) to contingency-table counts as a simple estimate. Smoothing prevents extreme estimates from small samples and keeps the scoring calculation usable. [1]

### 4.3 Connection to IDF

When relevance judgments are unavailable, a practical approximation estimates non-relevant term statistics from collection-wide document frequency, under the assumption that relevant documents are a small fraction of the collection. The textbook derivation produces an IDF-like term contribution. [1]

This provides a probabilistic motivation for IDF, but the concepts are not identical:

- VSM IDF is a collection-statistics term weight.
- BIM derives term weights from relevance and non-relevance probabilities under independence assumptions.
- Under particular approximations, BIM weights can resemble IDF.

Similar-looking formulas do not mean the models have identical foundations.

## 5. BM25: From Binary Presence to Term Counts

BIM treats a term as present or absent. Full-text retrieval can also use how often a term occurs and how long the document is.

**BM25**, also called Okapi BM25, is a non-binary probabilistic weighting approach that incorporates term-frequency saturation and document-length normalization. It was developed to make probabilistic scoring more suitable for full-text documents than a purely binary representation. [1]

BM25 is not simply TF-IDF with a new label. It uses a different term-frequency function and document-length normalization structure.

## 6. BM25 Scoring Function

A common BM25 form is:

\[
score(d,q)=\sum_{t\in q} IDF(t)\cdot
\frac{f(t,d)(k_1+1)}
{f(t,d)+k_1(1-b+b\cdot |d|/avgdl)}
\]

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(f(t,d)\) | Frequency of term \(t\) in document \(d\) |
| \(|d|\) | Document length in the model's indexed units |
| \(avgdl\) | Average document length in the collection |
| \(k_1\) | Controls term-frequency saturation |
| \(b\) | Controls the strength of document-length normalization |
| \(IDF(t)\) | Inverse document frequency weight |

This is a common form, not a universal implementation contract. IDF expressions, field statistics, query-term treatment, token-length definitions, and defaults can differ. [1][2]

### 6.1 Term-Frequency Saturation: \(k_1\)

BM25's term-frequency contribution increases with frequency but approaches a limit rather than growing linearly forever.

- Lower \(k_1\): the contribution saturates sooner.
- Higher \(k_1\): term frequency has more influence before saturation.
- \(k_1=0\): the document term-frequency component is independent of frequency in the common formula.

The idea is that repeated evidence can matter, but each additional occurrence should not necessarily count as much as the first. [1][2]

### 6.2 Document-Length Normalization: \(b\)

The \(b\) parameter controls the influence of document length:

- \(b=0\): no document-length normalization in the common formula.
- \(b=1\): full normalization relative to document length in that formula.
- Values between 0 and 1: partial normalization.

The model compares document length with the collection's average length. The appropriate setting depends on the collection and retrieval task; it is not a universal constant. [1][2]

### 6.3 IDF Variants

The textbook presents this common BM25 IDF expression:

\[
IDF(t)=\log\frac{N-df_t+0.5}{df_t+0.5}
\]

where \(N\) is the number of documents and \(df_t\) is the number containing term \(t\). This expression can become negative for terms occurring in more than half the collection. [1]

Implementations may use a shifted or smoothed alternative. Apache Lucene 9.9.1 documents:

\[
IDF(t)=\log\left(1+\frac{docCount-docFreq+0.5}{docFreq+0.5}\right)
\]

Lucene's expression is not identical to the unshifted textbook expression. Its documented defaults are \(k_1=1.2\) and \(b=0.75\). Those are Lucene 9.9.1 defaults, not universal BM25 constants. [2]

### 6.4 Query-Term Frequency

Some BM25 variants include an additional query-term-frequency factor, often parameterized by \(k_3\). It can matter for longer queries where terms repeat. For short queries, implementations commonly omit or simplify this component. The textbook describes both document-term and optional query-term weighting. [1]

## 7. Worked Conceptual Example

Consider the query "solar panel". Suppose candidate A contains "solar" three times and "panel" twice, while candidate B contains each term once. A also has 500 indexed terms; B has 100. Assume the collection average document length is 300.

A's higher term frequencies can increase its score, but its above-average length also applies a stronger normalization denominator. B's lower length reduces that denominator, while its lower term frequencies contribute less. The final ordering depends on the exact IDF values, BM25 parameters, and collection statistics.

This is intentionally qualitative. Without a collection size, document frequencies, selected IDF formula, and parameter values, numeric scores would be fake precision. [1][2]

## 8. BIM, BM25, and VSM Compared

| Dimension | BIM | BM25 | Vector Space Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Probabilistic relevance model | Probabilistic weighting approach | Geometric vector-space model |
| Term representation | Binary term presence | Term counts and length statistics | Weighted term vectors |
| TF behavior | Presence/absence | Saturating contribution | Depends on chosen TF function |
| Length handling | Not central in basic binary formulation | Explicit normalization | Often vector normalization |
| Scoring | Log-odds evidence | IDF-weighted normalized TF | Dot product or cosine similarity |

The distinction is conceptual. Systems can share indexing and score-accumulation infrastructure while using different scoring formulas. The textbook explicitly notes that probabilistic and vector-space retrieval systems need not differ in their overall retrieval architecture. [1]

## 9. Implementation Example: Apache Lucene

Apache Lucene exposes BM25 through its BM25Similarity class. Its 9.9.1 API documents \(k_1\) as the term-frequency saturation parameter, \(b\) as the document-length normalization parameter, an IDF expression based on document frequency and document count, and default values \(k_1=1.2\), \(b=0.75\). [2]

This is Lucene behavior, not a universal BM25 specification. Other libraries and search platforms may use different defaults, IDF variants, field-length definitions, query processing, or score combinations.

A Lucene score is a score under Lucene's implementation and configuration. It is not directly comparable with another engine's score unless the scoring definitions and statistics are aligned.

## 10. Limitations and Failure Modes

| Limitation | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Imperfect relevance judgments | Labels may be incomplete, subjective, or biased. |
| Independence assumptions | BIM ignores dependencies between terms. |
| Sparse observations | Small judgment sets can produce unstable estimates. |
| Smoothing choices | Different estimates can change weights and rankings. |
| Collection dependence | IDF and length statistics depend on the indexed collection. |
| Parameter sensitivity | BM25 behavior depends on \(k_1\), \(b\), and implementation choices. |
| Vocabulary mismatch | Lexical models do not automatically recognize synonyms or semantic equivalence. |
| Score interpretation | A ranking score is not automatically a calibrated probability. |
| Task-dependent relevance | Relevance varies with user goals and context. |

BM25 is an influential lexical retrieval baseline, not a semantic understanding engine. It cannot infer conceptual equivalence when the representation provides no matching evidence.

## 11. Probability, Ranking Score, and Relevance Are Different

Keep three concepts separate:

1. **Probability estimate:** a model's estimate of the chance that a document is relevant under its assumptions and evidence.
2. **Ranking score:** a value used to order documents, such as log odds, RSV, or BM25.
3. **Human relevance judgment:** an assessment of whether the document satisfies an information need.

A ranking score may preserve an ordering without being a probability on the 0–1 scale. BM25 scores, in particular, should not be described as literal probabilities. [1][2]

## 12. Relevance to SEO — Evidence Boundary

Probabilistic retrieval explains how a lexical retrieval system can estimate document-query match using term evidence, collection statistics, and document-length behavior. It does not reveal the internals of Google Search or another proprietary engine.

- BM25 is a well-established IR model, not a verified public formula for Google's organic ranking.
- A BM25 score from an SEO tool or open-source library is not a Google ranking score.
- More keyword repetition is not a reliable ranking strategy. BM25's TF component saturates rather than rewarding unlimited repetition.
- Document-length normalization is a model component, not a rule that longer or shorter SEO content is inherently better.
- Term statistics are collection-dependent; scores can change when the indexed corpus changes.
- A lexical retrieval score does not establish factual accuracy, trustworthiness, usefulness, or search-intent satisfaction.

Use Domain 03 for search-engine ranking systems and ranking behavior. Use Domain 05 for SEO keyword research and search intent. Domain 04.7 documents general probabilistic retrieval theory—not a shortcut for reverse-engineering proprietary search engines.

## Key Takeaways

- Probabilistic IR models uncertainty about whether a document is relevant to a query.
- PRP recommends ordering documents by estimated probability of relevance under specified loss and estimation assumptions.
- BIM uses binary term presence and assumes conditional independence between terms.
- BIM derives term weights from relevance and non-relevance probabilities; smoothing prevents zero-probability estimates.
- IDF has a probabilistic connection, but it is not identical to the full BIM model.
- BM25 adds term-frequency saturation and document-length normalization.
- \(k_1\) controls TF saturation; \(b\) controls document-length normalization.
- BM25 formulas, IDF variants, defaults, and query-term handling differ across implementations.
- A BM25 score is a model-specific ranking value—not a calibrated probability, human relevance judgment, or verified Google ranking score.

**Bottom line:** Probabilistic retrieval gives ranking a formal way to reason about relevance under uncertainty. BM25 turns that foundation into a practical lexical scoring method. Useful model? Yes. Universal explanation of modern search ranking? Absolutely not.
