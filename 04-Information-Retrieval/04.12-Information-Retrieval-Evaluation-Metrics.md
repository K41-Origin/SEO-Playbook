# 04.12 — Information Retrieval Evaluation Metrics

> A metric is a measurement choice, not a verdict. Pick the wrong one and your system can look brilliant while failing the actual task.

## Purpose and Scope

Information Retrieval (IR) evaluation metrics quantify how well a retrieval system finds relevant items and, when results are ranked, how effectively it orders them. The right metric depends on the retrieval task, relevance labels, result depth, and what the user needs from the result list. [1][2]

This topic covers:

- Set-based effectiveness: Precision, Recall, and F-measure.
- Cutoff-based metrics: Precision@k, Recall@k, and R-precision.
- Ranked-list metrics: Average Precision (AP), Mean Average Precision (MAP), and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).
- Graded-relevance metrics: Cumulative Gain (CG), Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG), and normalized DCG (nDCG).
- Metrics designed for incomplete judgments or particular user models, including bpref, Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR), and Rank-Biased Precision (RBP).
- Query-level aggregation, metric selection, interpretation, and common evaluation errors.

This document defines the metrics and explains when they are useful. **04.11 — Relevance Assessment & Evaluation Frameworks** owns how relevance labels, qrels, assessors, test collections, and evaluation experiments are created. A metric cannot repair a weak judgment set. [1][4]

## 1. Before Calculating a Metric

A metric is only interpretable when its inputs and assumptions are explicit.

For a query (q), define:

- (D_q): the evaluation collection for the query.
- (Rel_q): the set of documents judged relevant under the task's relevance threshold.
- (R_q): the system's ranked result list for the query.
- (rel_q(i)): a binary relevance indicator for the item at rank (i), usually 1 if relevant and 0 otherwise.
- (g_q(i)): a graded relevance label for the item at rank (i), when the assessment uses multiple grades.
- (k): a rank cutoff, such as 5, 10, or 100.

For binary metrics, the relevance threshold must be stated. A graded qrels set may be converted to binary labels by declaring a minimum grade relevant—for example, grade 2 or higher. That threshold is a task decision, not a universal property of the metric. Different thresholds can change system comparisons. [2][4]

### 1.1 What Is Being Measured?

Keep three questions separate:

1. **Coverage:** Did the system retrieve the relevant items that exist in the collection?
2. **Early precision:** How many of the first results are relevant?
3. **Ordering quality:** Are the most useful items placed ahead of less useful ones?

Precision and Recall mainly describe coverage and set quality. Cutoff metrics focus on a chosen result depth. AP/MAP and MRR evaluate aspects of ranked order under binary judgments. DCG/nDCG evaluate ranked order using graded relevance. No single metric answers all three questions equally well. [1][2]

### 1.2 Confusion-Matrix Terms

For binary relevance judgments:

| | Relevant | Non-relevant |
|---|---:|---:|
| Retrieved | True Positive (TP) | False Positive (FP) |
| Not retrieved | False Negative (FN) | True Negative (TN) |

- **TP:** relevant items retrieved.
- **FP:** non-relevant items retrieved.
- **FN:** relevant items not retrieved.
- **TN:** non-relevant items not retrieved.

IR collections often contain vastly more non-relevant than relevant items. This is why raw classification accuracy can be misleading: a system that retrieves nothing may classify almost everything as non-relevant and still achieve a superficially high accuracy score. Precision and Recall focus on the relevant-result problem instead. [1]

## 2. Precision and Recall

Precision and Recall are foundational set-based metrics. They do not account for the order of results; they evaluate the retrieved set as a whole. [1]

### 2.1 Precision

**Precision** is the fraction of retrieved items that are relevant:

[
P = rac{TP}{TP+FP}
= rac{|R_q cap Rel_q|}{|R_q|}
]

Interpretation: of everything the system returned, how much was relevant?

High Precision means fewer non-relevant items among the retrieved set. It does not tell us whether the system missed many other relevant items.

### 2.2 Recall

**Recall** is the fraction of all known relevant items that the system retrieved:

[
Recall = rac{TP}{TP+FN}
= rac{|R_q cap Rel_q|}{|Rel_q|}
]

Interpretation: of all relevant items known in the evaluation collection, how many did the system find?

High Recall means the system found a large share of the known relevant set. It does not guarantee that the first results are useful or that the result list contains few irrelevant items.

### 2.3 Precision–Recall Trade-off

Retrieving more documents can increase Recall because more relevant items may be found. It can also lower Precision by adding non-relevant items. Retrieving the entire collection yields full Recall when all relevant items are included, but usually produces an unusably large result set with low Precision. [1]

The preferred balance depends on the task:

- A user who needs a small set of highly useful results may prioritize Precision.
- A systematic literature review or legal discovery task may prioritize Recall to reduce missed evidence.
- A candidate-generation stage may tolerate lower Precision if it must preserve as many relevant candidates as possible for a later stage.

Do not label one side universally superior. The task determines the cost of false positives and false negatives.

The F-measure combines Precision and Recall using a weighted harmonic mean:

- (F_1) weights Precision and Recall equally.
- (eta > 1) gives more weight to Recall.

The harmonic mean penalizes a large imbalance between Precision and Recall more strongly than the arithmetic mean. F-measure still ignores ranking order; two systems with different top-result ordering can receive the same F1 if their retrieved sets are identical. [1]

## 3. Metrics at a Rank Cutoff

Many retrieval tasks care more about the first (k) results than about the entire result set. Cutoff metrics make that evaluation depth explicit. [1][4]

### 3.1 Precision@k (P@k)

**Precision@k** is the proportion of the top (k) results that are relevant:

[
P@k = rac{sum_{i=1}^{k} rel_q(i)}{k}
]

Example: if 7 of the top 10 results are relevant, (P@10 = 0.7).

P@k is intuitive and useful when the result depth reflects a realistic inspection budget. Its limitations:

- It ignores everything below rank (k).
- It treats a relevant result at rank 1 the same as one at rank (k), provided both are inside the cutoff.
- Its value depends on the chosen (k).
- It does not require knowing the total number of relevant items in the collection.

The cutoff is part of the metric definition. Reporting “Precision” without saying whether it is set-based or measured at a cutoff is incomplete. [1][4]

### 3.2 Recall@k (R@k)

**Recall@k** is the fraction of all known relevant items found within the top (k):

[
R@k = rac{sum_{i=1}^{k} rel_q(i)}{|Rel_q|}
]

R@k is especially useful for evaluating candidate-generation or first-stage retrieval: the question is whether the candidate set retains relevant items before later processing.

Do not confuse Recall@k with **Success@k** or **Hit@k**. Recall@k measures the fraction of all relevant items found. Success@k typically records 1 if at least one relevant item appears in the top (k), otherwise 0. These answer different questions. The ir-measures documentation explicitly distinguishes the TREC-style Recall@k convention from Success@k. [4]

### 3.3 R-precision

**R-precision** evaluates Precision at a cutoff equal to the number of known relevant documents for the query:

[
R	ext{-precision} = P@|Rel_q|
]

If a query has 8 known relevant documents, inspect the top 8 results. If 6 are relevant, R-precision is 0.75.

At that cutoff, R-precision is also equal to Recall at rank (|Rel_q|), because the number of retrieved positions and the total number of known relevant items are the same. It adjusts the cutoff to the size of the relevant set, but it still summarizes only one point in the ranking. [1][4]

## 4. Average Precision and Mean Average Precision

Precision@k checks one cutoff. **Average Precision (AP)** instead samples Precision at the ranks where relevant documents appear, rewarding systems that retrieve relevant items early and consistently. [1]

### 4.1 Average Precision (AP)

For a query with (R_q) known relevant documents and a ranked list of length (L):

[
AP(q) = rac{1}{R_q}sum_{i=1}^{L} P@i 	imes rel_q(i)
]

Only ranks containing a relevant item contribute a non-zero term. A relevant item not retrieved contributes zero through the denominator (R_q). This means AP accounts for both the order of retrieved relevant documents and the relevant documents that were missed, relative to the known qrels set. [1]

**Important:** the denominator is the total number of known relevant documents for the query—not simply the number of relevant documents returned. Dividing only by retrieved relevant items would produce a different measure.

### 4.2 Mean Average Precision (MAP)

**MAP** is the arithmetic mean of AP across the evaluated query set (Q):

[
MAP(Q) = rac{1}{|Q|}sum_{q in Q} AP(q)
]

MAP gives each included query equal weight, regardless of how many relevant documents that query has. It is useful for comparing ranked retrieval systems over a sufficiently representative set of information needs. [1]

MAP is not the same as calculating one pooled Precision value across every document from every query. It first computes AP per query, then averages those query-level scores.

### 4.3 AP/MAP Limitations

- Standard AP uses binary relevance. Graded relevance requires a different metric or an explicit binary threshold.
- AP is influenced by the number of known relevant documents and the completeness of qrels.
- MAP can hide query-level variation. Two systems with the same MAP may behave very differently on individual queries.
- A small or unrepresentative query set can produce an unstable picture of system effectiveness.
- MAP does not model a specific user stopping point as directly as a cutoff metric.

Report per-query distributions or additional metrics when a single MAP value would hide important behavior. [1][4]

## 5. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)

**Reciprocal Rank (RR)** scores a single query using the reciprocal of the rank of its first relevant result:

[
RR(q) =

rac{1}{rank_q}, & 	ext{if a relevant result is retrieved} \
0, & 	ext{if no relevant result is retrieved}
end{cases}
]

**Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)** averages RR over a query set:

[
MRR(Q) = rac{1}{|Q|}sum_{q in Q} RR(q)
]

Examples:

- First relevant result at rank 1 → RR = 1.
- First relevant result at rank 2 → RR = 0.5.
- First relevant result at rank 5 → RR = 0.2.
- No relevant result in the evaluated list → RR = 0.

MRR is useful when finding the **first** satisfactory result is the main objective, such as a fact-answering task where one correct answer is sufficient. TREC's question-answering evaluation used MRR to score the rank of the first correct response and assigned zero when no correct response was returned. [3]

MRR is precision-focused and ignores all relevant results after the first one. A system that returns one relevant result at rank 1 and nothing else can score as highly as a system that returns many relevant results after that first result. Do not use MRR as a substitute for measuring exhaustive retrieval.

## 6. Cumulative Gain, DCG, and nDCG

Binary metrics treat every item above the chosen relevance threshold as equivalent. Many tasks need to distinguish highly relevant results from marginally relevant ones. **Cumulative Gain (CG)** and its discounted and normalized variants support graded relevance judgments. [2]

### 6.1 Cumulative Gain (CG)

Given graded relevance labels (g_i) at each rank:

[
CG@k = sum_{i=1}^{k} g_i
]

CG adds the relevance grades in the top (k) results. It captures total graded relevance but ignores rank order: swapping two documents inside the cutoff does not change CG.

### 6.2 Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)

**DCG** discounts the contribution of results lower in the ranking. One widely used formulation is:

[
DCG@k = sum_{i=1}^{k}rac{2^{g_i}-1}{log_2(i+1)}
]

Where:

- (g_i) is the graded relevance label at rank (i).
- (2^{g_i}-1) is the gain assigned to that relevance grade.
- (log_2(i+1)) discounts gain at lower ranks.

The exponential gain makes higher grades contribute disproportionately more than lower grades. The logarithmic discount gives more weight to high-ranked items. [1][2]

**Formula variants exist.** Implementations may use different gain mappings or discount functions, including linear gain or alternative discount conventions. The chosen DCG definition must be reported; two scores are not safely comparable if their gain or discount settings differ. The ir-measures documentation exposes the DCG formulation and gain mapping as parameters. [4]

### 6.3 Ideal DCG (IDCG)

**IDCG@k** is the maximum DCG achievable for the query under the same relevance labels and cutoff. It is calculated by sorting the available judged items in descending order of relevance and applying the same gain and discount formula.

### 6.4 Normalized DCG (nDCG)

**nDCG** divides the system's DCG by the ideal DCG:

[
nDCG@k = rac{DCG@k}{IDCG@k}
]

Under the usual non-negative gain formulation and a non-zero IDCG, nDCG ranges from 0 to 1:

- 1 means the evaluated ranking matches the ideal ordering at that cutoff.
- A lower value means the ranking accumulates less discounted gain than the ideal ranking.

nDCG is useful for graded relevance and top-heavy ranking evaluation. It is not a probability that a user will be satisfied, and it does not establish factual accuracy or overall content quality. [1][2]

If a query has no relevant judged items at the selected threshold, IDCG may be zero and the ratio is undefined. Toolkits may adopt a convention for this case. Report the convention or exclude such queries according to a predeclared evaluation protocol; do not silently mix conventions. [4]

## 7. Additional Metrics and Variants

The metrics above cover common IR evaluations. Other measures exist for specific judgment conditions or user models. Use them when their assumptions match the task—not just because a benchmark reports them.

### 7.1 Binary Preference (bpref)

**bpref** evaluates the relative ordering of judged relevant and judged non-relevant documents. Its design is useful when relevance judgments are incomplete: unjudged documents are not treated as if they were explicitly judged non-relevant. [4][5]

bpref can reduce some distortions caused by incomplete qrels, but it does not make an incomplete test collection complete or eliminate pooling bias. Its interpretation depends on the judged relevant/non-relevant set and the metric implementation.

### 7.2 Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR)

**ERR** extends the first-relevant-result intuition of reciprocal rank to graded relevance and a cascade-style model of user examination. It estimates the expected contribution at each rank while accounting for the chance that a user continues past earlier results. [4][6]

ERR can be useful when the task requires graded relevance and a user model that emphasizes early satisfaction. Its assumptions and relevance-to-satisfaction mapping must be understood before interpreting the score.

### 7.3 Rank-Biased Precision (RBP)

**RBP** models a user who examines results sequentially and continues to the next result with a persistence probability (p). Higher (p) represents a user more willing to continue deeper into the ranking. RBP can be configured with binary or graded relevance depending on the implementation. [4][7]

The persistence parameter is part of the model, not a universal user-behavior constant. Report it whenever RBP is used.

### 7.4 Success@k / Hit@k

Success@k is 1 when at least one result meeting the relevance threshold appears in the top (k), and 0 otherwise. Averaging this value across queries gives the proportion of queries with at least one satisfactory result in the cutoff. This is useful for one-answer tasks, but it discards how many relevant items were found and where they appear inside the cutoff. [4]

## 9. Choosing the Right Metric

There is no universal “best IR metric”. Select metrics based on the task and the system stage being evaluated.

| Evaluation goal | Useful metrics | What they reveal |
|---|---|---|
| Quality of an unranked retrieved set | Precision, Recall, F1 | Relevant-result purity and coverage. |
| Quality of the first page / fixed result budget | P@k, R@k, nDCG@k | Relevant items in a defined top-k window; nDCG also respects graded relevance and rank. |
| Candidate generation / first-stage retrieval | Recall@k, Success@k | Whether relevant candidates survive into the next stage; Success@k only asks whether at least one is found. |
| Broad ranked retrieval effectiveness | AP, MAP | How early and consistently relevant documents are retrieved across queries. |
| One satisfactory answer is enough | RR, MRR, Success@k | How quickly the first relevant answer appears and whether one appears within a cutoff. |
| Graded relevance and top-heavy ordering | DCG@k, nDCG@k, ERR@k | Whether highly relevant results appear early. |
| Incomplete relevance judgments | bpref, plus judgment-coverage reporting | Relative ordering of judged items without treating every unjudged item as explicitly non-relevant. |
| Explicit user persistence model | RBP | Ranking quality under a stated probability of continuing to the next result. |

A practical evaluation may report several complementary metrics. Keep the primary metric tied to the task objective, then use secondary metrics to diagnose trade-offs. Reporting ten metrics without explaining which one determines success is not rigor; it is metric confetti.

## 10. Aggregation Across Queries

Most benchmark results aggregate per-query metrics. The aggregation method changes what the final number represents.

### 10.1 Macro-Averaging

Macro-averaging calculates the metric for each query first, then takes the arithmetic mean:

[
M_{macro} = rac{1}{|Q|}sum_{q in Q} M(q)
]

MAP and MRR are common examples of this query-level averaging pattern. Each included query contributes equally, regardless of how many relevant documents it has. [1][3]

### 10.2 Micro-Averaging

Micro-averaging pools counts across queries before calculating a metric. For example, micro-Precision pools TP and FP across queries:

[
P_{micro} = rac{sum_q TP_q}{sum_q (TP_q+FP_q)}
]

Queries with more retrieved items therefore contribute more to the pooled result. Micro- and macro-averaged values answer different questions and should not be compared as if they were interchangeable.

### 10.3 Queries With No Relevant Judgments

Some metrics have undefined or special cases when a query has no known relevant documents:

- Recall and AP have a zero denominator if (|Rel_q|=0).
- nDCG has a zero denominator if (IDCG@k=0).
- MRR can be zero if no relevant result is retrieved, but the query's qrels still need to establish what “no relevant result” means.

Evaluation toolkits may omit such queries, assign a convention, or handle them through task-specific rules. The evaluation report must state the chosen behavior and ensure the same rule is applied to all compared systems. [4]

## 11. Incomplete Judgments and Metric Reliability

A metric is calculated from relevance judgments, not from an omniscient view of every document in the collection. If qrels are incomplete, a system may retrieve relevant but unjudged documents. Treating every unjudged item as non-relevant can penalize systems for finding documents that were never assessed. [4][5]

Practical safeguards:

- Report the source and completeness limits of qrels.
- Measure the proportion of top-k results that are judged.
- Keep relevant, non-relevant, and unjudged states distinct.
- Use bpref or another appropriate method when incomplete judgments are a material concern.
- Avoid claims that a small metric difference proves one system is universally better.
- Inspect per-query results and examples, not only the aggregate score.

Metric choice cannot compensate for poor topic coverage, ambiguous relevance criteria, or a biased test collection. Those are assessment-framework problems owned by 04.11.

## 12. Common Metric Misinterpretations

| Misinterpretation | Correction |
|---|---|
| “High Precision means the system found all relevant documents.” | Precision measures the fraction of retrieved items that are relevant. Recall measures coverage. |
| “High Recall means the top results are useful.” | Recall ignores rank position. Use a rank-sensitive metric for ordering. |
| “P@10 and nDCG@10 measure the same thing.” | P@10 counts binary relevant items in the top 10; nDCG@10 can use graded gains and discounts rank positions. |
| “MAP is just Precision averaged across queries.” | MAP averages per-query AP, which samples Precision at each relevant rank. |
| “MRR measures the quality of the entire result list.” | MRR only uses the rank of the first relevant result. |
| “nDCG is always calculated with one universal formula.” | Gain mappings and discount formulations can vary. Report the variant and parameters. |
| “A score of 0.8 means an 80% probability of user satisfaction.” | IR metrics are task- and judgment-dependent scores, not calibrated satisfaction probabilities. |
| “Unjudged means non-relevant.” | Unjudged means no recorded assessment. Any treatment in a metric is an explicit evaluation convention. |
| “One metric is enough for every retrieval stage.” | Candidate retrieval, ranked presentation, and one-answer tasks have different objectives. |
| “A higher score proves a better production search engine.” | The result applies to the benchmark, qrels, metric, and conditions used. Generalization requires further evidence. |

## 13. Relevance to SEO — Evidence Boundary

IR evaluation metrics provide a rigorous vocabulary for studying retrieval experiments, benchmark comparisons, and retrieval-model performance. They can help SEO practitioners understand research that reports Precision, Recall, MAP, MRR, or nDCG.

They do **not** provide a direct way to calculate Google's organic ranking score, reproduce its internal evaluation metrics, or predict a page's position in Google Search. Public IR metrics describe defined evaluation tasks and relevance judgments; they do not disclose proprietary search-engine scoring or experimentation systems.

Keep the ownership boundaries clear:

- **Domain 04.11:** relevance assessment, qrels, test collections, pooling, and evaluation frameworks.
- **Domain 04.12:** formal IR metrics, formulas, interpretation, and metric limitations.
- **Domain 03.12:** evaluation and testing of search-engine ranking systems.
- **Domain 14:** site-owner SEO analytics and observed organic search performance.

Search Console impressions, clicks, CTR, and average position are product-reporting measures, not substitutes for qrels-based IR metrics. Conversely, MAP or nDCG from an academic benchmark does not measure a website's organic traffic, conversions, or business value.

## Key Takeaways

- Precision measures retrieved-result relevance; Recall measures coverage of the known relevant set.
- F1 balances Precision and Recall but ignores ranking order.
- P@k and R@k evaluate a fixed result depth; Success@k only asks whether at least one relevant result appears.
- AP averages Precision at relevant ranks; MAP averages AP across queries.
- MRR focuses exclusively on the first relevant result.
- DCG discounts graded relevance by rank; nDCG normalizes it against an ideal ranking.
- bpref can help with incomplete judgments, but it does not fix a flawed test collection.
- Metric variants, relevance thresholds, cutoffs, aggregation, and zero-relevant-query conventions must be reported.
- Metric selection must follow the task—not whichever number makes a system look good.
- IR evaluation scores are not Google ranking scores or direct measures of SEO business performance.

**Bottom line:** Metrics do not tell you whether a retrieval system is good in the abstract. They tell you how it performed against a specific task, a specific judgment set, and a specific measurement rule. State those rules—or the score is just decoration.
